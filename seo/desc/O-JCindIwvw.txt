Think you're too skinny? Why skinny arms feel like the whole beach is watching, and what the action figures you grew up with did to your idea of a normal body.

It's August, and Dave is at the beach in a hoodie, because his arms are "too skinny". Says who? Not the beach: people who wore an embarrassing T-shirt into a room guessed half the room noticed, and fewer than one in four did. Not his genes: in a study of 585 beginners who trained one arm for twelve weeks, not one arm got weaker. The real culprit was in Dave's old toy box: action figures, scaled to a real man, grew from normal arms in the 1960s to arms bigger than any bodybuilder's by the 1990s.

Sources:
- Gilovich, Medvec & Savitsky (2000), Journal of Personality and Social Psychology: the "spotlight effect" T-shirt study (guessed about half noticed; fewer than a quarter did)
- Hubal et al. (2005), Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise: 585 adults, 12 weeks of one-arm training; no one lost strength, average strength gains about 40 to 64 percent
- Pope et al. (1999), International Journal of Eating Disorders: action figures grew far more muscular than even the largest bodybuilders; TIME (1999): G.I. Joe's biceps went from about 12 inches in the 1960s to 26 inches in 1997, scaled to a real man
- World Health Organization (2020) guidelines: muscle-strengthening activities for all major muscle groups on 2 or more days a week; NHS examples include push-ups and sit-ups

Try it this week: pick two days and do some push-ups and sit-ups. For you, not for the toy.
