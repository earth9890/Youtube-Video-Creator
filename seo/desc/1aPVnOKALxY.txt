No willpower at night? Why you keep losing to the biscuit tin, and how the people with the most self-control stop snacking at night: they skip the fight.

Every night at ten, Dave loses to the biscuit tin, and he thinks his willpower battery runs flat. But when twenty-three labs re-tested the willpower drain, it barely showed up. And in a week-long study, the people with the most self-control wanted things just as often as everyone else. They just resisted less, because they kept temptation out of reach.

Sources:
- Baumeister, Bratslavsky, Muraven & Tice (1998), Journal of Personality and Social Psychology: the radish-and-cookie study (radish eaters quit an impossible puzzle after about 8 minutes, cookie eaters after about 19)
- Hagger et al. (2016), Perspectives on Psychological Science: 23 labs, 2,141 people, ego-depletion effect near zero
- Vohs et al. (2021), Psychological Science: 36 labs, 3,531 people, no reliable depletion effect
- Hofmann, Baumeister, Förster & Vohs (2012), Journal of Personality and Social Psychology: 205 adults signalled 7 times a day for a week; high self-control meant as many desires, but fewer attempts to resist
- Ent, Baumeister & Tice (2015), Personality and Individual Differences: people high in self-control more often chose to work somewhere free of distractions
- Maas et al. (2012), Psychology & Health: a snack bowl placed 70 cm away instead of 20 cm meant less snacking
