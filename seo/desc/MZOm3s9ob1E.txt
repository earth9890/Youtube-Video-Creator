What would happen if you drank 100 energy drinks? How much caffeine is too much, what caffeine really does to tiredness, and why you'd never get near a hundred.

Dave is tired, and he has a theory: if one can of energy drink gives you energy, a hundred cans should give you a hundred times more. So what would really happen if you drank a hundred energy drinks? The honest answer is that you would never get anywhere near a hundred, and the reason isn't the one you'd guess.

Dave is a drawing, so he can try this on paper. You are not a drawing, so please don't try it. One can has about 80 mg of caffeine and up to 7 teaspoons of sugar; by can three he is past what Europe's food safety authority calls fine in one go, and by can five he has used up the FDA's whole-day amount. Along the way: what caffeine really does inside your head (it hides tiredness, it doesn't remove it), how long it stays in your body, and why even a hundred cans of nothing but water would be dangerous. If caffeine ever leaves you with a racing heart, chest pain or feeling really unwell, please get medical help straight away.

- Institute of Medicine (US) Committee on Military Nutrition Research (2001), "Caffeine for the Sustainment of Mental Task Performance", ch. 2, "Pharmacology of Caffeine": absorption within 45 minutes, half-life about 5 hours
- International Food Information Council (19 March 2026), "Caffeine At Work"
- Health Promotion Agency / Te Whatu Ora (NZ), HealthEd, "Behind the hype: Energy drinks" (NPA252): up to 7 teaspoons (28 g) of sugar in a 250 ml serve

- U.S. FDA, "Spilling the Beans: How Much Caffeine is Too Much?" (current as of 28 August 2024): 400 mg a day; the effects of too much caffeine; the term "energy drink" was developed by the food industry
