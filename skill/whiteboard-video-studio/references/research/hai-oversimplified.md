# Half as Interesting vs OverSimplified: how they stay funny while explaining real facts

**What I read:** all 6 HAI transcripts in full. In full for OverSimplified: WW2 Pt 1, American Revolution Pt 1, French Revolution Pt 1 and WW1 Pt 1 (that transcript is cut off at about 6 minutes). Skimmed: Cold War Pt 1 (opening and ending) and WW2 Pt 2 (first third). Speed figures come from `_stats.json`. Joke counts are my hand tallies, so treat them as approximate.

**The core difference.** HAI is **one narrator who has opinions**. Jokes come as asides, understatement and absurd specifics while he explains one system, such as airline pricing or drug-naming rules. OverSimplified is **a cast of characters acting out the facts**. Most exposition is fake dialogue between countries and leaders, and the narrator links one scene to the next. In both, the joke *is* the fact, put in a funnier shape. Neither one tacks a relatable list onto a fact, which is the main problem in our script.

## 1. Hook (first ~30 s)

**HAI, Skiplagged.** (1) A fairy-tale opener with a twist: "Once upon a 2016". (2) A small, concrete character: a man books two legal tickets. (3) A reversal: the airline *sued him*, with a precise dollar figure. (4) The term gets named. (5) A comic list of things airlines also hate (legroom, temperature, announcements). (6) A bigger mystery: airlines tried to sue the website "and failed". (7) The promise: "So today we're talking about…"
*Function: tiny story → surprising consequence → name the thing → joke → bigger mystery → promise.*

**HAI, Rolex.** (1) "This is my outside correspondent Amy." A real person sent on a mission. (2) "her mission / is doomed." (3) A pair of backhanded jokes: "not because she can't afford it, though she can't". (4) The real reason in one line, with "thanks in part to" a strange ownership setup. (5) A long, rushed aside about Amy wandering the store.
*Function: character + goal → doom → wrong reasons played for jokes → the real reason as the curiosity gap.*

**HAI, DC airspace.** It starts as a tour ("This is Washington D.C., the capital of the Unit–"), the narrator interrupts himself, and he plays the victim: "Oh, shoot." An F-16 turns up. Then comes the term (SFRA) and a superlative ("single most restricted airspace"), followed by a paradox (800+ flights a day land there anyway) and the promise.
*Function: you are in danger, right now → name it → paradox → promise.*

**HAI, Dinky train.** It sets up a contrast: the longest route described with grand scenery, then the shortest, starting in a "terrific parking lot". The pun on "dinky" comes next. Two questions follow ("how did… why have they kept it"), then a meta promise: watched to the end, this video is longer than the ride.

**OverSimplified, American Revolution.** It opens mid-scene: Columbus bursts in on the King and Queen, says "right?" four times, then shouts "Wrong!", and the reveal is "There's GOLD EVERYWHERE." Then a grim deadpan narrator line: "time of his life… by that, I mean… murder spree." Then a voiced viewer objection ("But OverSimplified, the Vikings!"), which turns into the sponsor.
*Function: cold-open sketch → narrator undercuts it with the dark truth → viewer heckle → segue.*

**OverSimplified, WW1.** "The world of 1914 // … truly the height of / civilization / let's have a war." That is a mock-grand build with a flat punch. Then each country's motive in one childlike line ("France wanted some stuff back").

**OverSimplified, Cold War.** A fetch gag: a soldier brings back Lennon instead of Lenin. Then four sentences of rushed history, ending "…caused a famine, and then he died."

**Hook templates (in my own words):**
- **Tiny-person-big-consequence:** "In [year], one ordinary [person] did [harmless thing]. // Then [huge institution] went after them. / The reason is [weird system], and today we're working out why it can't be stopped."
- **Doomed mission:** "This is [named person]. I've sent them to [do simple goal]. / They're going to fail. / Not because [funny wrong reason], (though, yes, [true]). They'll fail because [one-line real reason]."
- **Grand → flat:** "[Era/place]: [three grand adjectives]. / Truly the height of [X]. / [Stupid decision]."
- **Cold-open sketch:** two characters, one keeps saying "right?", the other agrees, then the twist line reveals the premise. The narrator then gives the grim real version in a deadpan voice.

## 3. Density and placement

**Hand count, HAI Rolex** (story part ≈ 6.3 min of 7.8): **~18 jokes, ≈ 2.9 per minute**. About **1 joke line for every 3 information lines**.

**Hand count, OS WW2 Pt 1** (story ≈ 12 min after the sponsor): **~36 jokes, ≈ 3 per minute**. But the jokes cluster. The set-up sections (Mussolini, Hitler, appeasement) run **1:1 or denser**. The war sections (Nanking, Blitzkrieg, Dunkirk, the Blitz) drop to roughly **1:8**.

**Where the jokes sit:**
- **HAI:** mostly *at the end of a fact sentence*, as a trailing clause ("…though she is") or a fragment after the full stop ("Maybe." / "Sorry."). Mid-sentence asides happen too, and get read fast ("which, sure, why not have a drinks counter"). The bigger jokes close each section.
- **OS:** the fact is *acted out*, so the joke is the delivery itself. The narrator gives one fact line, a 2–6 line dialogue carries the next fact, and the scene's last line is the punch. Then the narrator comes back in a plain voice.

**Longest stretch with no joke:**
- HAI DC airspace, the river visual approach: about **250 words / 75 s**. HAI Dinky, the 1834–1863 railroad history: about **200 words**.
- OS WW2 Pt 1, from Blitzkrieg through the fall of France: **about 250 words**, with only one innuendo joke in it. OS American Revolution, Lexington to Fort Ticonderoga: **about 230 words**.
- Both channels go **serious on purpose for atrocities and climaxes**. Each joke-free stretch is followed by a big joke right away (Benedict Arnold's "Never heard of him"; "Just the foot for now").

## 4. Story structure

**Beat map, HAI Skiplagged** (9.6 min; % = position in transcript):
- 0–8%: **hook** (the Lufthansa man, airlines failing to sue).
- 8%: **promise** ("So today we're talking about…").
- 9–18%: **the mechanism shown as a first-person demo** (narrator "books" Denver→Boston, $324 vs $116).
- 24%: **who does it** (Skiplagged's user numbers).
- 28%: **re-hook / reversal**: "But, consider… didn't create the airlines' problem. The airlines did."
- 31–52%: **root cause** (1978 deregulation, hub-and-spoke).
- 60%: **analogy that lands the point** (card counting).
- 64–75%: **conflict** (terms of service, lawsuits, American's $9.4M).
- 79%: **callback** to "not illegal" plus a self-aware lawsuit joke.
- 80–84%: **ending**: Gatsby parody, then "There are worse things."
- 84%+: **sponsor**, joined with "let's say, as a complete hypothetical".

**Beat map, OS American Revolution** (15 min):
- 0–4%: **cold-open sketch** (Columbus).
- 4–11%: **dark undercut**, then viewer objection, then sponsor.
- 12–17%: **prologue war** (George Washington's tomahawk scene).
- 19–31%: **cause in three escalating rounds of tax**: Sugar Act, Stamp Act sketch, the George III vanity scene, "there's a tax for that". Each round follows the same pattern: Britain taxes, colonists boycott, Britain backs down.
- 36–44%: **escalation of violence** (Boston Massacre, Tea Party, Intolerable Acts).
- 44–48%: **the cast assembles** (a roll call of the Congress with jokes).
- 48–57%: **first fighting** (Lexington, played straight).
- 57–64%: **comic relief** (Arnold vs Allen "I'm in charge" loop, "Never heard of him").
- 64–72%: **Washington appointed** (cut-the-crap joke), Bunker Hill played straight.
- 72%: **escalation gag** (the King's letter, one word at a time).
- 85–91%: **the turn** (Common Sense, the Declaration, the statue melted into musket balls).
- 91%: **re-hook**: "Guess who's back?"
- 98–100%: **cliffhanger low point**: "defeat, after defeat, after defeat", "survived to fight another day".

**Transitions they use.**
- HAI: "So, what is X?"; "That's how X works."; "But, consider…"; "And yeah, like so many [topic] topics, this goes back to…"; "Going forward…"; "All of which is to say…"; "Now, if you're [doing X]…" (into the sponsor).
- OS: "Next up: [country]"; "Let's rewind a bit"; "Meanwhile / Back in Europe…"; "Speaking of [X]…"; "So, here's the situation:"; "Now, where was I? Oh yeah, [3-word recap]"; "Guess who's back?"

**How a fact gets delivered.**
- HAI asks the question first ("So what is hidden city ticketing?"), then **demonstrates it as the narrator's own errand**, then names the rule, then explains the cause, then lands an analogy.
- OS gives a **fact line, then the same fact performed as dialogue, then a punch, then the consequence in a plain voice**. The tax sketch is the clearest case: the narrator says what the Stamp Act was, and the scene makes you *feel* it.

## 5. Sentence rhythm

- **Median sentence:** HAI ≈ 15 words. OS ≈ 8–14 words; the dialogue-heavy French Revolution is lowest at 8, with **33% of sentences five words or fewer**.
- **Fragments as punches:** "Maybe." / "Sorry." / "Fair enough." / "Boom. This is Germany now." / "In a war."
- **Rhetorical questions:** HAI uses about two per video as section openers. OS asks 18–46 per video, mostly inside dialogue.
- **"You":**
  - HAI DC puts the viewer in the cockpit (33 per 1,000 words).
  - HAI's drug-naming and Skiplagged videos use "I" as the protagonist (14–20 per 1,000 words).
  - OS uses "you" mostly character to character.
- **Lists:** long lists are *read without commas* and end on a flat or worst item. Repetition shows up as anaphora ("The Allies did nothing." ×2; "defeat, after defeat, after defeat").
- **Contrast pairs:** HAI loves "X, and the second, not-X" ("that it's a for-profit… that it's a not-for-profit").

## 6. Delivery from the pause marks

Speed and pauses (from `_stats.json`):
- **HAI:** 197–225 wpm, 2–4 long pauses per minute.
- **OS:** 185–222 wpm. The French Revolution is the slowest at 185 wpm and has the most pauses (6.5 per minute), because it is mostly dialogue. WW2 Pt 1, WW2 Pt 2 and the American Revolution are mostly narration: 220+ wpm with only 2.5–3.5 pauses per minute.

**So: dialogue slows them down and adds silence; narration and lists run fast.**

Concrete examples (text before pause | after):
1. HAI: "Once upon a 2016, **//** | a man boarded…": pause *after* the twisted opener.
2. HAI: "certainly my right… to not **/** | go to Boston.": a short beat *before* the punch.
3. HAI: "That's a **/** | watch joke. **//**": a beat before and a long beat after.
4. HAI: "$84,000? **//** | The price of two and a half Toyota Camrys? … Memphis? **//** | Sorry.": pause after each comparison, and the longest one before "Sorry."
5. HAI: "…15 **//** million, to be clear, not 15 dollars. It's not that Dinky. **//**": pause *after* the punch, before the next section.
6. OS: "let's / go with **//** | bald ¶ Italy **//**": a long beat *before* the punch, then a 2.5 s+ gap *after* it into the new section.
7. OS: "who shoots **//** | him **//**": the archduke's death split across pauses.
8. OS: "okay **//** no **//** don't do it **//** | stamp **//**": every beat of the sketch separated by silence.
9. OS: "i'm **//** gonna **//** kick **//** your **//** ass": the slow handwriting gag, with the pause as the joke.
10. OS: "oh **//** you **//** lied to me / what / do you expect I'm / Hitler": slow set-up, then the punch line comes *fast*.
11. OS: "I believe in you champ you / got this **//** | maybe **//**": an undercut with a beat on both sides.

**Pattern:**
- **Section changes get a long pause (often a 2.5 s+ newline) right after the punch.** Every section ends on a joke followed by silence.
- **One-word undercuts get a pause on both sides.**
- **Escalation lists get no pauses at all.** "got shot broke the economy caused a famine and then he died" has no marks; "snowballs… rocks… oyster shells" has none either. The rush is the joke.
- **Asides are read faster.** Amy's 60-word aside in the Rolex video has zero marks, and the "welcome to the channel" aside has none.

## 7. Signature moves and phrases

**HAI**
1. The narrator as a petty, vain protagonist ("my writers are misbehaving", "I've got status there").
2. "This is [X]." as the cold open, with an on-screen proxy (Amy, the narrator's own plane).
3. The understated adjective on a huge number ("a light 9.4 million").
4. An everyday analogy introduced with "It's less like… more like…".
5. A callback button on the final line, then a literary-parody ending.

**OverSimplified**

6. History as a sitcom: countries and leaders bicker like coworkers.
7. Running catchphrases ("there's a tax for that", "OOOoh NOoo", "fat, ill-bred boy").
8. A small modern word for a huge event ("pimped up his army", "relationship status").
9. "I made that last one up", "Now where was I? Oh yeah, [three-word recap]".
10. Tone whiplash: clown scene → atrocity told straight → clown scene.

## 8. Steal / avoid

**Steal (1-minute and 8-minute):**
1. **Give the idea a protagonist with a goal** (Amy and her Daytona; the narrator booking a flight). In 1 minute, one named stick figure. In 8 minutes, a cast.
2. **Turn every key fact into a 3–5 line exchange** that ends on a one-word punch. The fact goes *inside* the dialogue, not before it.
3. **Make the joke out of the fact itself.** Every gag should *carry* information: the price comparison, the waitlist analogy, "there's a tax for that". If you can delete the joke and lose nothing, cut it.
4. **Use one runner** introduced in the first 20 s and paid off 2–3 times, the last one in the final line.
5. **Escalate lists to a flat ending** and read them fast with no pauses.
6. **Undercut with one-word fragments** ("Maybe." / "Probably." / "Fair enough.") with `//` on both sides.
7. **End every section on a joke plus a long pause.** Open the next section with a plain question.
8. **Name the sceptic as a character** ("Other scientists, in a lab coat: 'Small sample.'") instead of describing them.
9. **Allow one serious stretch** (20–40 s in 8 minutes) for the most awe-inspiring fact. Contrast makes the next joke land harder.
10. **The 8-minute shape:** hook (0–8%) → promise → mechanism demo → "But consider…" reversal (~30%) → cause → analogy (~60%) → conflict → callback ending (~80–85%). The 1-minute shape: character + premise (10 s) → mechanism (15 s) → evidence (15 s) → doubt (10 s) → callback (10 s).

**Avoid:**
1. **Generic relatable-life gags** (Mondays, traffic, WhatsApp groups, Wi-Fi). Neither channel uses them; their jokes are about *this* subject.
2. **"Like a [random pop thing]" similes** with no explanatory payload ("cosmic Kinder Joy"). HAI's analogies *explain*; ours only decorate.
3. **A joke on every line with no breathing room.** Even OS slows to 1:8 in places.
4. **Promising wonder without a reversal or conflict.** Each video has an "except no" or "but consider".
5. **Ending on a new joke.** They end on a *callback* plus a dry button.

## 9. Demo rewrite (≈180 words, ≈60 s, both channels' styles combined)

```
This is Dave. / Dave lives in a house, / in a city, / on a planet, / in a galaxy, //
inside a black hole. //
(slow) Probably. / Some physicists think so. //
Quick recap. (fast) A black hole is where gravity gets so strong nothing escapes — not light, not planets, not Dave. //
Now the idea: / when something huge collapses, it might not crush down to a dot. / It might bounce, // and inflate into a brand-new universe on the other side.
"So I'm inside one right now?" / Yes, Dave. /
"Shouldn't I be spaghetti?" // No. / One this big stretches you so gently you'd never notice. (fast) You'd still have to go to work. //
And here's the weird part. / The James Webb telescope checked hundreds of baby galaxies, / and most of them spin the same direction. //
That's not supposed to happen. / Unless everything was born inside something / that was already spinning. //
(slow) Like, say, / a black hole. //
Other scientists: / "Small sample. Probably nothing." //
Dave: / "Probably?" //
But if it's true, / every black hole up there has a universe inside. / With its own Dave. //
(fast) Also going to work.
```

What changed:
- There is a protagonist (Dave).
- The opening widens through nested places and ends on a punch.
- The spaghetti fact is carried by fake dialogue.
- "Probably" is a runner used three times.
- The sceptics become characters.
- The galaxy-spin evidence gets one beat played straight ("Like, say, / a black hole").
- The ending calls back to the opening.

One fact to check before recording: the galaxy-spin claim is based on one 2025 JWST study of about 260 galaxies, and the share reported varies (roughly 60% to two-thirds). That is why the script says "most" rather than a number.
