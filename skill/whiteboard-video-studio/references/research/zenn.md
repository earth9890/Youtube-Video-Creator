# Zenn writing research: 10 Zenn0009 transcripts and 4 whisper references

**The main finding:** Zenn is not a joke channel. Across the 10 videos I counted about 100 humorous beats in roughly 90 minutes, which is about 1.1 per minute. The two most-viewed videos have the fewest jokes: *Ancient Humans at Night* (8.05M views) has about 0.7 per minute and *Calhoun Effect* (5.0M) has about 0.35. The funnier ones, *WE Are The Aliens* (829k) and *Ancient Humans All Day* (445k), reach about 2 per minute. Even so, the fun comes from four things: putting "you" in the scene, contrasts across deep time, very specific weird facts, and a calm, dry narrator. **Every joke is made out of the fact it sits next to.** I found no generic relatable gags (Mondays, Wi-Fi, traffic, family group chats) anywhere in the corpus. That is the clearest diagnosis of our script: its jokes could be pasted onto any topic.

Corpus: the 10 timed transcripts (7 also have clean versions; the text matches apart from the pause marks) plus the 4 SRTs: night, calhoun, aliens, and cooking. Cooking is a 28-minute Past Tense-style video, which I used only as a contrast.

## 1. Hook (first ~30 s)

**Ancient Humans at Night (0:00–0:39)**
1. "Tonight… you're going to flip a switch." Puts the viewer in a mundane future moment.
2. "You won't think twice about it." Points out the blind spot.
3. "For 99.9% of human history, that switch didn't exist." Flips the scale.
4. "Couldn't even see your own hand in front of your face." Makes it physical.
5. "Every single one of your ancestors… half their life in near total darkness." Gives the viewer a personal stake.
6. "So, what did they actually do for all those hours?" Asks the title question.
7. "The answer changes everything we think we know about sleep, storytelling…" A promise plus a 3-item roadmap. The three items become the three sections.

**Calhoun Effect (0:00–0:22)**, with no "you" at all:
1. "Eight mice were placed inside a perfect world." The premise.
2. Four males, four females, unlimited food and water, no predators. Makes the paradise concrete.
3. "Five years later, every single one of them was dead." The paradox.
4. "Not a single death… a vet could have diagnosed." Opens the mystery gap.
5. "This is Universe 25, // the most disturbing experiment ever run…" Title drop plus a superlative.

**WE Are The Aliens (0:00–0:29)**
1. Three species that fit Earth perfectly: fish gills, hollow bird bones, polar bear fur. Sets a pattern.
2. "And then there's us." Breaks the pattern.
3. "We burn in our own sunlight. Our spines are falling apart." Roasts the species.
4. The only species that looked around and thought "Something's off." A fake inner voice.
5. "What if… we're not from here?" The wild hypothesis, asked as a question.

**Raising the Titanic (0:00–0:35)**
1. "We know exactly where it is." Establishes the known.
2. "We've sent cameras… submarines… We've even sent tourists." Rule of three with a dark twist.
3. "So, why hasn't anyone just // brought it back up?" The naive question, with a pause before the obvious move.
4. "Turn it into a museum. Charge admission. You'd make a fortune." Voices the naive plan.
5. "Some were creative. / Some were completely insane." An undercut.
6. "Every single one of them failed. And here's why." The promise.

**Spotlight Effect.** A second-person nightmare: late, coffee stain, everyone staring. Then "At least, / that's what it feels like." Then "here's what actually happened": tiny deflating details, then the name of the effect, then what knowing it will do for you.

**What Happens After We Die / Can't Remember Being a Baby.** These open on a blunt second-person shock: "Your heart just stopped." and "You lived an entire life before the age of three." Both go straight into a paradox promise, for example that dying might be when your brain is most alive.

**Hook templates**
- **T1, the switch flip:** [You'll do a modern mundane X tonight] → [for nearly all of history, X didn't exist] → [a sensory picture of life without it] → [so what did they do instead?] → [the answer rewrites A, B and C].
- **T2, perfect setup then disaster:** [N subjects got everything] → [list the comforts] → [time skip: the opposite outcome] → [and the obvious cause wasn't it] → [This is NAME, the most SUPERLATIVE X ever].
- **T3, the odd one out:** [everything else does X perfectly: 3 quick examples] → ["And then there's us."] → [3 of our failures, stated bluntly] → [a "what if" hypothesis].
- **T4, the obvious question:** [we know A, did B, even did absurd C] → [so why hasn't anyone just DO-THE-OBVIOUS?] → [voice the naive plan] → [everyone tried, all failed, here's why].
- **T5, the second-person deflation:** [you in a scary or embarrassing moment] → ["at least, that's how it feels"] → [what really happened, in tiny details] → [name it, plus what knowing it gives you].

The rule behind all five: the question or paradox lands by about 0:30, and the title is answered as a promise, never as a summary.

**Placement:**
- About 70% of jokes are the closing line of a sub-beat. A fact paragraph ends on a short punch, then a new topic starts. Examples: "That's just us.", "Most of us cannot do that with a mortgage and a job." (7:06), immediately followed by "Late afternoon."
- About 20% are mid-sentence word swaps or one-word tags, such as "retrofitted, badly" and "overnight. Literally."
- A few open a section as an aside: "Then there's childbirth. This one's rough."

**Gaps and clustering:**
- Aliens: about 60 s with nothing between 3:31 and 4:35 (the Crick authority setup). The final about 2:00 (8:05 → 10:30) has no jokes; it is an earnest landing.
- All Day: about 60 s with nothing from 7:35 to 8:37 ("This is not paradise").
- Calhoun: about 3:40 with nothing (1:36 → 5:15). The horror escalation runs straight.

Two patterns: jokes cluster in the evidence-list part of a video (the first 40%), and **the last 15–20% is always joke-free.**

## 5. Sentence rhythm

- **Length:** the mean is 10.5–12.3 words and the median is 9–11 in every video. 18–26% of sentences are 5 words or fewer, and only 8–15% run 20 or more. The pattern is short, medium, short, then a long list sentence, then a fragment.
- **Fragments as punches:** "Everything." "Light." "Extra hours." "Especially honey." "Not one."
- **Rhetorical questions** are sparse (0.1–0.9 per minute) but sit exactly at the hook and the section pivots. Often they come in pairs: "Can you remember…? The color of their shoes?"
- **"You" sets the mode.** There are two modes:
  - *You-mode* for body and psychology topics. Uses of "you/your" per minute: Spotlight 13.0, Baby 9.7, Die 6.8, Eat 6.6.
  - *Case-file mode* for stories: Calhoun 0.0, Slotow 0.1, Titanic 1.5. The narrator stays third-person and turns to "us" only in the last 20 seconds.
- **Anaphora and parallelism:** "The food never ran out. The water never ran out." / "They ate, / they slept, / and they groomed…" / "Night was when you slept. Night was when you were vulnerable."
- **Lists** have 3–7 items, comma-paced, often ending on the strangest one or swerving after it: cheese, yogurt … pickles → "They are all rot."

## 6. Delivery from the pause marks

**Speed:** 186–208 wpm (Spotlight is fastest at 208 with only 0.4 long pauses per minute; Titanic and Die are slowest, 186–187, with the most pauses). Long pauses run 0.4–2.9 per minute and short ones 4–14 per minute, so long pauses are rare.

**Caveat:** 81 of the 134 `//` marks (60%) sit right next to a numeral, such as "300,000 // years" or "In 1994, //". Part of that is caption timing on numbers. Still, the habit is real: they slow down around years and big numbers. The other ~53 are rhetorical.

**Examples (text before | text after):**
1. "The very first thing that happens when your heart stops //" | "is nothing." — **before** an anticlimax
2. "which is, to put it mildly, //" | "not practical." — **before** an understatement punch
3. "believed the Titanic sank in one piece. //" | "It didn't." — **before** the correction
4. "one craving we still have trouble admitting, //" | "each other. //" — **both sides** of the reveal
5. "So, why hasn't anyone just //" | "brought it back up?" — **mid-question** suspense
6. "And when she falls, //" | "nobody runs." — **before** the emotional punch
7. "a mortgage and a job. //" | "Late afternoon, //" — **after** the joke, plus a section change
8. "winding down like a machine losing power. //" | "That's not what they found." — **after** a false expectation
9. "did not use that minute to shut down quietly. //" | "It used it to turn on." — **before** the twist
10. "nervous, / unruly, //" | "aggressive." — a list where the **pause grows** before the last item

Short `/` pauses do the same job at smaller scale: "Outside that circle, / you die." and "They were also, / functionally, / dead."

**Pattern:** a long pause comes **before** the punch or twist (examples 1, 2, 3, 6, 9), and a second pause comes **after** it only when a section changes or the reveal is dark (4, 7). Questions get a pause after them before the answer.

**Asides do not speed up; the setup does.** I checked speaking rate per segment in the whisper output for Night and Calhoun (median 182 and 192 wpm). Punch lines get slowed down and isolated: "Everything." / "Light." / "Literally." each take about 1 s on their own, and "They were also, functionally, dead." runs about 104 wpm. Connective pivots run fast, around 300+ wpm: "And then we conquered it.", "But not every male went quiet.", "But what if it's not a disorder at all?" Asides like "Seriously." and "This one's rough." are delivered flat, in the same register, with no pause around them.

The formula: **rush the pivot, brake on the punch.**

## 7. Signature moves (what makes it sound like Zenn)

1. **A second-person, present-tense cold open:** "Tonight…", "Right now…", "Open your fridge right now."
2. **The deep-time contrast:** a modern object set against 300,000 or 3 million years, or "99.9% of human history".
3. **The "ancient program" metaphor:** "your body still remembers", "running ancient software", "a 3 million-year-old program".
4. **Year-first citations with a pause:** "In 1992, // psychiatrist Thomas Wehr…", with a real name in every section.
5. **"Not X. Y." corrections and myth-busting:** state the popular reading, then "But that's not what [X] actually said."
6. **Re-hook phrases every 45–75 s:** "the part nobody talks about", "it gets stranger".
7. **Reductive aphorisms as section buttons:** "Cooking is pre-digestion.", "a kitchen, not an accident".
8. **Named characters and coined labels:** "the beautiful ones", "Tom Thumb", "the big piece", "the watch".
9. **Dry, blunt taboo** delivered with zero change of tone (cannibalism, orgasm, sex, death).
10. **Endings that call back to the opening image, now changed, then turn on the viewer:** "We still do." / "you have no idea how rare that is." / "most of us never even knew it was gone."

(Contrast: the Past Tense cooking video includes a mid-video "smash that like button" at 1:04, literally says "pattern interrupt", and ends with an end-screen plug. Zenn's own videos do none of this on-screen.)

## 8. Steal / avoid

**Steal (top 10 rules):**
1. **Make the joke out of the fact.** Reframe the real mechanism ("controlled rot") instead of attaching an unrelated gag.
2. **Open with "you" in a concrete present moment, then flip the scale within 10 s.** Ask the title question by 0:30.
3. **Promise in one line that lists 2–3 things the answer changes,** and make those your sections.
4. **Name one real study per section,** year first, followed by `//`.
5. **Re-hook every 45–75 s** (in a 1-minute video, one re-hook at about 0:30).
6. **Escalate:** weird, then weirder, then darkest or most personal. Save the best fact for 70–80%.
7. **Myth-bust or fake ending once:** "So that settles it, right? Well, not quite."
8. **The punch is the shortest sentence in its paragraph, placed last,** with `/` or `//` before it.
9. **Keep humor at 1–2 beats per minute** (2–3 in a 60 s short), and keep the final 15% sincere.
10. **End on a callback to the first image, now changed, aimed at the viewer.** For TTS, pace at about 180–190 wpm, use `//` only 1–3 times per minute, and give one-word punches their own sentence.

**Avoid:**
1. **Generic relatable gags** (Mondays, Wi-Fi, WhatsApp, bills). They appear zero times in about 90 minutes of this channel.
2. **A joke after every line.** Our script has about 12 gags in 60 s; Zenn's peak is about 2.
3. **Hedges that deflate the premise** ("science is mostly maybe"). Zenn hedges once, late, and turns it into wonder: "almost certainly is not definitely."
4. **Vague "some scientists".** Always give a name, a year and a number.
5. **Ending on a cute gag** ("wave hi"). End on a quiet turn instead. Also avoid hype words ("boom", "Surprise!"); the voice is calm and dry.

## 9. Demo rewrite (~185 words, ~60 s)

> Right now, you're inside something. / A room. / A city. / A planet. / A galaxy. //
> Some physicists think the list keeps going.
> (slow) The next item / is a black hole. //
> Here's what bugs them. Take all the stuff in the visible universe, and ask how big a black hole it would make. / The answer lands / in the same ballpark as the universe itself.
> (fast) Could be a coincidence. Could be a very large hint.
> In 2010, // physicist Nikodem Popławski did the math on the bottom of a black hole. Matter doesn't crush into a point. / It bounces. // Into a brand-new universe.
> And you wouldn't notice. Fall into one that big, and the edge doesn't shred you. / You just drift across. (fast) No bump. No sign saying "Now leaving reality."
> In 2025, // the Webb telescope checked 263 of the oldest galaxies. Two out of three / spin the same way. / It should be half and half.
> Most astronomers say: probably nothing. Wait for more data. / Fair.
> But if they're wrong, (slow) every black hole up there is a door. //
> You're inside something. / A room. / A planet. / A galaxy. / A black hole. //
> (slow) Looking out / at all the others.

**Why it works:**
- **Hook:** T3/T1 style. "You" in the present, an escalating list, and the title claim by 0:08.
- **Jokes:** three, all built from the facts. "Could be a very large hint" is an understatement, the "Now leaving reality" sign is absurd specificity, and "Fair." is a deadpan undercut. The galaxy-spin claim gets one honest hedge.
- **Ending:** a callback that reruns the opening list with the new item, then a quiet turn with no gag.

**Fact checks:**
- The black hole size match holds only roughly (comparable to the Hubble radius), which is why the script says "in the same ballpark".
- Popławski's work is from 2010.
- The galaxy-spin result is Shamir's 2025 JWST/JADES study (about 2/3 of 263 galaxies). It is disputed, which the hedge covers.
