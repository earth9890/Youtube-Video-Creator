**Main finding:** PastTense is not a comedy channel. It is calm, sincere and curiosity-driven, and it has about 0.8 light-humour moments per minute. Hardly any of those are punchlines. Its two 1M+ videos are close to joke-free. They work because of three things:
- a second-person scenario at the start
- a "this is a story, not a law" reframe
- an escalating counter or list that pays off in a callback

Use this channel for **structure, hooks and how a fact is delivered**, not as a source of jokes. Its humour does teach one thing our script gets wrong: the funny part comes out of the fact itself, never from a pop-culture comparison added on top.

## 1. Hook (first ~30 s)

**N2Y4 (3 meals), 1.27M views**
1. *Viewer's day in second person:* breakfast, then lunch, then "tonight, you will sit down for dinner." It is ordinary on purpose.
2. *Calls out the habit:* "You did not question it. / You never have."
3. *Teases a secret:* "here is what almost no one talks about."
4. *Denies the obvious reasons in three steps:* "Nobody decided this… No doctor prescribed it. No ancient wisdom…"
5. *Reframe:* "not a law of nature. It is a story."
6. *Promise plus time jump:* "Let us go back. / Way back."

**KTw (Rarest Human), 1.18M views**
1. *A strange second-person scene:* you meet a striking woman in a park.
2. *Details pile up one by one:* silver hair, blue eyes, then albinism, then left-handed. Each detail is a trait the video will later score.
3. *The story moves fast:* dates, falling in love, she becomes your girlfriend.
4. *Undercut:* "your alarm goes off. / It was just a dream."
5. *Inner question:* "Does a person like that actually exist? / Statistically?" The answer is a deadpan "Yes."
6. *A bigger question:* what would the rarest possible person look like? Then a method promise: "break humanity down trait by trait."

**TZ (Rarest Diseases)**
1. *Meme format:* "I have a trade offer."
2. *Stakes:* $1 billion against the rarest diseases for 1 year, and you keep the money afterwards.
3. *Shift to second person and present tense:* "you are now allergic to water."
4. *Escalation run:* "not just salt water, all water," then rain, tears and sweat. The deal-or-no-deal question is held back until the last line.

**tPy (Hot food)**
A relatable small misfortune (the pizza is late, then cold), then a sensory list (same cheese, same sauce, same crust), then an understated punch: "but somehow / it feels worse." The microwave fixes it, and the narrator generalises: "We do this with rice, meat, soup…" Then the question, and a note that it is not a necessity: your stomach is "perfectly capable" of handling cold food.

**Generalised templates**
- **A: everyday ritual exposed.** Describe the viewer's routine in second person → "You never questioned it" → deny the obvious explanations → "It isn't a law. It's a story" → time jump.
- **B: dream or fantasy, then the real question.** A vivid second-person scenario that carries the video's variables → hard undercut (alarm, wake-up) → "Is that even possible? Statistically?" → one-word answer → a bigger "what's the extreme version?" question → method promise.
- **C: the deal.** A tempting offer plus a terrible price → put the viewer in it now ("you are now…") → escalate the first consequence 3 to 4 times → keep the decision for the outro.
- **D: small misfortune.** A tiny shared annoyance → list what is technically unchanged → "and yet it feels wrong" → state the "why" question → rule out the boring answer.

## 3. Density & placement

**tPy (Hot food, 5.06 min, 84 sentences):** 5 clear wit lines (understated pizza, warm cola, chicken broth, leftover fries, callback) plus 3 lighter ones, for about 1.0 clear and 1.6 total per minute. That is roughly 1 humour line for every 10 info lines. Placement:
- 5%
- 35–37% (a back-to-back pair)
- 47%
- 84%

They sit at the **end of a fact block**, usually as the "imagine…" example that shows the fact in action. The longest dry stretch runs from 52% to 84% (the fire, cooking and culture section), about 1.6 minutes.

**TZ (Diseases, 7.83 min, 78 sentences):** about 10 wit lines, about 1.3 per minute, but they are tightly clustered. They sit at 0–10% (deal, water allergy run, "Water / is now your enemy", spilled water, sweating) and at 84–99% (the combination chain, the "would you still take the deal" callback). From 17% to 84%, about **5.2 minutes**, there is not one joke. That middle runs on second-person dread ("You're getting X… expect…").

**Rule across the channel:**
- Humour opens and closes a video.
- In the middle it is rare and always comes after a fact, never before one.
- Wit never interrupts mid-sentence.
- The middle relies on new facts and a rising counter to keep attention.

## 4. Story structure

**Beat map: N2Y4 (3 meals)**
- 0–6%: hook (you, the ritual, "it is a story")
- 6%: "Let us go back. Way back." Ohalo II, 23,000 years ago: eating was "not a routine. It was a result."
- 16%: re-hook. "Then something happened that changed everything." Agriculture.
- 22–35%: three-civilisation list (Egypt has 2 meals, Greeks 3 loose ones, Romans 1). The Romans are flagged as "the most surprising case."
- 35%: settlers vs Native Americans. A bias seeds the twist.
- 41%: question re-hook. "So, where exactly did… actually come from?" Royal Navy.
- 48%: escalation. "But what truly locked…" Industrial Revolution: "not a discovery. It was a solution."
- 56%: breakfast and dinner each shift.
- 66%: twist character. "Someone you might not expect": Kellogg, "not pure science… also commerce."
- 71%: biggest question. "here is the most important question of all": the body clock.
- 86%: emotional widening. "biology is only part of the story": eating together.
- 92%: recap as a run of "Because…" clauses (six of them).
- 99%: callback to tonight's dinner, "And now, you will know why."

**Beat map: KTw (Rarest Human)**
- 0–9%: dream scenario. 9%: alarm undercut. 12%: question plus method.
- Trait blocks, each ending on an updated odds line ("our person has now a 1 in…"):
  - ethnicity (16%)
  - albinism (30%)
  - eyes (40%), with a "green is rare, but…" one-up
  - left-handedness (49%)
  - tetrachromacy (60%)
  - hyperthymesia (74%)
- 85%: "To recap…" list. 87%: "In other words, this person." That is the visual reveal.
- 89%: scale comparison (stars, grains of sand on 910,000 Earths).
- 93%: "And this is not even the rarest…"
- 98%: moral plus pun sign-off.

**Transition phrases they use:**
- "Let us go back. Way back."
- "Then something happened that changed everything."
- "So, where exactly…?"
- "But what truly…"
- "Now, here is the most important question of all."
- "But X is only part of the story."
- "Moving on to [X]" (15 times in 7f)
- "Now, the eyes." / "Now, let's make this person…"
- "The next disease…" / "This one…" / "For this one,"
- "To understand X, we need to understand what X actually is."
- "The honest answer is probably yes, but not for the reasons people usually think."
- "First, the numbers."
- "There is one more piece of science worth knowing."
- "Which brings us back to…"

**How a fact is delivered.** It is almost always **question first, then the fact, then a reframe**. In order:
1. Ask the question, or set up the expectation ("You might expect them to have had a strict meal schedule").
2. Deny it ("They did not.").
3. Give the specific evidence: a named historian or lab, plus a number.
4. Close with a short "not X, it's Y" verdict.

There are about 25 of those "not X, it's Y" lines across the corpus, 7 of them in N2Y4 alone.

## 5. Sentence rhythm

- **Average sentence length** ranges from 10 words (svx, tPy) to 19 (KXv, 7f). The medians are 9 to 19.
  - Short-sentence videos have 25–30% of sentences at 6 words or fewer (svx 28%, tPy 30%, N2Y4 25%).
  - List videos (7f, TZ) carry long clinical sentences: 24–28% are 25 words or more.
- **Fragments come in runs of three:** "Permanent settlements formed. / Cooking grew more complex. / Meals became more communal." Also anaphora: "It steals years. // It steals people."
- **Rhetorical questions** are few, 0 to 4 per video. Each one does a structural job: it opens a section. They are not used for filler.
- **"You" density** is 4.0–4.5 per 100 words in the scenario-driven videos (TZ, OEW) and 0.6–0.8 in the lecture-style ones (svx, KXv, 7f). The successful N2Y4 front-loads "you" in the hook, then mostly drops it until the outro.
- **Lists** are the default unit, usually three or four items with a "/" beat between each, for example "blood glucose, / insulin sensitivity, / metabolism."
- **Repetition:** a refrain that changes each time ("our person has now a 1 in…", "You're getting…", "Moving on to…"), plus the climactic "Because… because… because…" recap.

## 6. Delivery from the pause marks

There are 82 long pauses across the corpus. Roughly:
- **~50%** fall inside numbers, splitting the figure from its unit or magnitude ("1 in 5.33 // quadrillion", "1600 // BCE")
- **~20%** come after a jargon name, before its explanation
- **~25%** fall at section or paragraph changes
- **~5%** come after a dramatic or comic line

Long pauses come **after** punch lines. Before a punch line there is only a short "/" beat, and it lands right before the key word.

**Pace:** steady at 172–180 wpm, which is faster than typical documentary voice-over. Long-pause rate follows content: highest in the number-reveal video (KTw 2.6 per minute) and the dramatic one (svx 2.2), lowest in tPy (0.6), which gets its rhythm from short sentences and "/" beats instead. Asides ("Too many to add to the video.") are **not** marked off by pauses. They are read flat at normal speed. The data cannot show any speeding up for asides. The style is even, flat, and lets the wording carry the effect.

## 7. Signature moves & phrases

1. **A second-person, present-tense opening scene** (6 of 8 videos): pizza, park, spider, breakfast, dog staring at your burger, trade offer.
2. **"It's not X. It's Y."** reframes, about 25 in total, for example "not a discovery. It was a solution."
3. **The "but rarer still" one-up:** a cool variant first, then the rarer and stranger one.
4. **A running counter or refrain** that adds up across sections (odds, diseases, body parts top to bottom).
5. **Bookend callback** to the opening object or scene (7 of 8 videos).
6. **Named authority plus a precise number:** historian, lab, sample size (48 infants, 252 volunteers, 0.14 mm).
7. **The question that opens each section** ("So, where exactly…?", "Here is the thing that should puzzle you").
8. **Lists of three with "/" beats,** and anaphora ("It takes the young. It takes the old.").
9. **Warm verdict aimed at the viewer** at the end ("you will know why", "you are the product of their caution", "They're your friends").
10. **Humour that comes from the fact itself:** cats can't taste sugar, raccoons got heavier, flies taste with their legs. It is always understated and never a pop-culture comparison.

## 8. Steal / avoid

**Steal (for both 1-minute and 8-minute scripts)**
1. Open on the viewer in a concrete present-tense moment that uses the topic's real objects (bed, ceiling, phone), not a list of generic gripes.
2. In the first 10 seconds, deny the obvious explanation, then reframe it ("It isn't a fact. It's a guess with a telescope.").
3. Give every section a question first, and answer it with one specific, named, numbered fact.
4. End each block on a short "not X, it's Y" verdict line. That is the clip-worthy beat.
5. Build a counter or refrain that rises (8-minute: odds or stakes go up each section; 1-minute: one "and it gets weirder" step).
6. Find humour inside the science (an absurd true consequence, an animal's indifference, a grim-but-mundane pairing), then deliver it flat.
7. Short "/" beat before the key word, long "//" after the punch or reveal, long "//" at every section change.
8. In an 8-minute script, put humour up front and at the end. In the middle, let new facts and rising stakes carry it, with no more than about 1.5 minutes without a "huh" beat.
9. Close with a callback to the opening scene, plus a warm line addressed to the viewer.
10. 1-minute shape: scene (8 s) → reframe (5 s) → one mechanism (15 s) → one piece of evidence with a number (12 s) → honest doubt (8 s) → callback (10 s).

**Avoid**
1. Bolted-on relatable comparisons (Mondays, WhatsApp group, Kinder Joy). This channel never uses them, and they undercut the topic.
2. A joke after every fact. Wit should arrive about once every 8–10 lines, not every line.
3. Pausing **before** a weak punch. A long setup pause signals "joke incoming" and kills an understated line.
4. Vague sourcing ("some scientists"). Name the study, the telescope or the number.
5. Ending on a gag ("Spoiler: they do."). End on the callback and a sincere beat. A light pun is fine only if it ties back to the opening.

## 9. Demo rewrite (~175 words, ~60 s at 176 wpm)

> You're lying in bed. / Lights off. / Phone on three percent. //
> Walls, / ceiling, / sky outside. / All normal. //
> Now imagine none of it is *outside* anything. // (slow) It's all inside a black hole. //
> Is that even possible? / Some physicists say... / maybe. //
> Here's the idea. / A black hole isn't a hole. / It's a place where mass is packed so tight / not even light escapes. //
> Fall into a small one, / and it stretches you like spaghetti. //
> Fall into one big enough, / and you might not notice at all. // (fast) Which is either comforting, / or deeply suspicious. //
> Some theories say the inside of a black hole could open into a brand-new universe. / Ours might be one of them. //
> And there's a clue. / In 2025, a study of James Webb images checked 263 early galaxies. / About two-thirds spun the same way. // If space had no favourite direction, / it should be close to half. / It wasn't. //
> Other astronomers say it's probably a measurement quirk. / Honest answer? / We don't know yet. //
> So tonight, / when you stare at that ceiling, // (slow) you might be looking at the inside of something. /
> And inside every black hole up there... // someone might be staring back.

**What I changed and why:**
- I cut every relatable gag (Wi-Fi, WhatsApp, Monday, Kinder Joy).
- I used the channel's opening scene, its "isn't X, it's Y" line, and one named piece of evidence with a number.
- The one dry undercut ("comforting, or deeply suspicious") follows the fact. It is not stuck onto it.
- The ending calls back to the bed and ceiling.
