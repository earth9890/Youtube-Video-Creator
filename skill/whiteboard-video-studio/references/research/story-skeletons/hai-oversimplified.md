# Story skeletons: Half as Interesting + OverSimplified

12 videos: 6 HAI, 6 OverSimplified. Data: `data/hai-oversimplified.json`.
Timestamps come from the YouTube `.vtt` files: the `.timed.txt` files have pause marks but no `[mm:ss]` stamps. Each stamp marks the ~12 s caption block the beat starts in, so it can be up to 12 s early.
"Story runtime" leaves out sponsor reads and merch plugs, so the % figures measure the story only. Everything below is paraphrased.
This file covers story structure only. Hooks, jokes and delivery are in `../hai-oversimplified.md`.

Link labels: **BUT** = something blocks or reverses. **THEREFORE** = a consequence of the beat before. **SO** = the narrator moves the story on, or a quest step. **AND** = a fact added with no cause.

## Half as Interesting

### HAI — Why Almost Every Airline Has Sued This One Website (2.20M, 9.6 min; story 8:08)
- **Story engine:** mystery (framed as David vs Goliath: one website vs every airline)
- **Protagonist:** the host as a cheapskate traveller who wants to fly Denver to New York cheaply. The underdog is the website. The airlines want it dead.
- **Central question:** opened at 0:36. Airlines keep trying to sue this site out of existence and keep failing. Why, and how did they create the problem themselves? The mechanism is answered at 4:52. The full answer comes at 7:56 (98% of the story): they can't stop it without pricing against their own business model.
- **Stakes:** about $54M a year leaks out of airline pockets. On the traveller's side, a $2,400 lawsuit.
- **Obstacle / antagonist:** the airlines (lawsuits, terms of service). The deeper antagonist is the airlines' own pricing model.
- **Beat chain:**
  1. 0:00 AND: a traveller flies two legitimate tickets.
  2. 0:12 BUT: years later the airline sues him.
  3. 0:36 BUT: the practice has a name, and a website airlines can't kill, because it isn't illegal.
  4. 1:01 SO: the host's own trip. The nonstop costs $324.
  5. 1:13 BUT: a $116 one-stop to another city starts with that same flight. Skip the second leg.
  6. 2:13 THEREFORE: at scale, the airlines lose tens of millions a year, so they hate it.
  7. 2:38 BUT: the blame flips. The airlines built the incentive.
  8. 3:03 THEREFORE: deregulation leads to hub-and-spoke networks.
  9. 3:27 BUT: the nonstop is cheaper to fly yet priced higher. Competition at non-hub cities explains the cheap fare.
  10. 5:04 SO: airlines knowingly sell cheap connecting seats. Skiplagging breaks that balance.
  11. 5:53 BUT: it's legal, like card counting. Airlines can only use their terms of service, and they almost never do.
  12. 6:42 THEREFORE: they sue the website instead.
  13. 7:19 BUT: one airline wins only on its logo, and the other suit is dismissed.
  14. 7:43 THEREFORE: they'll keep suing and failing. Sting.
- **Open loops:** why sue a man for skipping a flight? (0:12 → 6:42, dismissed). Why can't they kill the site? (0:36 → 7:56). How did airlines create it? (0:48 → 4:52). What is the practice? (0:52 → 1:37). What's the first leg of the cheap flight? (1:21 → 1:25). Why is the nonstop pricier? (3:27 → 4:52). How would they even crack down? (6:42 → 7:31). New loops open about every 26 s in the first 4 minutes, then the gaps get longer.
- **Turns:** 2:38 the blame flips from the website to the airlines. 3:27 the product people want most costs the airline least. 6:42 the opening lawsuit is dismissed. 7:19 the big courtroom win turns out to be about a logo.
- **Ending:** the airlines are stuck because of their own choices. The last line parodies a famous novel's closing line ("losing $180 at a time"), then adds a dry "there are worse things." It calls back to the cold-open lawsuit.
- **Where facts sit:** (1) The $54M-a-year figure is the answer to "why do airlines hate it so much?" (2) The hub-monopoly fact is the answer to "why is my $116 fare cheaper than the $324 one?"
- **Where jokes sit:** on the story beats of the host's trip (he flies to New York to spray his writers with water; a free man's right not to go to Boston). Asides on the FAQ (a chatbot answer) and on the lawsuit ("I've got status there").
- **Transplant template:** [Small person] did [harmless-looking act] and [giant institution] sued. The act has a name, and a [tool] the institution keeps failing to kill. BUT it's legal. Here's how it works, shown on my own example: [walkthrough]. BUT the institution created this itself, BECAUSE [history/economics]. THEREFORE it fights back with [rules], then [lawsuits]. BUT each attempt fails or wins only on a technicality. THEREFORE it can only win by breaking its own model. Sting that calls back to the opening.

### HAI — Why Rolex Won't Sell You Their Most Expensive Watches (1.57M, 7.85 min; story 6:39)
- **Story engine:** mission/quest (a doomed one)
- **Protagonist:** Amy, the "outside correspondent". She wants to walk out of a boutique with the priciest Daytona.
- **Central question:** opened at 0:06. Her mission is doomed. Why? Answered at 6:17 (94%): Rolex is owned by a foundation, so it has no reason to sell more.
- **Stakes:** low and comic: Amy fails. The real stakes are how much revenue Rolex leaves on the table.
- **Obstacle / antagonist:** Rolex's structure: no shareholders, a waitlist that isn't a queue, loyalty hoops.
- **Beat chain:**
  1. 0:00 AND: Amy is sent to buy the watch.
  2. 0:06 BUT: she's doomed. Not money, not her stained sneakers: the ownership structure.
  3. 0:36 SO: the founder's story.
  4. 1:00 THEREFORE: he leaves 100% of the company to a nonprofit.
  5. 1:37 BUT: both common beliefs are wrong. Rolex is both for-profit and not.
  6. 2:14 THEREFORE: no shareholders means no pressure to meet demand.
  7. 2:38 BUT: surely somebody buys it from Rolex? There's no online store, and the shelves are display only.
  8. 3:02 SO: Amy asks. No advisor slots are left, and the best she'd get is a years-long "expression of interest".
  9. 3:51 BUT: the waitlist isn't a queue. You must prove loyalty by buying lesser watches.
  10. 4:28 THEREFORE: a huge resale market. The watch flips far above retail the same day.
  11. 5:28 SO: twist: the fastest way to buy it is not to buy from Rolex, and to pay more.
  12. 5:40 BUT: why does Rolex tolerate this? The money flows to the foundation either way.
  13. 5:53 AND: other luxury brands also sell access.
  14. 6:05 THEREFORE: the verdict, plus a wry line.
- **Open loops:** why doomed? (0:06 → 6:17). Why is that employee counting watches? (0:24 → 0:36). What's a perpetual trust? (1:13 → 1:25). Surely someone buys it? (2:38 → 3:39). How long is the waitlist? (3:39 → 3:51). What if you're an investor? (4:28 → 5:16). Why does Rolex tolerate resale? (5:40 → 5:53). Median gap between new loops is about 55 s.
- **Turns:** 0:06 two fake reasons before the real one. 1:37 a double myth-bust. 4:52 the narrator derails mid-sentence at the price reveal. 5:28 the way to buy from Rolex is not to buy from Rolex.
- **Ending:** the central question is answered as a moral, closed with "may we all be so lucky". Amy is never shown leaving. The twist resolves her quest.
- **Where facts sit:** (1) The perpetual-trust ownership is the answer to "why won't they just make more?" (2) The $84k retail vs $120k resale prices are the answer to "who actually gets the watch?"
- **Where jokes sit:** on Amy's quest beats (the stained sneakers, the guy counting watches, the drinks counter). Fact-puns ("a self-winding trust"). A price-shock digression (Camrys, a house in Memphis) that comes back as a callback.
- **Transplant template:** [Proxy character] is sent to do [ordinary-sounding task]. She will fail. Not because of [obvious reason 1] or [obvious reason 2], but because of [hidden structure]. Cut between her attempt step by step and the history and structure that explain each wall she hits. BUT "surely someone does X?" Nobody does. THEREFORE the world builds a workaround. Twist: the fastest way to [goal] is to avoid [the obvious route]. Ending: why the institution doesn't care, plus a wry line.

### HAI — How Iran Smuggles 777 Airplanes (1.17M, 6.45 min; story 5:28)
- **Story engine:** mission/quest (a heist)
- **Protagonist:** Iran's airline, which wants modern wide-body jets despite a total ban.
- **Central question:** opened at 0:25. Nobody may sell Iran a Boeing, yet it got five. How do you smuggle five tracked jumbo jets? Answered at 5:03 (92%).
- **Stakes:** an aging, dangerous fleet on one side. A sanctions "hammer" on the other.
- **Obstacle / antagonist:** US export rules that reach any plane with 10% US parts, plus aviation oversight (registration, a 72-hour rule, transponders).
- **Beat chain:**
  1. 0:00 AND: new routes, new 777s.
  2. 0:12 BUT: that's illegal.
  3. 0:25 BUT: yet Iran has five. How?
  4. 0:45 SO: first, why they must: the rules cover every wide-body in production.
  5. 1:37 THEREFORE: no new planes and no parts, so smuggling is the only option.
  6. 2:01 SO: the target is a desert boneyard.
  7. 2:26 THEREFORE: step 1, a near-invisible US company buys the jets.
  8. 2:50 THEREFORE: step 2, a shell company in a country with weak oversight.
  9. 3:26 THEREFORE: step 3, Cambodia, where nobody is watching.
  10. 3:50 BUT: the last hop is the hardest. The jets are still US-made, and Iran may only host them 72 hours.
  11. 4:27 THEREFORE: an expired ferry permit gets a new month written over the old one.
  12. 4:39 SO: transponders go off over loosely watched airspace, and the jets vanish.
  13. 5:03 AND: they land, and nobody can be sanctioned because the company isn't real.
- **Open loops:** why illegal? (0:12 → 1:37). How do you smuggle them? (0:37 → 5:03). Why must Iran smuggle? (0:45 → 1:49). Is this a real company? (2:38 → 2:47). How past the last border? (3:50 → 4:39). How do you fly without a valid route? (4:15 → 4:27). Median gap is about 25 s, the tightest in the group.
- **Turns:** 0:12 the good-news launch flips to "this is illegal". 3:50 the laundering is done but the hardest step remains. 4:51 the planes vanish. 5:15 nobody can be punished because the buyer doesn't exist.
- **Ending:** the heist completes, closed with a mock fairy-tale line ("happily ever after").
- **Where facts sit:** (1) The 10%-US-parts rule is the answer to "why not just buy Airbus?" (2) The Madagascar and Cambodia registration hops are the answer to "how do you make a tracked plane untracked?"
- **Where jokes sit:** on each heist step (a one-page company website, a logo the host draws himself, a country with bigger problems). One long digression (the kid with a jar of Nutella).
- **Transplant template:** [Underdog] just did [impossible thing]. That's forbidden by [rule]. So how? First, why they had to: the rule's reach plus its human cost. The heist in steps: [step 1 launders X], [step 2 launders Y], [step 3 removes the last watcher]. BUT the final step is the hardest, because [constraint]. THEREFORE [clever trick or minor crime]. Payoff: vanish, then arrive. Sting: nobody can be punished, because [reason]. Fairy-tale close.

### HAI — Why It's Illegal to Call a Drug "Stroebenazoline" (877k, 8.65 min; story 7:33)
- **Story engine:** mission/quest (trial and error)
- **Protagonist:** the host as boss of "Half as Interesting Pharmaceuticals". He wants to name, then sell, a decongestant that only works on the left nostril.
- **Central question:** opened at 0:12–0:37. Who decides drug names, and why are so many banned? Let's try to name ours. The generic name is solved at 3:41. The brand name is still unresolved at 7:21 (97%).
- **Stakes:** set at 1:13: look-alike names kill about one person a day.
- **Obstacle / antagonist:** layered rulebooks (a national council, the WHO, the FDA) and a similarity-scoring tool.
- **Beat chain:**
  1. 0:00 AND: a pharmacy roll-call of real drug names.
  2. 0:12 BUT: naming has a million rules.
  3. 0:24 SO: the mission is to name our drug.
  4. 1:13 BUT: bad names kill people.
  5. 1:25 THEREFORE: generic name = stem + prefix. Attempt 1 uses the channel name.
  6. 2:26 BUT: rejected three ways.
  7. 3:15 SO: more attempts, each with two violations, and back-and-forth with the regulators.
  8. 3:41 THEREFORE: a generic name finally passes.
  9. 4:06 SO: now the money-making brand name, from a different regulator.
  10. 4:30 BUT: it copies the generic name.
  11. 4:54 BUT: it sounds like an existing drug.
  12. 5:43 BUT: it's trademarked, and a real hospitalisation story shows why that matters.
  13. 6:07 BUT: it promises results.
  14. 6:45 THEREFORE: a plain name passes the basics, then the checklist comes out a coin-flip.
- **Open loops:** how do we name it? (0:37 → 3:41). Why three names? (0:45 → 1:13). How will it start? (1:50 → 2:02). What will regulators say? (2:26 → 3:15). Can we get a brand approved? (4:06 → never fully). Will this one work? (repeats each attempt, closing within about 12 s). Might we be in business? (6:45 → 7:33). Median gap is about 50 s. In the brand-name half, every attempt opens a new micro-loop.
- **Turns:** 1:13 a silly game becomes life-and-death. 2:02 the scientific-looking syllables mean nothing. 5:43 one name means two different drugs in two countries. 7:21 even the "safe" name may fail.
- **Ending:** left open on purpose, with the joke that maybe he'll have to pay the half-million consultant. It doubles as the sponsor bridge.
- **Where facts sit:** (1) The stem rules (about 600 stems; each ending signals the drug type) are the answer to "how do we name it?" (2) The real cases of fast-sounding and hair-regrowth names that got rejected are the answer to "why can't my name promise anything?"
- **Where jokes sit:** almost all on the try-fail beats: each rejected name is a punchline, then a rule. One opening digression parodies a famous poem.
- **Transplant template:** [Host] must [make/name/build X] under [rulebook]. Stakes: getting it wrong causes [real harm, with a number]. Attempt 1: the obvious choice, rejected for [rule a, b, c]. Attempts 2 to n each break a new rule, and each rule is shown through a real case. THEREFORE gate 1 is passed. Gate 2 is a stricter authority: more attempts, more real cases. The final attempt passes the basics, BUT the last test is a toss-up. End on wry uncertainty.

### HAI — This Is The Shortest Train in the United States (766k, 8.2 min; story 7:06)
- **Story engine:** mystery (an origin story)
- **Protagonist:** the Dinky (an object), plus the town it serves. It wants to survive. The town wants a link to the main line.
- **Central question:** opened at 1:01, in two halves. Why does Princeton get its own 3-mile train? Answered at 3:27 (48%). Why keep it? Answered at 6:43 (95%).
- **Stakes:** in the 1860s, the town is cut off from the world. Today, $101M to replace it vs $15M to maintain it.
- **Obstacle / antagonist:** a rerouted main line, cars, a university that keeps building over the terminal, and replacement proposals.
- **Beat chain:**
  1. 0:00 AND: the longest route.
  2. 0:12 BUT: the shortest route, 8 minutes.
  3. 0:49 BUT: anywhere else this would be a shuttle bus. Why a train?
  4. 1:13 BUT: correction, a shorter line existed until 1957.
  5. 1:38 SO: the Dinky exists because of the big Northeast Corridor.
  6. 2:02 AND: the corridor was built in pieces along a canal, so no Dinky was needed.
  7. 2:51 BUT: the winding route was too slow once the Civil War raised demand.
  8. 3:03 THEREFORE: the line is straightened, and the town is left 3 miles away.
  9. 3:27 THEREFORE: the Dinky opens.
  10. 4:04 BUT: cars arrive, and the Dinky grows.
  11. 4:29 BUT: the university keeps cutting it shorter.
  12. 5:30 BUT: a replacement debate.
  13. 6:18 BUT: the counter-case, and it's 7x cheaper to keep.
  14. 6:43 THEREFORE: it survives. Sting about reopening the shorter rival line.
- **Open loops:** how did it get a branch? (1:01 → 3:27). Why keep it? (1:01 → 6:43). Was it always the shortest? (1:13 → 1:38). Why move the line? (2:51 → 3:15). How did it survive cars? (4:04 → 4:17). Do we still need it? (5:30 → 6:43). Median gap is about 80 s. The question arrives late (1:01), the slowest open in the HAI set.
- **Turns:** 1:13 the title claim gets a caveat. 3:03 a faraway war strands the town. 4:17 cars make it grow. 4:29 the institution it serves keeps shrinking it.
- **Ending:** both halves answered: "for now, it survives". The last joke calls back to the rival line, then the sponsor.
- **Where facts sit:** (1) The 1863 realignment is the answer to "why did a small town need its own branch?" (2) $101M vs $15M is the answer to "why not replace it?"
- **Where jokes sit:** wordplay on the name ("dinkying around", "not that dinky"), and the early-train failures (running it backwards). Little dialogue.
- **Transplant template:** This is the [biggest X]. This is the [smallest X]. Anywhere else the small one would be [cheap alternative], so why does it exist, and why keep it? A precedent or caveat. Origin: [a bigger system] changed because of [distant event] and left [place] cut off, so [small X] was born. BUT [threat 1]: it survives, even grows. BUT the very thing it serves keeps shrinking it. BUT a replacement debate. THEREFORE it survives on cost. Callback sting.

  1. 0:00 AND: a sightseeing flight.

  3. 0:24 BUT: yet 800+ flights a day land here. How?
  4. 0:49 SO: layer 1 rules.
  5. 1:01 AND: layer 2.
  6. 1:26 THEREFORE: the escalation ladder if you stray.
  7. 2:03 SO: so strict that emergencies divert to other airports.
  8. 2:27 BUT: that isn't even the strictest zone.
  9. 3:28 THEREFORE: planes snake along the rivers.
  10. 4:05 SO: the north-wind routine.
  11. 4:41 BUT: in a south wind, a straight approach would cost your licence, so pilots follow the river bends and turn at 200 ft.
  12. 5:43 BUT: it's also the busiest runway, and trainee controllers quit.
  13. 6:31 THEREFORE: do it right and it's the coolest job. Do it wrong and you see an F-16 up close.
- **Open loops:** what's that light? (0:06 → 0:12). How do airliners manage? (0:24 → 6:31). What if you screw up? (0:36 → 2:03). How serious is it? (2:15 → 2:27). Why is the approach so tricky? (3:28 → 5:30). What else? (5:43 → 6:19). Median gap is about 73 s.

- **Ending:** a two-sided verdict. The final image calls back to the cold-open F-16.
- **Where facts sit:** (1) Where the forbidden zone sits is the answer to "why do planes follow the river?" (2) The intersecting runways are the answer to "why is the airport so congested?"
- **Where jokes sit:** asides on the rules (the Santa-tracker people, a haunted-portrait joke) and the cold-open slapstick. Fewer jokes on the beats themselves than the other HAI videos.
- **Transplant template:** Cold open: [you] blunder into [danger zone] and [consequence] arrives. Yet [big number] of professionals do it daily. How? Rule layer 1, rule layer 2, the escalation ladder if you break them. BUT the inner zone is even stricter. THEREFORE professionals take [a weird route]. Mode A is easy. Mode B is extreme ([one vivid number]). BUT extra pressure: [traffic, time]. Ending: right = best day, wrong = worst day. Call back to the cold open.

### OverSimplified — WW2 Part 1 (105.3M, 13.75 min; story 12:10, after a 1:18 sponsor read)
- **Story engine:** versus/conflict (the grudges rise, then the Allies vs Hitler)
- **Protagonist:** Hitler, with Mussolini and Japan as fellow "wanters", against the Allies. The dictators want to undo their humiliation and take land. The Allies want to avoid another WW1.
- **Central question:** no question is spoken in the first 30 s. The video opens on a character (Mussolini's flip-flopping life). The loop is dramatic irony: viewers know a war is coming and watch the fuse burn. The explicit loop "will appeasement / Britain hold?" opens at 6:07 (40%) and is answered at 13:16 (98%).
- **Stakes:** Europe, and then Britain's survival.
- **Obstacle / antagonist:** for the Allies, Hitler plus their own wish to avoid war (appeasement, the Phony War). For Hitler, Britain's refusal to quit.
- **Beat chain:**
  1. 1:18 AND: Mussolini, a socialist arrested again and again.
  2. 1:54 BUT: he flips to pro-war and invents fascism.
  3. 2:48 THEREFORE: Italy feels cheated, and he takes power.
  4. 3:13 SO: Germany is humiliated and Hitler rises. Shared enemies make the two friends, and Japan joins.
  5. 5:29 THEREFORE: Hitler breaks the treaty step by step, and the Allies do nothing, twice.
  6. 6:07 BUT: appeasement.
  7. 7:10 BUT: he breaks the signed promise.
  8. 8:00 THEREFORE: a line is drawn at Poland. Pact with Stalin, invasion, war.
  9. 8:39 BUT: the Phony War: France is still fighting the last war.
  10. 10:22 THEREFORE: the Norway fiasco, and Churchill replaces Chamberlain.
  11. 10:47 BUT: Blitzkrieg through the "impenetrable" forest. France falls.
  12. 11:36 BUT: Britain won't quit, and the air war nearly breaks the RAF.
  13. 12:37 BUT: a small raid on Berlin makes Hitler switch to bombing London.
  14. 13:04 THEREFORE: the RAF wins, and the invasion is postponed.
- **Open loops:** what will pro-war Mussolini do? (1:54 → 2:19). Who's the new friend? (4:27 → 5:29). Will appeasement hold? (6:07 → 7:10). Will they fight for Poland? (8:00 → 8:27). Will anyone fight? (8:39 → 10:22). Hitler's "trick up his sleeve"? (10:47 → 11:00). Will Britain quit? (11:36 → 13:16). "But then..." (12:37 → 12:51). "Just the foot for now" (13:16 → Part 2). Major loops open about every 80 s, but dialogue sketches open and close a micro-question every 10–15 s (offer, refusal, reaction).
- **Turns:** 1:54 an anti-war socialist becomes a pro-war nationalist. 7:10 the signed promise is broken. 8:13 the Hitler-Stalin pact stuns the West. 10:47 the expected WW1 replay becomes Blitzkrieg. 12:51 the villain's anger at a tiny raid saves his enemy.
- **Ending:** Britain survives. The last line, "shot himself in the foot... just the foot for now", plants the Part 2 payoff.
- **Where facts sit:** (1) The list of Versailles terms is the answer to "why would Germans love this angry man?" (2) The belief that the Ardennes forest was impassable is the answer to "how did France fall so fast?"
- **Where jokes sit:** almost entirely on story beats, as dialogue where characters state their wants ("I want that thing", "What did you expect? I'm Hitler"). A few fake-outs (a made-up "Chamberlain Day" cut off by bad news). Barely any off-story digressions.
- **Transplant template:** [Character A] wants [X] and was wronged by [past deal], so he seizes power. [Character B] has a similar grudge. They team up over [a shared enemy]. [Defenders] want to avoid [past pain], THEREFORE they appease. A breaks the promise. A line is drawn, and conflict starts. BUT the defenders fight the last war and lose. A lone holdout refuses to quit. The villain nearly wins, BUT [a tiny provocation] makes him overreact, and the holdout survives. Foreshadow line: "just the foot, for now".

### OverSimplified — The Cold War Part 1 (75.1M, 16.05 min; story 14:34 after removing a merch plug and a mid sponsor)

### How a 1-minute version would compress the skeleton
Keep 5–6 beats, about 10 s each. The first BUT, the central question and the twist must survive. The AND setup collapses into half a line.
1. **Odd fact + protagonist with a want** (0–8 s). "Amy is sent to buy Rolex's top watch."
2. **BUT: it's impossible/illegal/doomed** (8–15 s). The central question, said out loud.
3. **THEREFORE / BECAUSE: one mechanism beat** (15–30 s). The single most counter-intuitive cause (foundation ownership; nonstops cost less to fly; the 10% US-parts rule).
4. **BUT: the "surely..." assumption fails** (30–40 s). One concrete number.
5. **Twist** (40–52 s). "The fastest way to buy it is not from Rolex."
6. **Verdict + sting / callback** (52–60 s).

What gets cut first: the history beats (founder biography, deregulation, canal routes), the second and third attempts in any try-fail chain (keep attempt 1 and the final one), and every digression. For an OverSimplified-style minute, keep: want A → want B → the clash (dialogue) → reversal → sting. Most of the beat chain disappears, but the dialogue joke at the reversal stays.

### 5 rules a writer can apply tomorrow
1. **Give the facts a person who wants something, in the first 15 s.** The 3 most-viewed HAI videos open on a protagonist with a concrete goal (a traveller sued for skipping a flight, Amy sent to buy a watch, an airline that "somehow" got five banned jets). The 3 least-viewed open on a train, a fly-over tour and a pharmacy shelf. On OverSimplified every one of the 6 states each character's want in a single line, usually as dialogue ("I want that thing"; "France wanted some stuff back"). If no character fits, make the host or a stand-in the protagonist (the host plays a pharma boss, a traveller, a stray pilot).
2. **Say "it's impossible" before saying how it works.** Every HAI video plants a BUT within 6–40 s that makes the premise look impossible: the mission is doomed, it's illegal, the site can't be sued, you'll be intercepted. That BUT then becomes the question, held for a median 94% of the runtime and answered in the last 5–8%. Our scripts gave the answer first.
3. **After the cold open, never add a fact with "and". Feed each fact as the answer to a question the story just raised.** 89% of the 166 beats are BUT/THEREFORE/SO, and 12 of the 18 AND beats are the very first line. Examples: the $54M lost per year arrives only after "why do airlines hate it?"; the Versailles terms arrive as the reason Germans loved Hitler. Test each beat: if it can't start with BUT or THEREFORE, cut it or move it.
4. **Pair every BUT with a THEREFORE.** Top videos run about 1:1 (30 THEREFORE to 34 BUT). The bottom three in each channel skew to BUT (23 to 41) and read as a list of obstacles: four BUTs in a row about the Dinky, five failed drug names. A consequence beat after each obstacle is what turns a list into a chain. OverSimplified's escalation ladders show this best: tax → boycott → repeal → tax again → troops → massacre → tea → crackdown → arm → first shot.
5. **Turn every 1.5–2 minutes, and end on a callback or a new loop, not a summary.** 55 turns across 12 videos (4–6 each). The most reused shapes: the blame flips (the website didn't cause it, the airlines did), the obvious route is the wrong one (to buy a Rolex, don't buy from Rolex), the villain repeats his greatest hit and it fails (the Ardennes twice; King George taxing again), and the fake-out ("and the war never happens. Except no"). Endings: 3 of 6 HAI videos call back to something from their first 75 s (the F-16, the lawsuit, the rival train line). The other 3 close on a wry one-liner instead. OverSimplified ends all 6 on a sting that opens the next question.

### Patterns worth stealing as named moves
- **Proxy quest cut against the explanation (HAI, Rolex):** a stand-in character's attempt is intercut with the history that explains each wall she hits. The quest supplies the "why?", and the history beat is the answer.
- **The heist ladder (HAI, Iran):** a 3–4 step "how they did it", where each step hides one more watcher, then "BUT the last step is the hardest".
- **The try-fail gauntlet (HAI, drug names):** each attempt breaks a rule, and each rule comes with a real case. It needs a THEREFORE gate halfway through or it turns into a list.
- **Want-collision dialogue (OS):** characters say their want out loud, get refused, react. Each exchange is a 10–15 s micro-loop and the joke lands on the reaction.
- **Rule of three escalation (OS):** Mussolini's three conquests, each worse than the last. Britain's tax, repeal, tax again. The fat, ill-bred boy line, three times.
- **Sarcastic dismissal as the plant (OS, French Revolution):** "What are they gonna do, revolt?" The question is asked sarcastically, and the rest of the video answers it.
