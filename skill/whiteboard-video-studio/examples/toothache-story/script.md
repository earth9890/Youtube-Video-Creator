# What Did Ancient Humans Do About Toothache?
# One spoken line per line. "#" = section note (not spoken). [S] = sarcasm beat. {0.6} = pause after (default 0.2).

# COLD OPEN (0:00–0:45)
Tonight, if one of your teeth starts to throb, you know exactly what to do.
You'll take a painkiller, complain to someone, and book a dentist.
Fourteen thousand years ago, a young man in the Italian Alps had the same pain.
His dentist had a rock. [S]
And that wasn't even the worst option.
For most of human history, there was no painkiller, no dentist, and no idea why teeth rot at all.
So what did ancient humans actually do about toothache?
The answer involves flint, worms, hair, frogs, and one very bad decision we are still paying for.
And to understand any of it, we have to start with why it hurts so much.

# WHY IT HURTS + IT COULD KILL (0:45–1:45)
Inside every tooth is a soft core of nerves and blood vessels, called the pulp.
It sits inside a box made of the hardest material in your body.
When bacteria get in, the pulp swells.
But the box doesn't.
So the swelling crushes the nerve, again and again, in time with your heartbeat.
That throbbing? That's your own pulse, used against you. [S]
And for most of history, the pain was the good news.
An infected tooth could spread into the jaw, the neck, and the blood.
Before antibiotics, one of these neck infections killed more than half the people who got it.
Three hundred thousand years ago, a man in what is now Zambia had cavities in ten of his fifteen remaining teeth.
Some researchers think the infection is what killed him.
So when our ancestors had toothache, they weren't being dramatic.
They were in danger.

# THE FIRST DENTISTS (1:45–3:30)
The first treatment we can see is surprisingly human.
About one hundred and thirty thousand years ago, a Neanderthal in Croatia had a tooth pushed out of place.
And they did what you do with your tongue every time something feels wrong.
They poked at it.
Over and over, with a stick or a blade of grass, until it left grooves in the teeth.
The first dental patient was also the first dentist. [S]
Then, fourteen thousand years ago, came the young man in the Alps.
His molar was rotting, so someone took a tiny, sharp flint point and scraped the decay out.
Scratch by scratch.
While he was awake.
Relaxing. [S]
It is the oldest dental operation ever found.
A thousand years later, near Lucca in Italy, someone went further.
They opened two rotten front teeth all the way to the nerve, and then they filled the holes.
With tar.
Mixed with plant fibres.
And what looks like hair.
Nobody knows why the hair was there.
Honestly, nobody wants to know. [S]
But it is the oldest filling on Earth.
Nine thousand years ago, in what is now Pakistan, farmers went full power tool.
They took the bow drills they used to make beads, and drilled into living people's molars.
We know they were alive, because the edges of the holes kept wearing down from chewing.
Eleven drilled teeth, from nine adults.
And here's the strange part.
Most of those teeth show no sign of decay.
So were they treating pain?
Testing a new hobby?
Nobody knows.
Nine thousand years later, the drill is still the scariest sound in the building. [S]
And in Slovenia, about six and a half thousand years ago, someone sealed a cracked tooth with beeswax.
So the tools were there.
What was missing was an explanation.

# THE WORM (3:30–5:00)
And when people don't have an explanation, they invent one.
In ancient Mesopotamia, scribes wrote down a myth about where toothache comes from.
The gods created the heavens, the earth, the rivers, the mud.
And the mud created a worm.
The worm asked to live between the tooth and the gum, and drink its blood.
That was it. That was the diagnosis. [S]
The cure was a spell, read aloud, while a peg was driven in.
You'd think a theory like that would last a few years.
It lasted for thousands.
In Egypt, a man named Hesy-Ra carried a title that may mean "chief of the dentists."
Or possibly "chief of the ivory carvers."
Historians are still arguing.
Either way, the man worked with teeth.
Egyptian doctors recommended honey, onions, and incense.
In Rome, the writer Pliny suggested rinsing your mouth with a frog boiled in vinegar.
Or, if you had a strong stomach, eating the frog. [S]
You may have heard that Romans also used urine as mouthwash.
They actually made fun of a foreigner for doing that.
So the urine thing was a roast, not a routine. [S]
People were trying to keep their teeth clean, too.
A papyrus from Egypt, about seventeen hundred years old, holds the oldest toothpaste recipe ever found.
Rock salt, mint, dried iris flower, and twenty grains of pepper.
Fresh breath, and a mouth on fire. [S]
Meanwhile, the Etruscans made tooth bridges out of gold.
And in China, around six hundred and fifty-nine, a medical book described filling teeth with a paste of silver, tin and mercury.
Over a thousand years before Europe did the same thing.
But everywhere, the worm survived.

# THE WORM WON'T DIE (5:00–6:00)
Partly because people kept seeing it.
Tooth-pullers would burn henbane seeds near a patient's mouth.
The seeds burst into tiny curled shapes that looked exactly like worms.
Others simply slipped bits of string into a bowl of water, and pointed.
There it is. Your worm. That'll be one coin. [S]
In medieval Europe, toothache had its own patron saint, and teeth were pulled by barbers, in the street, as a show.
In Paris, a giant tooth-puller called Big Thomas worked on a bridge, with a crowd watching.
Then in seventeen twenty-eight, a French surgeon named Pierre Fauchard wrote eight hundred pages on teeth.
He said there was no worm.
He filled holes with tin, lead, and even gold.
He scraped, he cleaned, he wrote down over a hundred dental diseases.
He blamed something else.
Sugar.

# THE SUGAR ERA (6:00–6:40)
Europe had just discovered it, and couldn't stop eating it.
In fifteen ninety-eight, a visitor described Queen Elizabeth the First's teeth as black, and blamed the English love of sugar.
Even the rich and powerful lost their teeth.
George Washington lost his first tooth at twenty-four.
By the time he became president, he had exactly one of his own left.
One. [S]
His dentures were not made of wood.
They were made of ivory, metal, animal teeth, and human teeth, including teeth his household bought from enslaved people.
After the Battle of Waterloo, scavengers pulled teeth from the dead soldiers, and dentists sold them as dentures.
People called them Waterloo teeth.
And you may have seen the famous claim that in the sixteen hundreds, "teeth" was one of the top causes of death in London.
It's on the old death records.
But the man who studied those records grouped "teeth" with children under two or three.
It wasn't adults dying of rotten teeth.
It was mostly babies, dying while their first teeth came in, from fevers and infections doctors didn't understand.
History is full of facts that are true on paper, and wrong in real life.

# THE TWIST (6:40–7:30)
But here is the part almost nobody talks about.
We tend to imagine that ancient humans had terrible teeth, and modern dentistry rescued us.
The bones say something different.
Most hunter-gatherers had cavities in only a small fraction of their teeth.
The big exception lived in Morocco, fifteen thousand years ago, and ate huge amounts of starchy acorns.
Half their teeth were rotten.
Then farming arrived, and grain and starch took over our diet.
Scientists who studied the DNA in ancient tooth plaque found the main cavity bacterium exploded right around that time, about ten thousand years ago.
Then it boomed again when cheap sugar and white flour filled the shops.
The worm was never real.
But the monster was.
And we fed it.

# THE RELIEF (7:30–8:15)
The good news also came from a toothache.
In eighteen forty-four, a dentist named Horace Wells watched a stage show where people breathed laughing gas and felt nothing.
The next day, he breathed it himself, and let a colleague pull out his own tooth.
He felt no pain.
A few weeks later, he tried to prove it in front of students at Harvard.
The patient cried out.
The audience laughed and called him a fraud.
Less than two years later, his former student used ether at a Boston hospital, in front of an audience of surgeons.
One of the greatest gifts in medicine was first shown to the world by dentists.
Dentists. Of all people. [S]

# TODAY + CALLBACK (8:15–end)
Today, untreated tooth decay is the most common health condition on the planet.
About two and a half billion people have it.
So tonight, if your tooth starts to throb, take the painkiller.
Book the dentist.
And remember the young man in the Alps, holding still while someone scraped his tooth with a rock. {0.6}
We beat the worm. {0.4}
We never beat the sugar. {0}
