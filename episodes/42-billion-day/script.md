# chapter: The prize
This is Dave. At one minute past midnight, Dave won a strange prize: a billion dollars. / The rule is simple. Spend it all by the next midnight, or whatever's left vanishes. //
"Breaking news. A local man has won a very large prize with a very short deadline." (@news) // [J]
"How hard can it be? I love spending money." (@dave) //
So what would happen if you had to spend a billion dollars in a day? /
Twenty minutes in, he's fourteen million dollars behind. By lunch, he's looking at a hundred thousand government forms. /
And by the evening, he's racing a deadline that isn't midnight. //
First, the maths. A day has eighty-six thousand four hundred seconds, / so Dave has to spend about eleven thousand five hundred dollars every second, including the seconds he spends deciding. // [J]
If he stops to sleep for eight hours, it's more like seventeen thousand dollars a second. So sleep is cancelled. // [J]
And a billion is bigger than it sounds. A million seconds is about eleven and a half days. / A billion seconds is about thirty-one years. //

# chapter: The supermarket
Dave starts at the only shop open at one in the morning, a twenty-four-hour supermarket. /
The trouble is, nothing here costs eleven thousand dollars. The priciest thing on the shelves is a forty-dollar cheese. // [J]
He fills a trolley with the most expensive things he can find, and twenty minutes later he has spent about four hundred dollars. /
In those twenty minutes, he was supposed to spend nearly fourteen million. // [J]
Then there's his credit card. It has a five-thousand-dollar limit, which at prize speed lasts less than half a second. // [J]
"Fine. I'll take the whole thing in cash." (@dave) //
But the bank doesn't open until nine, and by then, more than a third of Dave's day is gone. // [J]

# chapter: Ten tonnes of cash
Here's the thing about cash. An American banknote weighs about one gram, whatever it's worth, and the biggest note still issued is the hundred. /
So a billion dollars in cash is ten million notes, about ten tonnes of paper, roughly the weight of two big elephants. /
Stacked up, they'd make a tower about a kilometre high, taller than any building on Earth. //
Dave has come with a backpack. // [J]
America did once print a hundred-thousand-dollar note, in nineteen thirty-four. With those, his billion would weigh about ten kilos. /
But that note was only ever for moving money between Federal Reserve Banks, so Dave gets the elephants. // [J]
And counting the notes, one every second, would take about a hundred and sixteen days. Dave has fifteen hours. // [J]
So he takes out just a hundred thousand dollars. It weighs about a kilo, and it fits in the backpack. //

# chapter: A hundred thousand forms
His first stop is a car showroom, where the whole backpack goes on the shiniest car in the building. At prize speed, that's under nine seconds of shopping. // [J]
"Cash over ten thousand, son? That's a form. Every single time." (@old) //
He's right. In America, a business that takes more than ten thousand dollars in cash has to report it to the government within fifteen days. /
Spend a billion that way, just over ten thousand at a time, and that's nearly a hundred thousand forms. /
Each one asks for Dave's occupation, and he's no longer sure what to put. // [J]
His money would be gone by midnight, and the forms would keep arriving for two weeks. // [J]
By noon, Dave has spent about a hundred thousand dollars, and he still has nine hundred and ninety-nine million, nine hundred thousand left. // [J]

# chapter: Think bigger
Dave needs to think much bigger. A typical American home sells for about four hundred and seventeen thousand dollars. /
A billion buys about two thousand four hundred of them, one every eighteen seconds until midnight. /
So Dave rings an estate agent, who explains that even paying cash, buying a house usually takes a week or two of title searches, inspections and signatures. /
Dave would own two thousand four hundred houses, sometime next month. // [J]
So what costs a billion dollars all by itself? //
A standard rocket launch costs about seventy-four million dollars. A billion buys thirteen of them, and Dave has nothing to put on any of them. // [J]
NASA's Webb Space Telescope cost about nine point seven billion dollars, so a billion buys you roughly a tenth of a space telescope. //
"Which tenth? Can I have the gold bit?" (@dave) //
All the gold on Webb's mirrors weighs about fifty grams. Dave's tenth would weigh about the same as five of his banknotes. // [J]
And in twenty twenty-five, a university in Michigan opened a twelve-storey hospital with two hundred and sixty-four private rooms and twenty operating rooms. /
It cost about nine hundred and twenty million dollars, and took about a decade of planning. //
Dave doesn't have a decade. It's two in the afternoon, and he sits down on the pavement, next to his empty backpack. // [J]

# chapter: Twelve cents each
Then Dave has one last idea. If he can't buy one enormous thing, he'll buy everybody on Earth a tiny thing. //
About eight point two billion people live on Earth. / Split a billion dollars between all of them, and everyone gets about twelve cents. //
"Mum, a man called Dave just sent me twelve cents." (@kid) // [J]
And with eight hours left, Dave would need to make about two hundred and eighty-five thousand payments every second, to eight billion sets of bank details. //
A billion dollars is enough to change one life completely. Or to change every life on Earth, by twelve cents. //

# chapter: Six forty-five
At half past six, with his billion still in the bank, Dave learns something worse. Even if he finds something to buy, the money has to get there. /
Big payments between American banks often run through the Federal Reserve's wire system, / and it stops taking customer payments at six forty-five in the evening, Eastern time. //
Dave's real deadline was never midnight. It's six forty-five, and it's already six forty-one. // [J]
"Four minutes? My trolley took twenty." (@dave) //
He's lucky it's a weekday. On Saturdays and Sundays, that system doesn't run at all. // [J]

# chapter: Four minutes
And here's the twist. In those same four minutes, without even trying, the rest of us will spend nearly a billion dollars. //
All the world's economies together come to about a hundred and eighteen trillion dollars a year. / That's nearly four million dollars every second, or a billion about every four and a half minutes. / Dave needed eleven and a half thousand a second, and couldn't manage it. //
All of us, buying bread and bus fares and haircuts, spend Dave's whole prize in less time than it took him to fill one trolley. // [J]
Nobody needs ten tonnes of cash. Nobody even notices. // [J]
So the problem was never the billion. It was one person trying to spend it alone. //
So Dave makes one phone call. He can't build a hospital in four minutes, but he can pay the people who will. /
At six forty-four, he sends the billion to pay for one hospital, about the size of the one in Michigan. /
"That's about nine hundred and twenty million. Keep the change." (@dave) // [J]
The change is about eighty million dollars. // [J]

# chapter: Gone to work
So what would happen if you had to spend a billion dollars in a day? / Shopping, you'd lose. You'd run out of seconds somewhere under ten tonnes of cash and a hundred thousand forms. /
The only way is to pay lots of people at once, and then something odd happens. The money doesn't disappear. /
Economics textbooks put it in one line: one person's spending is another person's income. /
Builders and electricians get paid, and so do the people who make the steel and the glass. / Then their wages go on rent, food and haircuts. / And one day, a hospital that size needs about nineteen hundred staff. //
Even Dave's four hundred dollars of shopping paid part of a cashier's wages. She buys lunch. The café pays its baker. The baker buys flour. //
A hundred-dollar note lasts about twenty-four years, going from hand to hand to hand. /
How much each dollar adds to the whole economy is still argued about. / When America's Congressional Budget Office estimated it for government purchases, it gave a range from fifty cents to two dollars fifty. / It depends on who gets the money, and what they do with it next. /
What nobody says is that the money vanishes. //
Dave still loves spending money. It just turns out a billion takes a lot of help. //
At midnight, Dave's phone says zero, and nothing vanishes. /
(slow) His billion just went to work.
