# chapter: Dave Is Here
This is Dave. Dave has read one article about sourdough, and he is opening a bakery tomorrow. // [J]
"I understand bread now. Honestly, I could teach it." (@dave)
Dave's friend sends him a chart: a curve with a huge peak labeled "Mount Stupid," and an arrow that says "you are here." // [J]
The chart says the less you know, the more confident you feel. / So here's the question. Is it true that the less you know, the surer you are? //
The chart also says Dave is about to fall off a cliff. He has already set the price at thirty dollars a loaf. // [J]
To find out, we need a bank robber, eight comedians, and a card puzzle where the weakest group got, on average, less than one answer right. / Plus the chart itself, which has a problem. //

# chapter: The Man Who Wore the Juice
In nineteen ninety-five, a man named McArthur Wheeler walked into two banks in Pittsburgh, in broad daylight, with no disguise. / He was arrested that night, less than an hour after the surveillance video aired on the eleven o'clock news. // [J]
Shown the footage, he stared. "But I wore the juice." / He had rubbed lemon juice on his face, believing it made him invisible to cameras. // [J]
Two psychologists at Cornell University, David Dunning and Justin Kruger, opened their paper with him, and with an idea. / What if the skills you need to do something well are the same skills you need to notice you're doing it badly? //
Their first test was humor. They collected thirty jokes, some by Woody Allen, and asked professional comedians to rate each one. / Eight replied, and one comedian's ratings matched nobody else's, so the team dropped him. // [J]
Sixty-five Cornell undergraduates then rated the same jokes, and guessed where their sense of humor ranked among Cornell students. /
The weakest joke asked what is as big as a man but weighs nothing, and answered, "his shadow." The comedians gave it one point three out of eleven. //
"The shadow one is some of my best material." (@dave) //
The students whose ratings matched the comedians least, the bottom quarter, ranked at the twelfth percentile. They placed themselves at the fifty-eighth. //
The top quarter did the opposite. They guessed low. Slightly. //

# chapter: Zero Point Three
So the team tried subjects with less taste in them: logic and English grammar. The result repeated. / In grammar, the bottom quarter scored at the tenth percentile and put themselves around the sixty-seventh. /
Maybe they just misjudged classmates? So the team asked for raw scores. / On a twenty-question logic test, borrowed from a law school admissions guide, the weakest group thought they'd gotten fourteen right. They'd gotten nine and a half. //
Then, the strangest study. A hundred and forty undergraduates took a card puzzle called the Wason selection task, ten problems long. /
You see four cards, A, seven, B and four, and a rule: a vowel on one side means an odd number on the other. / Which cards must you turn over? Dave turns over all four, to be safe. // [J]
The bottom quarter, who landed at the thirteenth percentile, believed they'd solved about five and a half problems correctly. / On average, they had solved zero point three. //
Dave, who turned over every card, rates himself. / "I'd say I got a solid six." (@dave) // [J]
Then half the students got ten minutes of logic training, and the weakest group marked their own tests again. / Their guesses fell from five point three right answers to one. //
The authors saw the paradox. In their words, "the way to make incompetent individuals realize their own incompetence is to make them competent." //
In two thousand, the paper won an Ig Nobel Prize, for what the committee called a "modest report." // [J]

# chapter: It Takes One to Know One
Next, the team brought back the best and worst performers from the grammar study, and handed each of them five classmates' tests to grade. /
The strong graders could tell a good test from a bad one. The weak graders were worse at it, even when it was somebody else's work. //
After seeing five better tests, the weakest group still believed they had done just fine. // [J]
The strongest group changed their minds in the other direction. They had assumed everyone found grammar as easy as they did, and the five tests showed them otherwise. //
In the authors' words, "the miscalibration of the incompetent stems from an error about the self, whereas the miscalibration of the highly competent stems from an error about others." //
Dave is asked to rank five loaves from a baking contest. His favorite is the one that is still raw in the middle. // [J]

# chapter: Where Is Mount Stupid?
So Dave looks for his cliff. The paper has four graphs, one per study, each comparing what people thought they scored with what they really scored, by quarter. /
There is no peak. No valley. Nobody's confidence climbs a mountain and falls off it. //
And the weakest group didn't beat the strongest. In the logic and grammar studies, the bottom quarter still rated itself lower than the top quarter did. /
A footnote spells it out: incompetence is "a matter of degree," in specific domains, and "we make no claim that they would be incompetent in any other domains." //
"Mount Stupid" appears nowhere in the paper, and nobody seems to know who drew it first. // [J]
What the paper says is smaller and odder. In one skill at a time, the people at the bottom guess far above their score, / and the people at the top guess a little below. //
"The chart is made up? But my friend sent it to me." (@dave) / Dave has not yet learned to check where a graph came from. // [J]
So the chart is fake, the paper is real, and Dave's bakery is doomed. Case closed? Well, not quite. //

# chapter: The Noise Problem
The authors saw it coming. They wrote that the overestimation "could be taken as a mathematical verity." / If you score near the bottom, you have more room to guess too high than too low. //
Statisticians call part of this regression to the mean. Add the habit most people have, thinking they're better than average, and the pattern could appear on its own. /
Researchers named Krueger and Mueller raised the same objection in two thousand two, and Kruger and Dunning replied. //
In two thousand sixteen, Edward Nuhfer and colleagues showed that the usual way of graphing these data creates artifacts that invite misreading. Random numbers, graphed that way, look overconfident too. // [J]
In two thousand twenty, Gilles Gignac and Marcin Zajenkowski tested nine hundred and twenty-nine people on a reasoning test and asked them to rate their own intelligence. /
They found no extra misjudging at the bottom, and titled their paper, "The Dunning-Kruger effect is mostly a statistical artefact." //
"So it was never my fault. It was regression to the mean." (@dave) / Dave, relieved, orders a second tray of flour. // [J]
But in two thousand twenty-one, Rachel Jansen and colleagues replayed the original grammar and logic studies with about four thousand people each. /
Their modeling supported part of the original idea: low performers really are less able to tell whether an answer is right. //
And in two thousand twenty-two, Curtis Dunkel and colleagues, using a more representative sample, found a real effect. A tiny one. // [J]

# chapter: Ask the Baker
So here is what survives. Almost everyone thinks they're a little above average, and experts guess a little low. / People weakest at a specific skill tend to miss by the most, though less than the famous chart says. /
And it isn't about being stupid. It's one skill at a time. //
Comparing themselves with better performers didn't help the weak group. Learning the skill did, because the skill of doing something is the skill of judging it. //
Dave's bakery opens, in the form of one loaf. His friend, who bakes for a living, picks it up and knocks on it. //
"Dave, it's a brick. The crumb is dense, and the dough never proofed." (@doc) //
"Sorry, what exactly is a crumb? Is that the soft bit?" (@dave) / It is the first question Dave has asked all week. // [J]
"Sorry, I assumed everyone knew what a crumb was." (@doc) / That is the other half of the paper: the expert assumes everyone else finds it as easy as she does. //
He did not climb out of anything. He just found out there was a mountain, and that he couldn't see most of it. //
