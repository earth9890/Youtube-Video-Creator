# Episode 20 "You Are Not a Failure": sources (verified 2026-10-14)

| Script line | Claim | Source |
|---|---|---|
| "James Dyson, five thousand one hundred and twenty-seven prototypes before one vacuum worked." | Dyson's own account: "I made 5,127 prototypes of my vacuum before I got it right. There were 5,126 failures. But I learned from each one." Built by hand in his shed, roughly 1979–1984 (the James Dyson Foundation says "five years and 5,127 prototypes later"). The count is Dyson's own telling, not an independent tally, so the script presents it as the famous story, not as data. | Fast Company, "Failure Doesn't Suck" (interview with James Dyson, 2007): https://www.fastcompany.com/59549/failure-doesnt-suck · James Dyson Foundation, Our story: https://www.jamesdysonfoundation.co.uk/who-we-are/our-story.html · ScienceBlog on the count being self-reported and on his memoir *Invention: A Life*: https://scienceblog.com/?p=597407 |
| "Failure stories only come from winners." | Survivorship bias: the people who quit after failing are not the ones interviewed. This is the reason the study below is needed. It is a reasoning step, not a study result. | Same paper's framing: screening "if early-career failures screen out less-determined researchers" (Wang, Jones & Wang 2019, intro) |
| "To know for sure, you'd reject people at random. No ethics board allows that." | The paper uses a regression-discontinuity / near-miss design precisely because funding can't be randomly withheld: applicants just above vs just below the payline are compared as a natural experiment. "No ethics board" is the narrator's gloss. | Wang Y., Jones B.F., Wang D., "Early-career setback and future career impact", *Nature Communications* 2019;10:4331, doi 10.1038/s41467-019-12189-3: https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-019-12189-3 · PDF: https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/faculty/jones-ben/htm/Early%20Career%20Setback%20and%20Future%20Career%20Impact.pdf |
| "In twenty nineteen, Dashun Wang's team found it happened anyway." | Authors Yang Wang, Benjamin F. Jones and Dashun Wang (corresponding author), Northwestern University. | Same paper |
| "Nearly twelve hundred young scientists, a hair above or below a grant cut-off." | Junior PIs (first NIH application within the previous three years) applying for NIH R01 grants, 1990–2005. Normalised scores within (−5, 5) of the funding threshold: 561 narrow wins + 623 near misses = 1,184. | Same paper, Results and Fig. 1a |
| "Same records before." | "We see no significant difference between the two groups across any dimension we measured" (11 features incl. prior publications, prior hit papers, citations, years since first publication, institution). | Same paper, Fig. 1b caption |
| "That 'no' pushed over one in ten of them out for good." | "predicting more than a 10% chance of disappearing permanently from the NIH system" (abstract); the RD estimate is 12.6% over the next ten years. Near-miss group had 11.2% fewer active PIs the following year. | Same paper, abstract and "Screening hypothesis" section |
| "But over ten years, the ones who stayed landed hit papers a fifth more often than the winners." | Hit paper = top 5% of citations in its field and year. First five years: 16.1% of near-miss papers were hits vs 13.3% for narrow wins ("outperformed narrow wins significantly, by a factor of 21%"); a similar gap held in years 6–10 (odds ratio 1.19). Both groups published a similar number of papers. | Same paper, Fig. 2b and text |
| "Only the tough ones stayed? They checked. Even without the weakest winners, the near misses came out ahead." | Conservative removal: the authors removed narrow-win PIs who published the fewest hit papers but the most papers until both groups had the same attrition; "near misses still outperformed narrow wins" (odds ratio 1.17). The authors say screening "may have played a role, but it appears insufficient to entirely account for the observed difference". | Same paper, Fig. 3 and "Screening hypothesis" section |
| "One 'no' didn't decide who they became. Staying did." | Narrator's summary of the two findings: same records before the score; after it, the outcome split by whether people left (attrition) or stayed (outperformance). Authors: "these findings are consistent with the concept that 'what doesn't kill me makes me stronger'". | Same paper, abstract |
| "Tonight, write down the date of your next try." | The one safe action. The study's advantage only appeared among those who stayed in the system; writing down a date for the next attempt is a small way of not quitting on the day of the "no". It is a suggestion drawn from the finding, not a tested intervention, and no outcome is promised. | As above |

## Checked and NOT used
- **J.K. Rowling's "twelve rejections":** widely repeated, but the count varies by source (12 in most retellings via her agent; Rowling herself reportedly told CBS 60 Minutes in 2002 that "four or five" publishers turned it down). Her 2016 posted rejection letters were for *The Cuckoo's Calling* (Robert Galbraith), not Harry Potter. Cut: the number is shaky and one famous example (Dyson) does the job of the "survivor story". https://www.cbsnews.com/news/the-magic-behind-harry-potter/ · https://www.cbsnews.com/amp/news/j-k-rowling-shares-two-rejection-letters-for-the-cuckoos-calling
- **Dyson timeline:** sources say 4, 5 or 15 years (15 counts until DC01 sold in 1993). The script gives no duration.
- **Generalising beyond science:** the study is about NIH-funded junior biomedical scientists. The script names who was studied and does not claim the effect for job applications; Dave's email is the story frame. The near-miss advantage is an average effect with small effect sizes (Cohen's d ≈ 0.06–0.08 for citations).
- **Lefkowitz "Science is 99 percent failure" and John Roberts "I wish you bad luck":** quoted in the paper's intro; colourful but they answer no question the story asks.
