# chapter: That's one
This is Dave. It is three in the afternoon, Dave is presenting the quarterly figures, and a man in a cardigan at the back of the room yawns. //
"That's one. I'm keeping count, by the way." (@dave) //
Within a minute, six people at the table have yawned, one after another, like dominoes. Dave takes this personally. // [J]
So why is yawning contagious? And does it mean Dave is boring? //
The answer involves a bag of frozen peas, a chimpanzee, and a review whose own authors admit they can't prove it. //
And fair warning. Researchers have found that simply reading or thinking about yawning can set one off.
So you'll probably yawn at some point in the next few minutes. It isn't you. It's the topic. // [J]
Let's start with what a yawn is. //

# chapter: What a yawn is
The word comes from the Old English for to gape, which is why chasms yawn too. // [J]
The psychologist Robert Provine at the University of Maryland has studied yawns for decades. The average yawn, he found, runs for about six seconds. //
There are no half-yawns. Once it starts, it runs its course with the inevitability of a sneeze. //
Provine had volunteers sit alone in an isolation chamber and press a button when they yawned, because people hold yawns back when they think they're watched.
Dave's colleagues, who are being watched by Dave, have been trying. // [J]
Dave tries to stifle one by clenching his teeth. Provine tested exactly this.
It gives you the sensation of being stuck in the middle of a yawn, which is somehow worse. // [J]
Yawning is ancient. Fish, turtles, crocodiles and birds all do it. People start long before they're born: it's present by the end of the first trimester of pregnancy. //
And it feels good. Volunteers rated yawning about eight and a half out of ten for pleasure. //
So a yawn is a six-second, unstoppable, built-in jaw stretch that nobody orders and most people enjoy. // [J]
Yawns also come in bouts, about sixty-eight seconds apart on average. Dave's meeting is beating the average. // [J]
Provine also noted that even a picture of a yawning face can provoke one. Dave's slide eleven has a stock photo of a yawning hippo. This is not helping. // [J]
Dave has a theory about the room's air quality. / "It's because the room has run out of oxygen. I'll open a window." (@dave) //
He opens the window. The yawns continue. Experiments that raise or lower oxygen and carbon dioxide in the blood have failed to change how much people yawn. // [J]
So where does the yawn come from, and why does it spread? The first idea is about temperature. //

# chapter: The brain-cooler
In two thousand seven, the psychologists Andrew Gallup and Gordon Gallup proposed that yawning cools the brain.
A yawn pulls in a rush of air and stretches the jaw, which could send cooler blood towards the head. //
They tested it on contagious yawns. Forty-four students watched videos of other people yawning. //
Of the students who breathed normally or through their mouths, about half yawned. Of the students told to breathe only through their noses, none did. //
In a second experiment, students held a pack to their foreheads. With a warm or room-temperature pack, forty-one percent yawned. With a cold pack, nine percent. //
Dave first runs a quick test of his own. He asks the room to think about yawning, for science. Nine people yawn.
He is thrilled, until he remembers whose meeting it is. // [J]
Then Dave decides to try the cooling idea. At the next meeting, he sits breathing only through his nose, with a bag of frozen peas on his head. // [J]
He doesn't yawn once. Nobody else notices anything unusual, which says something about the meeting. // [J]
According to the Gallups, contagious yawning evolved to keep groups alert. If one person's brain is overheating, the others catch the yawn, and the whole group sharpens up. //
It's a neat idea. And it has a sceptic. //

# chapter: The critics
If you've just yawned, that was the topic. Carry on. // [J]
In twenty ten, a Swiss team led by Guggisberg reviewed the evidence on why we yawn. //
Their conclusion: theories that give yawning a physiological job, such as breathing, arousal or temperature, lack evidence.
No study had found a specific effect of a yawn on the person who yawns. //
Their preferred idea is communication. A yawn tells the people around you that you're drowsy, bored or a little stressed. //
Dave's Brain looks up from a stack of books. / "I didn't order that yawn, Dave. I found out when the room did." (@brain) // [J]
And look at the numbers in the cooling study. Of the twenty-two students in the nose-breathing and cold-pack groups combined, one yawned. Small groups can mislead. //
Gallup replied that the review was unbalanced and left out evidence for cooling. Both sides have kept writing.
So the cooling idea is alive, but it may not carry the whole load. //
Meanwhile, Dave's meeting has reached fourteen yawns. //

# chapter: The closeness gradient
The second idea is about people. Perhaps contagious yawning is empathy. //
In two thousand eleven, Ivan Norscia and Elisabetta Palagi watched a hundred and nine adults from four continents, going about their lives for a year.
They logged four hundred and eighty yawns, and who yawned in response. //
Only one thing predicted whether a yawn spread: how close the two people were. Gender and nationality made no difference. //
It spread most to relatives, then friends, then acquaintances, and least to strangers. Strangers also took longer to respond. //
Dave checks his meeting. His mum, who came to watch, yawned within two seconds. His boss still hasn't. // [J]
"That's one for Mum. She's a tough crowd." (@dave) // [J]
So a yawn is the one thing you catch faster from your mother than from a stranger. //
Maybe that's why other animals do it too. //

# chapter: Dogs and chimps
In two thousand eleven, Matthew Campbell and Frans de Waal showed twenty-three chimpanzees videos of yawning chimps. The chimps yawned more at familiar group members than at strangers. //
And they watched the strangers' yawns more closely. The chimps looked through a small eyehole, so the researchers knew they were looking, and the bias remained. //
Dogs came next. In two thousand eight, a British team showed twenty-nine dogs a person yawning. Twenty-one dogs yawned. When the person made ordinary mouth movements instead, none did. //
The authors noted that stress or tension could explain the yawns, and the study couldn't tell the two apart. //
Dave, who has a dog, tries it. The dog watches Dave yawn, looks at him thoughtfully, and goes back to sleep. // [J]
Which is roughly where the science landed. In two thousand nine, a later team tested fifteen dogs. One yawned more at the yawn videos. //
So dogs may or may not catch yawns. And that is the thing about all of this. //

# chapter: Nobody knows
In twenty seventeen, a review examined the evidence for the empathy theory. One of its two authors was Andrew Gallup, the brain-cooling researcher. //
Their conclusion: the evidence linking contagious yawning to empathy is inconsistent and inconclusive. //
One problem is attention. We look more closely at people we know, so we may simply notice their yawns more. That alone could produce the closeness gradient. //
They add that contagious yawning might even be a by-product of how groups naturally copy each other, and not something that evolved for a reason. // [J]
So here is where the science stands. A yawn is real, and it spreads fast. It spreads faster between people who are close.
And three ideas are live: a brain-cooler, a signal that keeps a group alert, and a kind of social glue. //
Nobody has settled which. The honest answer to why yawning is contagious is that we're still working on it. //
Which is a lot of science for a six-second stretch. // [J]

# chapter: The yawn I promised
Dave stops taking it personally. A yawn is not a review. At worst, it means a room full of people close enough to catch each other's. //
He finishes the figures, thanks everyone, and as he puts the cap back on his pen, he yawns. //
"That's one. I'm keeping count, by the way." (@dave) //
The table follows. His mum first. Then the man in the cardigan. His boss, last, with a look of mild alarm. //
A yawn lasts about six seconds. It crossed that table faster than the quarterly figures did. // [J]
(slow) And here's the yawn I promised you. Go on. I won't tell.
