# chapter: Dave in shorts

# chapter: The spark
Fire first. In Wonderwerk Cave, in South Africa, researchers found burned bone and ashed plant remains from about a million years ago. /
It's the earliest secure evidence of burning in a cave. It doesn't prove who lit it, or whether they could light it again. //
For that, we need Barnham, in Suffolk, England. Archaeologists there found a patch of clay baked by heat of more than seven hundred degrees. /
It took the team four years of tests to rule out a wildfire. That's a lot of effort to prove a campfire was a campfire. // [J]
Beside it lay flint hand axes cracked by fire, and two tiny fragments of pyrite, each about two centimetres long. //
Pyrite is better known as fool's gold. These people were not fooled. // [J]
Strike it against flint and it throws sparks. It doesn't occur naturally in that landscape, so somebody carried it there. /
One of the dig's researchers said it shows they had the ability to make fire at will. //
The site is about four hundred thousand years old. Before it, the oldest clear evidence of making fire was about fifty thousand years old. /
That pushes the date back by around three hundred and fifty thousand years. Quite a lot. //
The Natural History Museum notes that fire let people stay warm, push into colder regions, and cook and make tools after sunset. //
Dave strikes two stones together for twenty minutes. He now has sore hands and no fire. // [J]
And when he finally gets one, it warms the side of him that faces it. The other side is still in the Ice Age. // [J]

# chapter: The coat
Which brings us to the coat. And the problem with coats is that they rot. /
There are almost no ancient clothes left to find. So scientists look for other signs. //
In twenty eleven, Melissa Toups and her colleagues studied lice. Clothing lice live in garments, and they split from head lice at some point in the past. /
The paper's title says it indicates early clothing use by modern humans in Africa. /
They estimated that happened at least eighty-three thousand years ago, and possibly as early as one hundred and seventy thousand. //
So the oldest evidence for the coat is a louse that moved into it. // [J]
Dave, who has no clothes, checks himself for clothing lice. For the first time in his life, he hopes to find some. // [J]
There are tools, too. At Contrebandiers Cave, in Morocco, a team led by Emily Hallett found more than sixty bone tools. /
They're between ninety and one hundred and twenty thousand years old. /
Nearby carnivore bones had cut marks consistent with skinning for fur. The team calls it highly suggestive proxy evidence for the earliest clothing. //
In France, Neanderthal-era tools called lissoirs, made of animal rib, are about fifty thousand years old. /
Their wear matches modern tools for making hides softer and more waterproof. A rib, used as fabric softener. // [J]
Then comes the needle. By about forty thousand years ago, eyed bone needles turn up at Denisova Cave in Siberia. /
A coat sewn to fit stops the wind getting in. Nobody in the Ice Age wore a poncho on purpose. // [J]
The oldest complete outfit anyone has found is Ötzi's, a man frozen in the Alps about five thousand three hundred years ago. /
His shoes were laced with cattle leather, his loincloth was sheep, and his quiver was roe deer. His hat was brown bear. //
His coat was sheep and goat. Dave takes the hat first. And then, after some thought, the loincloth. // [J]

# chapter: A house of bones
A coat is not much use in a snowstorm with no roof. //
In central Ukraine, at a site called Mezhyrich, archaeologists found four round structures, twelve to twenty-four square metres each. /
They were built from hundreds of mammoth bones and tusks, and surrounded by pits, hunting weapons, ivory ornaments and workshops. //
In twenty twenty-five, a team led by Wei Chu dated one of them to about eighteen thousand years ago, in the harshest phase of the last Ice Age. /
Their results suggest it was used, on and off, for as long as four hundred and twenty-nine years. /
They say it shows how communities turned the remnants of giant animals into protective architecture. //
Dave asks about the mortgage. It would have been a long one. // [J]
Archaeologists still argue about what these structures were. Houses, food stores, burial sites or something more ritual. /
The new dating supports the dwelling idea, but it isn't settled. //
Whatever they were, building one meant gathering hundreds of enormous bones in a freezing landscape. /
Dave gathers one bone, and has to sit down. // [J]
So now Dave has fire, a coat and a house. He is almost warm. Almost. /
He's also sure he knows the missing piece. "Evolution. Some people were just built for the cold." (@dave) //

# chapter: Built for the cold
He has a point. Partly. The point is Neanderthals. //
They lived across Europe and western Asia for about three hundred and sixty thousand years. /
Neanderthals or their ancestors lived through at least eight glaciations, with shorter warm spells in between. //
The Smithsonian says their short, stocky bodies were adapted to winter climates. Dave, who is tall and thin, takes this personally. // [J]
A twenty twenty-five review, led by Trenton Holliday and including Cara Ocobock, goes through the evidence. /
It finds big bodies, broad trunks and short limbs, all consistent with holding on to heat. /
The deeper physiology, like a fast metabolism, it calls inferential but plausible. //
Even the Neanderthal nose. Long considered a puzzle, it now looks well suited to cold, dry air. A kind of air conditioner, working in the other direction. //
Dave, who has no idea where his air conditioner is, checks his nose. // [J]
But here's the twist. The same review says the body was probably not what did most of the work. /
It says technological buffers, including fire, hide processing tools and possible clothing and footwear, "likely played a primary role" in reducing cold exposure. //
Some populations lived outside the glacial extremes, but all of them faced periodic cold stress. Survival, it says, depended on a diverse set of strategies. //
The body helped. The fire, the hides and the shelter did most of the work. Fair. //
And the body had a price. The review points to a high metabolism and an energy-dense diet. /
In other words, running warm means eating a lot. Cold is partly a food problem. //
Even digesting protein makes heat, so a meaty dinner works like a small stove. //
The review also raises brown fat, a tissue that burns energy just to make warmth. That part is still a guess. //
Not one special trait, say the authors, but an integrated response, shaped by geography, culture and genetics. //

# chapter: Dave keeps the shorts
So how did ancient humans survive the cold? /
They didn't wait for evolution to warm them. They changed what was around them. //
They carried a fire kit. They wore the animals they ate. And they built houses out of what was left over. //
The cold wasn't beaten by anyone being tough. It was beaten by being organised. //
Dave spends the night in a house of bones, wearing a bear hat, with a fire and a very large dinner. /
When he wakes up, the meter says warm. //
(slow) He is dry, fed and warm, and has never been so pleased to see a mammoth rib. Dave keeps the shorts.
