# chapter: Dave Loses the Clock
This is Dave. Dave is tired of being told what time it is, so he has unplugged every clock and taped cardboard over every window. // [J]
"From now on I wake up when I'm ready and eat when I'm hungry. My body knows what time it is." (@dave)
Which raises a question nobody properly tested until nineteen sixty-two. / If you take away every clock and every sunrise, how long is a day to a human body? //
The man who went to find out was a twenty-three-year-old French geologist named Michel Siffre. / He took off his watch, climbed down a rope into the Alps, and stayed in the dark for sixty-three days.
Psychologists have no official name for what happened to him down there, so we'll borrow his: the Siffre effect. / It's what your body and your sense of time do when nobody tells you the time.
It involves a glacier, a telephone, and a man who counted to a hundred and twenty and took five minutes. / Dave is keeping score too, with one pencil mark on the fridge for every sleep. Remember the fridge. //

# chapter: A Hole in the Alps
In nineteen sixty-one, Siffre found an underground glacier at the bottom of the Scarasson abyss, in the Maritime Alps. His plan was to camp beside it for fifteen days.
Then he started thinking about space. / Yuri Gagarin had just flown, and everyone assumed that with no day or night, a person would simply carry on in a twenty-four-hour cycle. Nobody had checked. // [J]
So Siffre stretched fifteen days into two months, and he was honest about the planning. / "I was a geologist, not a biologist. I raised the funds myself, picked the two months arbitrarily and invented the experimental protocol." // [J]
On the sixteenth of July, nineteen sixty-two, he took off his watch and went down a rope ladder, a hundred and thirty meters into the dark.
His camp was a tent beside the glacier, with one phone line up to his team. He would call when he woke, when he ate, and just before he slept. /
The team had one rule. Answer anything he asks, except the time. // [J]
Dave's sister has agreed to the same rule. She posts a sandwich through the letterbox every day and says nothing else. // [J]
Dave eats it, and honestly can't tell whether it's breakfast, lunch, or his second sandwich. / "Nobody has told me otherwise, so I'm going to say it's probably Tuesday." (@dave) // [J]
Siffre wasn't the first man to try a cave. In nineteen thirty-eight, the sleep researcher Nathaniel Kleitman spent thirty-two days in Mammoth Cave, Kentucky, on a forced twenty-eight-hour day. /
His body temperature kept its own twenty-four-hour rhythm, and he still got sleepy at ten at night, which is awkward for the man who ran the world's first sleep laboratory. // [J]
Siffre's idea was different. Impose nothing. Let the body choose. //

# chapter: The Long Day
The glacier camp was three degrees Celsius, his feet were permanently wet, and lumps of ice and rock sometimes crashed down nearby.
After one rockfall he stayed on the phone for more than ten hours, and it didn't feel like ten. He said later, "Two seconds passed, I perceived one."
So he tested it. He asked his team to listen while he counted from one to a hundred and twenty, one number a second. It took him five minutes. // [J]
Dave tries the same thing. He counts to a hundred and twenty out loud, and he's quite sure it took two minutes. His sister has been told not to say. // [J]
His body had a plan of its own. He went to sleep a little later each day, until his cycle came out at about twenty-four hours and thirty minutes.
Half an hour doesn't sound like much. For Dave, it means bedtime slips by thirty minutes every day. / By day twelve he's six hours behind the sun, eating breakfast at lunch. // [J]
On the fourteenth of September, the team finally phoned down to say the experiment was over. Siffre was stunned. / "I thought that it was only August twentieth."
Twenty-five days had vanished. "My psychological time had compressed by a factor of two." Two months had felt like one.
When he said the body keeps a clock of its own, the scientists who read his results didn't buy it. "They thought I was mad." //

# chapter: Forty-Eight Hours
So he kept going. Over the next decade, volunteers spent months underground. / One man slept for thirty-three hours straight, and Siffre feared he was dead. "And then at thirty-four hours, he snored." // [J]
Some volunteers drifted into days of forty-eight hours, so in nineteen seventy-two, Siffre went back to catch one himself. / "I decided I would stay underground for six months to try to catch the forty-eight-hour cycle."
This time it was Midnight Cave, in Texas, backed by NASA and the French Defence Ministry, who wondered how sailors on submarines would cope. /
As he went down, his mother said, "He must be mad. But there's no means of stopping him." // [J]
Even in a sealed cave, the weather found him. Lightning ran down the cable attached to his electrodes and gave him a violent jolt. // [J]
Twice, he caught a forty-eight-hour day, thirty-six hours awake, then twelve asleep. And here is the strange part. / "I couldn't tell the difference between these long days and the days that lasted just twenty-four hours." // [J]
But the cave was not kind to him. Three months in, he tore the sensors off, convinced he was wasting his life. / Ten days later, a sense of duty made him put them back on.
He said the cave became his prison. A small rat kept him company, and the low mood lasted for months after he left. /
When his team called down after two hundred and five days to say it was time to come out, he didn't believe them. "You're just messing with me." //
For decades, that was the textbook answer. Without the sun, a human day runs long, about twenty-five hours, and the clock shortens with age. / Dave sets his alarm for twenty-five hours and feels scientific. // [J]

# chapter: The Lamp
But in nineteen ninety-nine, a team at Harvard Medical School, led by Charles Czeisler, tried again, with carefully controlled light. / They tracked melatonin, body temperature and cortisol.
The average day came out at twenty-four hours and eleven minutes. Not twenty-five. Not forty-eight. And the same for young and old. //
The earlier numbers, they said, came from people living in light levels "sufficient to confound" the result. / Light is the steering wheel of the body clock, and a volunteer who can switch on a lamp whenever he likes is steering all the time.
So the clock was real, which was the hard part. Siffre's number was just a little too long. /
He kept testing himself. At sixty, in nineteen ninety-nine, he rang in the new millennium underground with foie gras and champagne, three and a half days late. // [J]
For Dave, eleven minutes a day is a very slow drift. It would take about sixty-six days to put his breakfast at lunch, which is roughly how long Siffre stayed down. // [J]
So was all that suffering for nothing? Not at all. In the seventeen hundreds, a French astronomer named de Mairan put a mimosa plant in constant darkness. / Its leaves kept opening each morning anyway. A plant had run the experiment two hundred years early. //
In two thousand seventeen, the Nobel Prize went to three scientists who found the genes behind that clock in fruit flies, and the same machinery runs in us. / And the other half of the Siffre effect still holds. Take away the cues, and hours bend. //

# chapter: Probably Tuesday
On the ninth day, Dave peels the cardboard off the window. The sun lands on his face. / His sister holds up her phone. "Probably Tuesday," he'd said. It's Friday. //
But the sun does something the fridge never could. It resets his clock, which is what it does every morning, for everyone.
Your body runs a little over twenty-four hours, and the sky quietly corrects it. The Nobel committee describes the mechanism by which light synchronizes the clock. //
Michel Siffre died in Nice in August two thousand twenty-four, aged eighty-five. He once wrote about time this way. /
"I had the impression of being immobile, and yet I knew I was being carried along by the uninterrupted flow of time." //
