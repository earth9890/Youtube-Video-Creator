# chapter: Noon
This is Dave. Dave has a meeting at noon, on the day of the full moon, and he is determined not to be late. //
There are three problems. It is forty thousand years ago, he has no watch, and nobody has invented noon. // [J]
"What time is it? What day is it? Is anyone else even coming?" (@dave) //
He tries asking a passer-by when noon is. The passer-by points at the sky and keeps walking. // [J]
So how did ancient humans know what day it was? //
The answer runs through a notched bone, a field of pits in Scotland, / a tomb that lights up for seventeen minutes a year, and a sundial that ran Rome for ninety-nine years while being wrong. //
Dave checks his empty wrist twice, and asks to start with the oldest clue. //

# chapter: The notched bone
In a cave in the Lebombo Mountains, on the border between South Africa and Eswatini, archaeologists found a baboon's leg bone about eight centimetres long. /
It has been dated to between forty-two and forty-three thousand years old, and it carries notches. //
Twenty-nine of them. A lunar month lasts about twenty-nine and a half days. //
So the bone might be a moon counter. Or a tally stick. Or a record of a ritual, which is what its discoverer, Peter Beaumont, thinks. //
And the bone is broken at one end, so the real count could be higher. / Dave, whose entire calendar is this bone, takes the news badly. // [J]
Other notched bones are even older, around eighty thousand years, and nobody can say if the marks are counting or decoration. //
The Ishango bone from the Congo, about twenty thousand years old, has the same problem. / Some say tally marks, some say a lunar calendar, and some say a better grip. //
A calendar, a tally, or a handle. Dave would like to know which. // [J]
So far, the oldest time-keeper is a stick that argues with itself. It may be the first calendar ever accused of being a handle. // [J]

# chapter: Twelve pits
A bone can only record. It can't plan. For that, you need something you can see from a distance. //
At Warren Field, in Aberdeenshire, Scotland, hunter-gatherers dug a row of twelve pits nearly ten thousand years ago. /
In twenty thirteen, a team led by Vince Gaffney argued that the pits mimic the phases of the Moon, to track months across a year. //
The row also lines up with the sunrise at midwinter. That would let people correct the drift between the Moon's year and the Sun's. /
Gaffney said it came almost five thousand years before the first formal calendars in the Near East. //
Dave asks which pit is today. // [J]
Nobody knows. And one of the team's own advisers, Clive Ruggles, adds a caution. /
He says the site did not mark particular moonrises, because those patterns are far too complicated. //
The argument, he says, is that it combines several cycles to track time, symbolically and practically. //
So the oldest possible calendar is partly a map, and partly a promise. // [J]

# chapter: Let the sun do it
Then people stopped digging the calendar and started aiming it at the sun. //
In Peru, at a site called Chankillo, thirteen stone towers stand along a ridge in the desert. /
Wood from the towers dates to about three hundred BCE. Stand in the right place, and over the year the sun rises behind a different tower. //
On the December solstice, seen from the west, it rises over the southernmost one. /
Ivan Ghezzi and Clive Ruggles reported in twenty oh seven that it worked as a way to identify each day of the seasonal year. //
Thirteen towers. Dave tries to read the date, and finds he first has to stand in exactly the right place. // [J]
Ghezzi pointed out something else. If you only wanted to measure seasons, you wouldn't build anything this big. /
His view was that it also carried a political message, about a ruler's close relationship with the sun. //
In other words, a calendar that was also a boss. // [J]
Newgrange, in Ireland, is over five thousand years old. / At sunrise on the winter solstice, light enters a small opening above the door and travels down a long passage into the chamber. //
At eight fifty-eight in the morning, a narrow beam reaches the floor. Seventeen minutes later, it leaves. //
If Dave misses it, he waits a year. // [J]
And thanks to changes in the tilt of the Earth's axis, the beam now stops two metres short of where it used to reach. /
Even the sun, it turns out, runs a little late. // [J]

# chapter: What time is it
But a calendar tells you which day, not which hour. And Dave has a noon meeting. //
In the Valley of the Kings, in Egypt, a team from the University of Basel found a flat piece of limestone. /
It has a semicircle drawn on it, split into twelve sections, with a small dent that once held a rod to cast a shadow. //
It dates to the thirteenth century BCE, and the workmen's huts were close by. They may have used it to count their working hours. //
The world's first clock-watchers may have been tomb builders. // [J]
Around four hundred and thirty BCE, Athens was timing speeches in court with water clocks. /
Water drained from one pot into another, and the amount depended on how serious the case was. Both sides got the same amount of time. //
A juror was even given the job of minding the clock. They could stop the flow while the laws or evidence were read out. //
A pause button, made of water. // [J]
Dave's presentation ends when the bowl does. // [J]
"So by Roman times, they must have had it all worked out. Noon, to the minute." (@dave) //

# chapter: The wrong clock
That's what Dave thinks. Here's what the Roman author Pliny the Elder says. //
In the earliest Roman laws, the only times mentioned are sunrise and sunset. Later, noon was added. //
And noon was announced by a man. A summoner of the consuls watched from the senate house. / When he caught sight of the sun between two landmarks, he shouted it out. //
Pliny adds that this only worked in clear weather. If it was cloudy, there was no noon. // [J]
One writer credits the very first Roman sundial to a man named Papirius Cursor. He doesn't say who made it, or where it came from. //
A first clock with no maker and no origin. Dave is not reassured. // [J]
Pliny's own account has a better paper trail. A public sundial was set up on a column in the first Punic war. /
It had been captured from the Sicilian city of Catania, and its lines did not exactly agree with the hours. //
A footnote in the standard English translation points out that Catania is about four degrees south of Rome. //
It still ran Roman time for ninety-nine years, until a censor, Quintus Marcius Philippus, put a more carefully made dial beside it. Ninety-nine years of meetings. // [J]
But on cloudy days even the better dial was useless. Around a hundred and fifty-nine BCE, a man named Scipio Nasica built a water clock. /
It was the first to divide the hours of the day and night into equal parts. //
Pliny's words: for so long had the Romans remained without any exact division of the day. //
Rome wasn't built in a day. Nobody could say exactly how long a day was. //
One caution. Pliny is reporting what an earlier writer, Varro, said. So treat the details as Roman tradition, not a lab result. //

# chapter: A view, not a number
So how did ancient humans know what day it was? /
They counted the Moon, watched where the Sun rose, and waited for the right shadow. A day meant a place in a cycle, not a number. /
For the hour, they had shadows, pots of water, and the occasional man shouting. //
And noon wasn't a number either. It was a view. A man looked at the sun between two landmarks and shouted. //
Dave takes two rocks and sets them a few metres apart, and stands behind them, looking at the sky. /
When the sun sits exactly in the gap, he shouts. //
(slow) Nobody else arrives for another hour. Nobody minds, and nobody was ever going to know exactly when. //
(slow) Noon was never a number. It was a view.
