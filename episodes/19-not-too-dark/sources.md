# Episode 19 "You Are Not Too Dark": sources (verified 2026-10-15)

| Script line | Claim | Source |
|---|---|---|
| "Dave took four hundred group photos. He's in none of them." / "In the old ones, his face is a shadow." / "Dave has already confessed." | Dave's story (the test dummy). Not a study result. | Story device |
| "In two thousand, Nina Jablonski compared skin colour with satellite maps of ultraviolet." | Jablonski and George Chaplin (2000) used remotely sensed (satellite) UV data to test skin colour against UV levels: "skin reflectance is strongly correlated with absolute latitude and UV radiation levels"; "predicted skin reflectances deviated little from observed values". Chaplin is co-author; the narration names the lead author only, as ep16 did with Roediger. | Jablonski N.G., Chaplin G., "The evolution of human skin coloration", *Journal of Human Evolution* 2000;39(1):57-106, doi 10.1006/jhev.2000.0403. Abstract: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10896812/ |
| "Strong sun, darker skin. Weak sun, lighter skin." | "Human skin pigmentation is the product of two clines produced by natural selection to adjust levels of constitutive pigmentation to levels of UV radiation." High UVR near the equator led to dark, eumelanin-rich pigmentation; low UVB led to depigmented skin. | Jablonski N.G., Chaplin G., "Human skin pigmentation as an adaptation to UV radiation", *PNAS* 2010;107(Suppl 2):8962-8968, doi 10.1073/pnas.0914628107: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20445093/ |
| "Dark skin guards folate, which growing babies need." | 2000 abstract: "highly melanized skin protected against UV-induced photolysis of folate … a metabolite essential for normal development of the embryonic neural tube". 2010: in the tropics "the primary selective pressure was to protect folate by maintaining dark pigmentation". This is the authors' adaptive explanation (a well-supported hypothesis), stated as their finding, with no health advice. | Both papers above |
| "Light skin lets weak sun make vitamin D." | 2000: "As hominids migrated outside of the tropics, varying degrees of depigmentation evolved in order to permit UVB-induced synthesis of previtamin D3." 2010: depigmented skin evolved where UVB is low, "to sustain cutaneous photosynthesis of vitamin D3". | Both papers above |
| "Every shade is a setting for a different sun." | The narrator's summary of the two clines: the colour gradient "represents a compromise solution to the conflicting physiological requirements of photoprotection and vitamin D synthesis" (2000). Skin colour "is adaptive and labile" and changed more than once in human evolution. No shade is ranked above another. | Both papers above |
| "In the fifties, labs tuned colour prints to one photo: a white woman called Shirley." | Skin-colour balance in still-photo printing used "a norm reference card showing a 'Caucasian' woman wearing a colourful, high-contrast dress"; the models were "named 'Shirley' … after the name of the first colour test-strip-card model". "Shirley's skin became an industry standard in North American photo labs … in the 1940s and 1950s." | Roth L., "Looking at Shirley, the Ultimate Norm: Colour Balance, Image Technologies, and Cognitive Equity", *Canadian Journal of Communication* 2009;34(1):111-136, doi 10.22230/cjc.2009v34n1a2196. Full text (archived): http://web.archive.org/web/20210329024758/https://www.cjc-online.ca/index.php/journal/article/download/2196/2055 · Summary: Sarah Lewis, "The Racial Bias Built Into Photography", *New York Times* Lens, 25 April 2019: https://www.nytimes.com/2019/04/25/lens/sarah-lewis-racial-bias-photography.html |
| "If Shirley looked right, the print was right. Darker faces lost detail." | Colour film chemistry "was designed originally with a positive bias toward 'Caucasian' skin tones". In 1950s group school photos, "the picture results showed details on the White children's faces, but erased the contours and particularities of the faces of children with darker skin". NYT: "Quality control meant ensuring that Shirley's face looked good." | Roth 2009, pp. 118-119; Lewis 2019 |
| "Parents complained. Nothing changed." | "Parents complained about this situation and demanded a wider continuum of darker skin tones." A photo from the early 1970s shows "even years after complaints … the issue remained uncorrected." | Roth 2009, p. 119 |
| "Then chocolate makers complained: milk and dark looked alike." | Earl Kage, former manager of Kodak Research Studios: chocolate manufacturers found "the subtle variations between the dark and bittersweet and milk chocolates weren't as discernible", so "some modifications were tried". Furniture makers made the same complaint about wood grains (mid-1960s-1970s). | Roth 2009, pp. 119-120 (Kage interview, 21 Aug 1995; Kathy Connor, 16 Aug 1995) |
| "So Kodak improved the film, then ate the test subjects." | Kage: "my little department became quite fat with chocolate, because what was in the front of the camera was consumed at the end of the shoot." | Roth 2009, p. 120 |
| "Photos of dark skin got better by accident." | Roth: "the improvement of dark skin colour reproduction came about quite incidentally in this context". Kage: "it was never Black flesh that was addressed as a serious problem that I knew of at the time". | Roth 2009, p. 120; Lewis 2019 |
| "Dave taps his face on screen. The camera sets itself for Dave." | The one safe action. Apple iPhone User Guide: the camera "automatically sets the focus and exposure"; "Tap the screen to show the automatic focus area and exposure setting", then "Tap where you want to move the focus area." Most phone cameras work the same way. No promise about how the photo will look. | Apple, iPhone User Guide, "Use iPhone camera settings": https://support.apple.com/en-asia/guide/iphone/use-camera-settings-iphb362b394e/12.0/ios |
| "Cameras were set for Shirley. Set yours for you." | Narrator's summary of the Roth history plus the tap action. Says "were", not "are": modern phones have improved (see below). | As above |

## Checked and NOT used
- **Google Real Tone (Pixel 6, 19 Oct 2021):** Google retuned auto-white-balance and auto-exposure with photographers of colour so the camera "shows you as you are — not unnaturally darker or brighter", and says "historically racial bias in camera technology has overlooked and excluded people of color". Verified (https://blog.google/products/pixel/image-equity-real-tone-pixel-6-photos/) but cut for length; it is also the reason the ending says cameras *were* set for Shirley.
- **Kodak Gold Max "dark horse in low light":** Richard Wien of Kodak described the film this way; Roth reads it as a coded message. Cut: an interpretation, and "horse" next to Dave reads badly.
- **Kodak's multiracial Shirley card (1995):** verified (Roth 2009; Wikipedia "Color chart"), cut for length.
- **"Everyone has the same number of melanocytes":** popular claim; sources disagree (histology teaching pages say yes, others say no), so not used. Alaluf et al. 2002 (*Pigment Cell Res* 15:112-118) shows the darkest skin types have about twice the epidermal melanin and larger melanosomes; not needed for the story.
- **Roth's description of erased faces "except for the whites of their eyes and teeth":** true to the source, deliberately not used in words or pictures: it echoes a racist caricature.
- **Episode overlap:** ep14 ("not ugly") used selfie distance, mirror image, within-person photo variability and (cut) the spotlight effect. None of those are used here.
