# chapter: A Horse and Sixty Rats
In nineteen oh four, a German horse named Clever Hans could add, subtract, multiply and divide by tapping his hoof. / His owner, a math teacher, made no money from him, and seemed honest. /
Then a psychologist named Pfungst noticed that Hans only got answers right if the questioner knew them, and could be seen. / When people asked Hans a question, they leaned forward slightly to watch the hoof. That was the signal to start tapping. /
As the taps approached the right number, they gave a tiny nod. That was the signal to stop. The questioners were telling Hans the answer without knowing it. // [J]
Rosenthal's book calls it the questioner "fulfilling his own prophecy." /
Later, Pavlov thought his mice were inheriting learning ability, until a check found the research assistant had simply become a better teacher, probably because he expected it. //
In nineteen sixty-three, the psychologist Robert Rosenthal ran an experiment with twelve student experimenters and sixty perfectly ordinary laboratory rats. / Half the students were told their rats were "maze-bright." The other half were told their rats were "maze-dull." //
Each rat got ten chances a day, for five days, to learn that the darker arm of a little maze led to food. //
The "bright" rats improved every day. The "dull" rats improved until the third day, and then got worse. // [J]
On some trials, a rat refused to leave the starting spot at all. / This happened eleven percent of the time to the "bright" rats, and twenty-nine percent of the time to the "dull" ones. //
The experimenters who expected bright rats described them as more pleasant, and said they handled them more, and more gently. // [J]
Rosenthal wrote that if rats became brighter when expected to, "it should not be farfetched" to think children could become brighter when their teachers expected it. /
Soon afterwards, a letter arrived from a school principal in South San Francisco named Lenore Jacobson: /
"If you ever 'graduate' to classroom children, please let me know whether I can be of assistance." //

# chapter: The Harvard Test of Inflected Acquisition
In the spring of nineteen sixty-four, every child in Jacobson's school sat a test. Teachers were told it was called the "Harvard Test of Inflected Acquisition." // [J]
It was a standard IQ test. The name was invented, and it sounded impressive. It was meant to. // [J]
Teachers got a letter. It said children show "hills, plateaus, and valleys" in their progress, / and that the top twenty percent of scorers would show "a more significant inflection or spurt" within a year. //
In truth, those names were picked at random. Sixty-five children out of three hundred and twenty. Dave's hat would have done the job. // [J]
The researchers made one ethical choice. They would only raise expectations, never lower them. No teacher was told that a child would do worse. //
Eight months later, they retested everyone. The chosen children had gained over twelve IQ points. The others had gained over eight. //
In the first grade, the gap was dramatic. The chosen children gained about twenty-seven points, and their classmates about twelve. /
Across the first two grades, nearly half of the nineteen chosen children gained twenty points or more. Among the rest, about one in five did. / The older the children, the smaller the gap. //
The teachers also described the chosen children as happier, more curious, and more likely to succeed. Nobody had told them anything about happiness. // [J]
Rosenthal later proposed four things that might be happening: a warmer climate, more teaching, more chances to answer, and better feedback. / Dave, it turns out, was doing all four before lunch. // [J]
"I'm not playing favorites. I'm encouraging the ones who are about to bloom." (@dave) // [J]

# chapter: The Clock Strikes Thirteen
In nineteen sixty-eight, the book came out, called Pygmalion in the Classroom, after the sculptor in Greek myth whose love for his statue inspired the gods to bring her to life. /
It reached the front page of the New York Times, and Rosenthal appeared on the Today show. //
Then came the testing expert Robert Thorndike. / He said the first IQ test had given faulty data, including starting scores that were impossibly low. /
In one ordinary class, the average reasoning score fell in the mentally disabled range. / "Pygmalion," he wrote, "is so defective technically that one can only regret that it ever got beyond the eyes of the original investigators." //
He added a line he borrowed from a colleague. "When the clock strikes thirteen, doubt is cast not only on the last stroke but also all that have gone before." // [J]
Rosenthal replied that even if the first test was faulty, the rise measured with the same test still counted. //
"I already bought the gold stars," (@dave) / Dave says, looking at his list. // [J]
Rosenthal teamed up with a statistician, Donald Rubin. In nineteen seventy-eight, they pooled three hundred and forty-five experiments on expectations. "The reality of the phenomenon is beyond doubt," they wrote. //
Experiments appeared in army camps, factories and clinics. / In Israel, instructors were told that some trainees, picked at random, had unusual leadership potential, and those trainees scored higher on four objective tests. //
So that settles it, right? Expect more, get more. // 

# chapter: Eighteen Experiments
Well, not quite. In nineteen eighty-four, Stephen Raudenbush at Harvard pooled eighteen experiments that tried to repeat the effect on IQ. //
The results depended on one thing. / The better teachers already knew their pupils when they were handed the false information, the smaller the effect. /
It was larger in grades one and two, and, surprisingly, it reappeared at grade seven. //
Raudenbush called the weak spot the Achilles' heel of Pygmalion experiments: persuading teachers to believe a lie about children they've been teaching for weeks. // [J]
In two thousand five, Lee Jussim and Kent Harber reviewed thirty-five years of research. / Self-fulfilling prophecies in classrooms do occur, they concluded, but are typically small, and do not accumulate greatly. They may be more likely to fade than to build up. //
Stronger effects may appear for students from stigmatized groups. And whether any of it changes actual intelligence remains unclear. //
Their last conclusion stings the most. Teacher expectations may predict how students do mostly because the expectations are accurate, not because they are self-fulfilling. //
In other words, most teachers who expect a child to do well are right. Which makes Dave's hat the one case where they couldn't be. // [J]

# chapter: The Habit, Not the Label
So is the Pygmalion effect a myth? / Not quite. It's a small one, and a different one. //
Between twenty eleven and twenty thirteen, the educational psychologist Christine Rubie-Davies ran an experiment in Auckland, New Zealand. Teachers were randomly assigned. //
Half were taught the habits of high-expectation teachers: flexible groups instead of fixed ones, a warm classroom, clear goals, and honest feedback. They even watched videos of themselves teaching. //
Their students gained twenty-eight percent more learning in mathematics in one year, compared with students of the control teachers. //
It wasn't a label on five children. It was a teacher's habit, aimed at everyone. //
Earlier researchers had counted the habits, and one found seventeen. Teachers with high hopes asked a child harder questions, in public. Teachers with low hopes asked simple closed questions, in private. //
The children could tell which was which. //
