# chapter: The router
This is Dave. Dave has unplugged his router and feels free. His router is not the internet. //
Tonight, the whole internet goes away. Everywhere. For one week. // [J]
"Good. I wasn't using it for anything important." (@dave) //
So what would happen if the internet disappeared for a week? / Would the world carry on without it, or would everything quietly stop? //
Here is the plan. Each day, Dave will lose something, and we'll look at a real case where it already happened. / By the end, we will meet the one realistic way this could really happen. //
Dave sticks a checklist on his fridge. It is titled: things that still work. The first line reads: everything. // [J]
"This just in. The internet has disappeared. We can't tell you more, because that is where we would look." (@news) //

# chapter: The wet wire
First, where does the internet live? Not in the cloud. On the sea floor. //
The figure that gets repeated is that ninety-nine percent of intercontinental data travels in undersea cables. It is quoted everywhere, usually with no source. / A mapping company checked it. It is true, with a footnote about satellites. // [J]
The cloud is mostly a very long wet wire. //
That means it can be cut. In January twenty twenty-two, a volcanic eruption and tsunami severed the main cable to Tonga. / The country's internet came back more than five weeks later, with a satellite service helping some people in the meantime. //
"Fifty-six miles of cable, replaced. And nobody had enough spare, so we borrowed some." (@old) //
Nobody keeps a spare ocean. Tonga was one island, and Dave's week is the whole planet. // [J]

# chapter: Hour one, money
Hour one. Dave goes to buy a loaf of bread. / A real case: in June twenty eighteen, one data centre in Europe had a partial hardware failure, and five million card payments failed. //
One switch. One building. Five million payments. // [J]
Sweden posts every household a booklet that says: be ready to survive for a week without help. The list includes food, water, heating and money. / The booklet arrives by post, which does not need the internet. // [J]
Dave has two coins. He has not read the booklet. // [J]
He crosses out the first item on the checklist. Cards. //

# chapter: Day two, the economy
Day two. In January twenty eleven, Egypt switched off internet and mobile services for five days. / The OECD estimated the loss at about eighteen million dollars a day. //
It warned that the long-term impact could be greater, because the shutdown would make it much more difficult to attract foreign companies in future. //
So switching off the internet turns out to be a very bad advertisement for a country. // [J]
Dave looks in his wallet. Eighteen million dollars a day is not in there. //
He crosses out the second item. The bank. //

# chapter: Day three, the hospital
Day three. A real case: in May twenty seventeen, a malicious program swept through the English health service. / It wasn't an outage, but it shows what happens when hospital computers go dark. //
The National Audit Office counted at least eighty-one of two hundred and thirty-six hospital trusts affected. / Thirty-seven were locked out of their devices. / Another six hundred and three organisations were infected, including five hundred and ninety-five family doctors' practices. //
About sixty-nine hundred appointments were recorded as cancelled, and the real number was probably over nineteen thousand. Five emergency departments had to divert patients. //
Nobody paid the ransom. The audit concluded that the attack could have been stopped with basic security. //
Dave crosses out the third item. The hospital. He decides to be very careful on the stairs. // [J]

# chapter: Day four, the sky
But one item on the list survives. GPS. //
The satellites in the sky do not need the internet. They broadcast their signal, and receivers decode it. //
A lot of the world borrows that clock. Major financial institutions use GPS to set the clocks that time-stamp transactions. / Mobile networks use it to keep their base stations in step. Power plants and substations use it too. So a bank's idea of the time arrives from space. // [J]
Dave's phone can still tell him exactly where he is. It just can't tell him what is nearby. // [J]
Dave leaves the item on the list, and eats the last of the flour. //

# chapter: Day five, the ships
Day five. In twenty seventeen, a shipping giant with seventy-six ports and about nine hundred ships had its network wiped by a malicious program, / and about twelve hundred business applications went dark. //
To rebuild, it reinstalled four thousand servers, forty-five thousand computers and twenty-five hundred applications. / The chairman called it a heroic effort over ten days, and said volumes fell by only about twenty percent. //
What saved the company was a blackout. A power cut in Lagos had knocked the local office offline, so it held the one clean copy of the network's directory. // [J]
Someone had to physically carry it to where the network was rebuilt. The cost was hundreds of millions of dollars. //
Dave's parcel is somewhere in a port. The port doesn't know either. // [J]
He crosses out the fourth item. Ships. //

# chapter: How it could really happen
So how could the whole internet really go away? Realistically, there is one way. The Sun. //
In September eighteen fifty-nine, a giant storm from the Sun hit Earth. Telegraph lines across North America failed. / In Pittsburgh, a manager reported streams of fire from the circuits. In Washington, an operator was badly shocked, and paper caught fire. //
In Boston, operators unplugged their batteries and still sent messages to Portland, Maine. They used only the current from the northern lights. The sky was the power supply. // [J]
The lights were seen as far south as Cuba. //
In July twenty twelve, a storm just as strong erupted from the Sun and missed Earth. / The space physicist Daniel Baker said it was at least as strong as the one in eighteen fifty-nine. The only difference, he said, is that it missed. // [J]
A study by the National Academy of Sciences put the total cost of such a storm at more than two trillion dollars. / And power would be the bigger problem: NASA notes that multi-ton transformers damaged by a storm like that might take years to repair. //
Scientists estimate the chance of one hitting Earth is between one point six and twelve percent per decade. That is a single estimate, not a forecast. //

# chapter: The twist
Here is the twist. In twenty twenty-one, the computer scientist Sangeetha Abdu Jyothi asked what such a storm would do to the internet. //
The fibre itself is immune. But long cables have repeaters every fifty to a hundred and fifty kilometres to boost the signal, powered along a wire. / Those repeaters are vulnerable to induced currents. //
GPS and communication satellites are directly exposed to a storm too, and could lose connectivity during it. //
Her paper warns of outages that could cover the entire globe and last several months. / Submarine cables are at greater risk than land cables, and the United States is at higher risk of disconnection than Asia. //
So a week is the gentle version. Tonga needed five weeks to fix one cable. / Dave crosses out the fifth item on his list. The sea cables. //
There is one more catch. The Sun was quiet for about three decades, exactly while the internet was being built. So nobody knows how it would hold up. //
"So the week I picked was the gentle one?" (@dave) //
It was, Dave. Very gentle, compared with what the Sun could do. //
Engineers who build the internet had mostly planned for broken cables, not for the Sun. The paper says the networking community has largely overlooked the risk. // [J]
That sounds grim. But the point of the paper is to plan ahead, and it lays out steps to make the internet more resilient. //
Day seven. At midnight, in Dave's version, the internet comes back. His phone finds one bar, then four thousand notifications. //
"This just in. We are told the internet has returned. We can't confirm, because we'd have to look it up." (@news) // [J]

# chapter: Next door
Dave looks at the fridge. Cards, the bank, the hospital, the ships, the sea cables. All crossed out. / Then he takes the pencil and writes one last item at the bottom of the list. Next door. //
He plugs the router back in, and turns the phone face down anyway. A router, as we said, was never the internet. //
He knocks. The neighbour opens the door, holding a very old paper map. //
"Hello. I'm Dave. I live next door." (@dave)
