# chapter: The woman with no music
This is Dave. It is July, fifteen eighteen, and Dave has just arrived in Strasbourg, where a woman is dancing alone in the street.
There is no music. There is no party. Nobody invited her. //
She isn't celebrating. She will keep going for four, maybe six days. And before the summer is over, one chronicle says four hundred people will be dancing too. //
So why did hundreds of people dance until they collapsed? And why did the city's cure make it worse? //
Dave has a plan: find out what is going on, and keep his feet absolutely still. // [J]
Before this is over, the city will give its dancers a stage, a band, and red shoes. And one famous number will turn out to be very hard to find. //
Let's start with the first dancer, and a cure that sounded sensible at the time. //

# chapter: The cure
The date was the fourteenth of July. Four of the six chronicles agree she was first, and call her Troffea. It is about the most they agree on. // [J]
Her husband reportedly begged her to stop. She stopped only when she collapsed, and reportedly started again the next day on swollen feet.
Within a week, more than thirty others had joined her. So the city council did what worried councils do. It asked the doctors.
According to the historian John Waller, the physicians ruled out the stars and the supernatural. They decided it was a natural disease, caused by overheated blood.
That idea came from Galen, a Greek doctor who taught that overheated blood could cause madness.
Galen had been dead for about thirteen centuries, and his reputation hadn't suffered at all. // [J]
For overheated blood, the usual treatments were bleeding, or a cooler diet. This time, the doctors prescribed something else: more dancing.
They believed the sufferers would only recover if they danced it out, day and night.
"Excuse me. But isn't that the opposite of a cure?" (@dave)
Not in fifteen eighteen. Overheated blood was the best medical idea around, and the council was following it. They weren't careless. They were working from the wrong map.
So the council cleared two guildhalls and the grain market, built a wooden stage opposite the horse fair, and paid musicians to keep playing.
Strong men were hired to hold up anyone who began to fall.
They even paid professional dancers to keep the sufferers moving. Dave checks the job listing. He does not apply. // [J]
A stage, a band, two guildhalls, and strong men on standby. As medical equipment, it was very well catered. // [J]
And the sick didn't recover. Instead, the number grew. The chronicles disagree about how fast, so Dave starts a tally on the guildhall wall.
It says four hundred dancers, because that's the number he keeps hearing. It also says fifteen deaths a day, because that's the other number. // [J]

# chapter: Red shoes and a saint
The council did not rely on the stage alone. It also sent the worst cases by wagon, about thirty miles, to a shrine of Saint Vitus near Saverne.
There, priests laid red shoes on the dancers' feet, sprinkled them with holy water, painted them with consecrated oil, and put small crosses in their hands.
Dave looks down at his own trainers. They are white. He has never been so grateful for white. // [J]
On the second of August, the council banned public dancing, and music too. The stage and the band had been tried. Now the band was taken away. // [J]
But why Saint Vitus? In that part of Europe, people believed he punished sinners by forcing them to dance, and that prayers at his shrine could set them free.
So the dancers weren't only exhausted. They may have been terrified that they were cursed.
And it wasn't new. In June thirteen seventy-four, a similar outbreak began in Aachen, and spread to Cologne and Flanders.
A physician named Paracelsus later called the condition choreomania, which is Greek for dancing madness. Almost five hundred years on, that is still a fair summary. // [J]
That brings us to the suspects. //

# chapter: Suspect one: the bread
Suspect one is rye bread. In the nineteen fifties, a researcher named Eugene Backman suggested the dancers had eaten ergot, a fungus that grows on rye.
Ergot contains chemicals related to LSD, so a city of dancers on LSD makes a great story. Unfortunately, it makes a better story than it makes a theory. // [J]
In the Middle Ages, ergot poisoning was called Saint Anthony's fire. Anthony got the burning, Vitus got the dancing. Medieval Europe ran a very busy filing system. // [J]
But Waller argued in The Lancet that the theory does not seem tenable, since people poisoned by ergot could hardly dance for days at a time.
Ergot squeezes the blood vessels in your limbs. As a dance enhancer, it is terrible. // [J]
And similar outbreaks happened along the Rhine, in different climates, where people ate different crops. The bread can't explain all of it.
Dave checks the bread. It's just bread. He eats it anyway. // [J]

# chapter: Suspect two: the fear
Suspect two is the one most often given today. It has an unglamorous name: mass psychogenic illness.
It means that many people suddenly show the same strange behaviour, with no physical cause that anyone can find. The cause lies in stress, belief, and the people around you.
Waller thinks Strasbourg fits. The region was, in his words, riddled with starvation and disease, and a famous saint was said to curse people with dancing.
Put hunger, fear, and a belief in a curse into one city, and Waller thinks some people could slip into a trance-like state, losing track of their own bodies.
Dave tests the theory by thinking very hard about Saint Vitus. His left foot starts tapping. He decides to think about something else. // [J]
It has modern relatives. In nineteen sixty-two, in what is now Tanzania, a laughing outbreak began at a girls' boarding school. Ninety-five of the hundred and fifty-nine pupils were affected.
Nobody can scan a brain from fifteen eighteen. So this is the best-fitting explanation, not a proven one.
But surely there's one part we can prove. How many people died?
Dave looks at his tally. Four hundred dancers. Fifteen deaths a day. At that rate, hundreds of people. //

# chapter: The number that isn't there
Here is the twist. The fifteen a day comes from later chronicles, written by people who were not there.
One of them, Daniel Specklin, wasn't born until fifteen thirty-six. As eyewitnesses go, he was eighteen years late. // [J]
Élisabeth Clementz, the Strasbourg historian who collected the old sources for an exhibition in twenty eighteen, warns that readers must be very careful with chronicles.
The city's own papers from that summer, the orders and letters of the council, are a different matter. And they mention no deaths at all.
"So the most famous number in this story was never counted by anyone who was there?" (@dave)
The earliest account is in annals attributed to Sebastian Brant, the city clerk, who also wrote the bestseller Ship of Fools.
It says about fifty people were dancing day and night, which was painful to see.
Only five cases are documented one by one, and only because their families had to testify to get financial help.
Dave rubs out the fifteen. He crosses out four hundred and writes fifty, then thinks again, and writes four hundred with a question mark.
His tally now has three numbers and no confidence. // [J]
So some may have died. Later writers say many did. But the number that made this famous isn't in the records from the time. //

# chapter: What the dance was
So why did hundreds of people dance until they collapsed? //
The honest answer is that nobody can prove it. The best-supported explanation is fear and belief working on a hungry city, with a medical idea that kept the dance going.
Not magic. Not mould. Frightened people, a saint they dreaded, and a cure that fit the medicine of the day.
They weren't fools. Some danced for days. Some families asked for help. And whatever the true count was, these were real people. //
By September, the remaining dancers had been sent to the shrine, and the dancing faded.
The first dancer had no music. The council's cure brought a band. The dance went on either way. //
Dave kept his feet perfectly still for the whole week, on purpose. //
His left foot tapped all the way home.
