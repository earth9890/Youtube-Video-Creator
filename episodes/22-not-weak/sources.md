# Episode 22 "You Are Not Weak": sources (verified 2026-10-14)

| Script line | Claim | Source |
|---|---|---|
| "Dave's excuse: willpower is a battery. By ten, it's flat." | This is the "strength" or "resource depletion" model of self-control: self-control draws on a limited resource that runs down with use ("ego depletion"). Dave's nightly battery is his own illustration of that model. | Baumeister R.F., Bratslavsky E., Muraven M., Tice D.M., "Ego depletion: Is the active self a limited resource?", *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 1998;74(5):1252-1265, doi 10.1037/0022-3514.74.5.1252. PDF: https://faculty.washington.edu/jdb/345/345%20Articles/Baumeister%20et%20al.%20(1998).pdf |
| "In nineteen ninety-eight, Roy Baumeister made hungry students eat radishes next to fresh cookies." | Experiment 1: 67 undergraduates who had skipped a meal. Chocolate chip cookies were baked in the lab room, so the room smelled of them, and a plate of cookies and chocolates sat beside a bowl of radishes. The radish group had to eat only radishes. All of them resisted, though some "looked longingly" at the chocolates and a few picked up cookies to sniff them. | Same paper, Experiment 1, Method and Results. APA summary: https://www.apa.org/topics/willpower-limited.pdf |
| "Then, an impossible puzzle. Cookie eaters lasted nineteen minutes. Radish eaters quit after eight." | Then came unsolvable tracing puzzles. Mean persistence (Table 1): radish 8.35 min, chocolate 18.90 min, no-food control 20.86 min. | Same paper, Table 1 |
| "But in twenty sixteen, twenty-three labs re-tested the drain on two thousand people. It barely showed." | A Registered Replication Report: 23 labs, total N = 2,141, ran a standardised depletion protocol (from Sripada et al. 2014, not the radish task). Pooled effect d = 0.04, 95 % CI [−0.07, 0.15], which includes zero. | Hagger M.S., Chatzisarantis N.L.D., et al., "A Multilab Preregistered Replication of the Ego-Depletion Effect", *Perspectives on Psychological Science* 2016;11(4):546-573, doi 10.1177/1745691616652873: https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1745691616652873 · Neuroskeptic, Discover: https://discovermagazine.com/mind/the-end-of-ego-depletion-theory |
| "Baumeister: wrong test." | Baumeister and Vohs replied that the borrowed computer task may not produce depletion. They called the project a "misguided effort with elusive implications" and promised their own multisite test using well-tested procedures. | Baumeister R.F., Vohs K.D., *Perspectives on Psychological Science* 2016;11(4):574-575, quoted in the Discover article above |
| "His own co-author ran thirty-six more labs. Same." | Kathleen Vohs, Baumeister's frequent co-author, led a preregistered multisite test: 36 labs, N = 3,531, two procedures chosen by the depletion researchers. The confirmatory result was not significant (d = 0.06), and the Bayesian analysis favoured the null about 4 to 1. An exploratory analysis without exclusions found d = 0.08. "Same" means the effect again barely showed. | Vohs K.D., Schmeichel B.J., et al., "A Multisite Preregistered Paradigmatic Test of the Ego-Depletion Effect", *Psychological Science* 2021;32(10):1566-1581, doi 10.1177/0956797621989733: https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0956797621989733 |
| "In twenty twelve, Wilhelm Hofmann pinged two hundred adults all week: what do you want, right now?" | An experience-sampling study: 205 adults in a German city (Würzburg) were signalled on handheld devices 7 times a day for 7 days, and gave 7,827 reports of desire episodes. "What do you want right now" paraphrases the prompt, which asked whether they were feeling a desire at that moment or had felt one recently. | Hofmann W., Baumeister R.F., Förster G., Vohs K.D., "Everyday temptations: An experience sampling study of desire, conflict, and self-control", *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 2012;102(6):1318-1335, doi 10.1037/a0026545 (abstract: https://doi.org/10.1037/a0026545) · BPS Research Digest: https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/already-struggling-keep-new-year-resolutions-heres-first-detailed-study-daily · Science News: https://www.sciencenews.org/article/fighting-willpowers-catch-22 |
| "The most self-controlled wanted things just as often. But they resisted less." | BPS Research Digest on the paper: "people who scored highly on a measure of trait self-control had just as many desires, but they were less likely to report experiencing internal conflict; their desires were generally weaker; and they attempted to resist them less often." Ent et al. (2015) restate it: "People with high self-control reported resisting impulses less frequently than others." | BPS link above · Ent et al. 2015, Introduction (below) |
| "Psychologist Michael Ent let people choose where to work: a boring spot, or a fun one. The strong-willed picked boring." | Abstract: "People high in trait self-control were more likely than those low in trait self-control to choose to work in a distraction-free environment rather than in a distracting, yet appealing, one (Studies 2 and 3)." Study 1: they also reported more temptation-avoiding habits. "Picked boring" simplifies "were more likely to choose"; it is not "every one of them". | Ent M.R., Baumeister R.F., Tice D.M., "Trait self-control and the avoidance of temptation", *Personality and Individual Differences* 2015;74:12-15, doi 10.1016/j.paid.2014.09.031: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0191886914005339 |
| "They don't win the fight. They don't turn up." | The authors' conclusion: "high trait self-control is linked to avoiding, rather than merely resisting, temptation". Hofmann (BPS): "they seem to avoid putting themselves in situations in which they are exposed to problematic temptations." | Both papers above |
| "Tonight, move the tin, or the phone, to another room." | The one safe action is situation change (avoidance), as above. There is direct experimental support for distance: in two Utrecht studies (N = 77 and N = 54), a snack bowl placed 70 or 140 cm away instead of 20 cm cut both the chance of snacking and the amount eaten, with no rise in craving. No miracle claim and no number are promised. | Maas J., de Ridder D.T.D., de Vet E., de Wit J.B.F., "Do distant foods decrease intake? The effect of food accessibility on consumption", *Psychology & Health* 2012;27(Suppl 2):59-73, doi 10.1080/08870446.2011.565341: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21678172/ |

## Checked and NOT used
- **"Eat sugar to refill your willpower" (Gailliot et al. 2007, glucose):** in July 2016 researchers reported evidence of bias in the published glucose literature (Discover piece above). It would have been a strong "last hope", but it costs about 20 words and the script is at the 200-word limit. It is also a food claim, so we left it out.
- **Ego depletion is not "proven false":** the script says only that it "barely showed" in the two big preregistered tests. Baumeister still disputes the 2016 test, and the script says so ("wrong test"). The 2021 exploratory d = 0.08 is small but not zero.
- **Wansink's candy-dish studies** (desk vs 2 m away): not used because much of Brian Wansink's food research was retracted. Maas et al. 2012 makes the same point independently.
- **Ward et al. 2017 "brain drain" (a phone's mere presence lowers capacity):** later replications failed. It was not used for the phone line, which rests only on the general avoidance finding.
- **Science News figures** (willpower failed 17 % of the time when resisted, rising later in the day) come from a 2011 conference talk on the same data. They were left out because their link to depletion is just what the replications question.
- **Overlap check:** none of ep22 "Not Too Skinny"'s facts (Hubal 2005, Pope 1999, Gilovich 2000, WHO 2020) are used.
