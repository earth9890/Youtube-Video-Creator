# chapter: Back in April
This is Dave. Dave is done with winter. He has hung a sign on his front door that says: back in April. // [J]
"I'm going to sleep through all of it, like a bear. Wake me when it's April. Not before." (@dave) //
So what would happen if humans could hibernate? / Could you really skip five months, or would your body quietly refuse? //
Here is the plan. We'll borrow tricks from a bear, a squirrel and a lemur, and meet the space agency that wants to try it on astronauts. / And Dave will find out that his sign is wrong about one very important word. //
Scientists keep the terms simple. Torpor is a deep, energy-saving chill. When it lasts for many days, it is called hibernation. //
Dave turns off the lights, climbs into bed, and waits to become a bear. //

# chapter: What a bear really does
Start with the bear. In two thousand eleven, researchers at the University of Alaska Fairbanks watched black bears hibernate in dens built for the study. //
The bears spent five to seven months without eating, drinking, urinating or defecating. // [J]
Their metabolism dropped to about twenty-five percent of normal. / A seventy-five percent discount on being alive. // [J]
Here is the strange part. Their core temperature fell by only five or six degrees. Normally, chemistry slows by about half for every ten degrees of cooling. / So the bears did far more than the cold alone could explain. //
Their heart rate slowed from about fifty-five beats a minute to about fourteen. / Their body temperature drifted between thirty and thirty-six degrees, in slow cycles of two to seven days. //
"Dave, a bear is not napping. It is running almost everything at a quarter speed, on purpose." (@doc) //
Dave adds a second line to his sign: bladder not available. // [J]

# chapter: The big dinner
But a bear's trick starts in autumn. The arctic ground squirrel often doubles its body weight before it goes under. Fat is the fuel for the whole winter. //
Then it spends seven to eight months in hibernation. Its out-of-office reply lasts most of the year. // [J]
Dave looks at his cupboards with a plan in his eyes. / To be clear, this is a thought experiment. Please don't try any of this. //
The squirrel goes further than the bear. In nineteen eighty-seven, a biologist at the University of Alaska Fairbanks, Brian Barnes, fitted twelve arctic ground squirrels with temperature transmitters. //
Their bodies cooled below freezing. Minus two point nine degrees Celsius: the lowest body temperature ever recorded in a mammal. //
"Minus three? Fine. I'll just wear a very good hat." (@dave) // [J]
A hat will not help, Dave. A Harvard researcher later pointed out that in humans, a drop of a couple of degrees leads to hypothermia and can be fatal. / The squirrel's route is closed to us. //

# chapter: The primate who does it in the heat
So look for a hibernator in your own family. In Madagascar, the fat-tailed dwarf lemur hibernates for up to seven months in the dry season. //
In two thousand four, researchers reported that these lemurs were the first primates known to hibernate. And they don't need cold at all. //
The lemurs settle in tree holes where the air regularly rises above thirty degrees, and their bodies simply follow the temperature of the hole. //
Their tails store the fat for the whole season. The animal is named after its best feature. // [J]
In twenty thirteen, two more dwarf lemur species were found hibernating underground in cold mountain forest. The lead researcher said they look more like temperate hibernators than we thought. //
The thread running through all of them is not cold. It is scarcity. When food and water run short, a mammal can migrate, or it can turn its metabolism way down. //
Dave is a primate. The lemur is a very distant cousin who got the family's best trick. // [J]
Dave adds a third line to his sign: small tail, hot room. //

# chapter: Where is the switch?
So far, Dave has three problems: the fat, the cold and the switch. / What flips the switch? In twenty twenty, a team at Harvard Medical School found a group of neurons in a mouse's hypothalamus that can start a torpor-like state. //
Stimulating them dropped the mice into torpor, and kept them there for days. Silencing them stopped fasting mice from entering torpor, but changed nothing in well-fed mice. //
One of the team, Sinisa Hrvatin, said the first step is studying the basic biology of torpor in animals, and that this is not science fiction. / His colleague Michael Greenberg said it was far too soon to say whether this could be done in a human. //
"Is there a button? Please tell me there is a button somewhere on me." (@dave) //
There is one last hope. Maybe we already did it, long ago. / In twenty twenty, two researchers argued that four-hundred-thousand-year-old bones from a cave in Spain carried winter damage like that of hibernating mammals. //
Even the authors thought it would have been unhealthy, because those people could not have built bear-sized fat stores. A hibernation plan with no pantry. // [J]
Critics pointed to other explanations, and said large mammals cannot cool enough to truly hibernate. They enter a lighter state called torpor. //
"Dave, nobody has found the switch in a person. Please stop pressing your own forehead." (@doc) // [J]
Dave adds a fourth line to his sign: switch not found. // [J]

# chapter: Mars
But one organisation is very interested in the switch. In twenty nineteen, engineers at the European Space Agency designed a spacecraft where the crew hibernates on the way to Mars. //
Their plan: a hundred-and-eighty-day cruise, with every astronaut's metabolism cut by seventy-five percent, using a drug to start a state called torpor. //
Sleeping crews don't need living space. Taking out the crew quarters made the spacecraft about a third lighter. // [J]
The agency's own research team calls metabolic reduction a game changer, for space and for medicine. / The reference mission was six people on a five-year round trip. Food and water needs could fall by up to seventy-five percent, which is the point of the whole idea. //
Like hibernating animals, the crew would build body fat beforehand, and need about twenty-one days to recover after waking. Based on animal hibernation, the planners do not expect them to lose bone or muscle. //
With the whole crew asleep, the ship would have to run itself, using artificial intelligence to manage faults. / A sign on the door is not fault management. // [J]
The engineers sketched a roadmap to validate it within twenty years, starting with animals and then moving to people. / Twenty years. Dave's sign says April. // [J]
Dave adds a fifth line to his sign: sleeping on the way to Mars. //

# chapter: The twist
So, what would happen if humans could hibernate? You would eat for months, then run at a quarter speed for several more. / And the first people to use it would probably be astronauts. //
Now, the one word Dave's sign gets wrong. It says April. Hibernation is not one long sleep. //
Every two to three weeks, a hibernating arctic ground squirrel, still asleep, shivers and shakes for twelve to fifteen hours, until its body is back near thirty-seven degrees. Then it cools down again. //
If Dave were a squirrel, he would spend half of week three shivering at the ceiling. The body sets the alarm, and it has no snooze button. // [J]
That warm-up is expensive. In one lab study at two degrees above freezing, the warm-ups made up eighty-six percent of the energy cost of the whole winter. / In a much colder burrow, the share fell to twenty-three percent. // [J]
So why wake at all? One idea is that the animals wake up to sleep. In nineteen ninety-one, researchers found that ground squirrels sleep during these warm-ups, / and that the need for sleep seemed to build in deep torpor. //
Later studies found mixed results. A twenty sixteen paper on dwarf lemurs put it plainly: it remains unclear. //
One more clue comes from the lemur. In hot tree holes, where its body passively warms above thirty degrees, it seems to get sleep-like states without waking. //
"So I'd wake up, in the middle of my sleep, in order to sleep?" (@dave) // [J]
Possibly, Dave. So hibernation is not skipping sleep. It may be a very long winter with a nap break, and a bill for every wake-up. //
