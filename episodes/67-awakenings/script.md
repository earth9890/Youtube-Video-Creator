# chapter: A woman who feels twenty-one
A woman sits up in a hospital bed in the Bronx and is asked what year it is.
She says she knows it is nineteen sixty-nine, and that she is sixty-four. But she feels twenty-one, and she feels it is nineteen twenty-six. //
This is Dave. Dave has come to visit, and he has brought a calendar. It is the only thing he could think of. // [J]
So how does a person lose forty-three years, and what happens when she gets them back? And why didn't the waking last? //
The story starts with a sickness, a drug, and a doctor who was not sure it would work. //

# chapter: The sleepy sickness
In nineteen seventeen, the neurologist Constantin von Economo described a strange new illness. He named it encephalitis lethargica, which means an inflamed, sleepy brain. Sleepy undersells it.
Doctors have never been known for poetry. // [J]
It spread around the world from about nineteen sixteen, and peaked in nineteen twenty and nineteen twenty-four.
Researchers estimate that more than a million people were affected, and about a third of them died in the acute phase.
Many who survived slowly became still. Years later, they developed a kind of parkinsonism, and some could barely move or speak. The doctor Oliver Sacks later described them as frozen.
Their minds were often there. Sacks said that very rarely, a sneeze or the sound of a fire engine could release a patient for a few moments.
One patient, using a spelling board, wrote that the body was a prison with windows but no doors. //
Where did the illness come from? After a hundred years of research, the cause is still unknown. The illness ended faster than the investigation. // [J]
By the nineteen sixties, many survivors had spent decades in chronic hospitals.
In one of them, in the Bronx, a nurse said of one patient that she had not changed in thirty years.
Sacks wondered if experience itself might have been arrested along with the body. On some of them, the years had hardly shown. //

# chapter: A pill and a hunch
Then came a drug. In nineteen sixty-seven, the doctor George Cotzias and colleagues reported that very large oral doses of dopa could ease Parkinson's disease.
Its active form is called L-DOPA. Dopamine can't cross the blood-brain barrier, so L-DOPA gets in disguised, and the brain turns it into dopamine once it is inside. // [J]
The trick was the dose: far more than anyone had dared. In medicine, more is usually the problem. Here, for a while, it was the cure. // [J]
Sacks decided to try it on a group of his own patients. In nineteen sixty-nine, he began.
In his book, the hospital is called Mount Carmel. It was really Beth Abraham. The patients appear under single letters, so Dave has asked for one too. // [J]
This is history, not advice. L-DOPA is a prescription drug, and it needs a doctor. //

# chapter: The awakening
What happened next, Sacks later said, had the quality of a fable. He thought of Sleeping Beauty, and of Rip Van Winkle.
Some patients came out instantly. Others thawed over days, beginning with movement, then speech, then attention. Sacks said it was like seeing frozen figures thawing.
Of the first awakenings, he said they seemed like the dawn of a new day, the birth of a new life.
He compared the patients to extinct volcanoes that had erupted into life.
Volcanoes are not known for moderation. Neither was the dose. // [J]
Sacks remembered one patient stroking leaves, and another looking at the night lights of New York on the horizon. Everything was a source of delight and gratitude.
Dave stands at the window, and looks at the same lights. //
Sacks wrote about a patient he called Rose R. She was excited, full of songs, and she kept talking about Gershwin, who was very current in nineteen twenty-six. // [J]
He recalled asking her a few questions. She told him she knew the date of Pearl Harbor, and the date of Kennedy's assassination.
It was all recorded in her head, she said, but none of it seemed real.
Dave, who has never once remembered a date, feels outclassed. // [J]
Forty-three years is about fifteen thousand days. In that time the world had made talking films, television, penicillin, jet planes, and the Moon. //
Dave flips his calendar forward one page at a time, forty-three of them. He starts using both hands. // [J]
"I'm so sorry. I don't know where to begin." (@dave) //

# chapter: Too much life
It was real. It did not last.
The first problems were problems of excess, Sacks said. His patients had not had enough movement, or enough dopamine, for decades. Now things ran away.
Joyful feeling turned into mania. Normal movement turned into violent motion, frenzy, and tics. Some patients swung between trance and frenzy several times a day.
A review of the illness, published in two thousand seventeen, says the period of benefit from L-DOPA was limited. Limited, in this case, meant months. // [J]
Within several months, most of the patients began to experience adverse effects.
Rose R. had about ten days of extraordinary excitement, Sacks said. Then she returned to the state she had been in before.
Sacks recalled her saying she hated the television world, and that nothing in it made sense to her.
Dave, who has been holding the remote, switches the television off. // [J]
Sacks said he could not help feeling that her own will had had the last word. He could not give her the missing years. The drug could not either. //
Other doctors saw the same pattern.
In a nineteen seventy-two study of thirty patients with the same illness, twenty-six stayed on the drug long enough to be judged, and they improved.
But the drug also made involuntary movements worse, and behaviour changes were a major side effect. //

# chapter: What stayed
So what was left, when the waking faded? For most of the patients, Sacks said, some sort of happy medium. Not trance. Not frenzy.
It is the least dramatic phrase in the whole story. In neurology, it is a large achievement. // [J]
Sacks said that people usually left that hospital only in a box.
Now some had friends and relatives outside, and walked out of a place they had never expected to leave.
Others stayed, but found gardens, workshops, music, and a full social life.
It sounds like a very good hospital. Dave asks whether they take visitors. // [J]
Sacks's explanation was Freud's: work and love. He called them the most important non-chemical thing in making life stable. Freud's formula is older than the drug, and it lasted longer. // [J]
In nineteen eighty-two, Sacks wrote that for a significant number of patients, the awakening had proved enduring.
It is a quieter ending than the drug promised, and it is a kinder one than the frenzy. //

# chapter: The footnote
And now the twist. Sacks published the book in nineteen seventy-three. W. H. Auden called it a masterpiece, and a film followed in nineteen ninety.
But the story we have been telling comes mostly from one man's telling. In December twenty twenty-five, the New Yorker published a report built on Sacks's private journals. //
In them, Sacks wrote that he had given his patients powers, starting with powers of speech, which they did not have.
He admitted that some details in his case studies were pure fabrications.
The reporting presents evidence that some details about the patient he called Leonard L. were invented.
At the time, it adds, other neurologists mostly met the book with silence or scepticism. //
Sacks called his writing a sort of autobiography. He renamed the hospital and the patients, and, by his own account, changed some of the facts. // [J]
So what is solid? The illness, the survivors, and the drug are real. Other doctors, with their own patients, saw benefit, and then movements and behaviour changes. //
What the book added was the people. And that is the part now in dispute. //

# chapter: Staying
So what happens when a person wakes up after forty years? //
The body can wake faster than the world can be explained. A drug can move a hand, and give back a voice.
It cannot give back the years, or the people who were in them. //
Whatever the exact details, a woman sat up in a hospital in the Bronx, and said she felt it was nineteen twenty-six. //
Dave puts the calendar down on the bedside table, face down, and stays.
