# chapter: Dave's party
This is Dave. Dave is forty, and his birthday is on the eighth of September. He is planning a party in England, in the year seventeen fifty-two.
On Wednesday the second, he goes to bed. On Thursday the fourteenth, he wakes up. // [J]
Dave looks at the calendar. Eleven days have gone, and the eighth is among them. He has already sent out the invitations. Nobody can find the date. // [J]
"Excuse me. Where is my birthday?" (@dave) //
So why did Britain lose eleven days in seventeen fifty-two? And did anyone riot about it? //
The answer involves a miscounted year, a pope, and a very famous painting. Let's start with eleven minutes. //

# chapter: Eleven minutes
The Julian calendar, brought in by Julius Caesar, gave the year three hundred and sixty-five and a quarter days.
The real year, from one spring to the next, is a little shorter.
It is shorter by about eleven minutes. That does not sound like much. After sixteen hundred years, it is ten days. // [J]
By fifteen eighty-two, the spring equinox was falling on the tenth or eleventh of March, instead of the twenty-first, the date fixed at the Council of Nicaea in three twenty-five.
Easter, which the church tied to the spring equinox, was slowly slipping later in the season.
So Pope Gregory the Thirteenth fixed it, using a plan from the astronomer Luigi Lilio, refined by Christopher Clavius.
In October fifteen eighty-two, Thursday the fourth was followed by Friday the fifteenth, in the countries that followed him.
He also changed the leap years. A century year is only a leap year if it divides by four hundred.
That makes the average year three hundred and sixty-five point two four two five days. //
Britain did not follow. In fifteen eighty-five, a bill to adopt the new calendar fell after its second reading. It was about as far as most calendar reform got. // [J]
By seventeen hundred, the gap had grown to eleven days, because the new rule skipped a leap day and the old one did not.
For about a hundred and seventy years, Britain was out of step with much of Europe. It was, at least, consistently wrong. // [J]

# chapter: Astronomical jargon
In seventeen fifty-one, the Earl of Chesterfield introduced a bill in the House of Lords. He was candid about it.
He said he had to talk some astronomical jargon, of which he did not understand one word.
That is the oldest tradition in Parliament. // [J]
The Earl of Macclesfield seconded it, and supplied the technical knowledge.
He put it more elegantly: time, that great discoverer of truth and falsehood, had shown the old calendar to be very erroneous. //
The bill passed, and became the Calendar Act.
It is dated seventeen fifty, though it was passed in seventeen fifty-one, because it belongs to the parliamentary session that began in the earlier year.
Even the Calendar Act has a confusing date. // [J]
Easter of seventeen fifty-three was celebrated in Canterbury and in Rome at the same time.
They do not always manage that on other subjects. // [J]
The Act is very precise. The day after the second of September shall be called the fourteenth, omitting for that time only the eleven intermediate days. //
For that time only. It is a rare parliamentary promise that was kept. // [J]
September seventeen fifty-two had only nineteen days. The Act also came with a table for finding Easter, good until the year eighteen ninety-nine. It was Parliament's idea of long-term planning. // [J]
Before the Act, February dates often carried two years, like seventeen fifty-one, slash, fifty-two, one for each calendar. It was England's way of hedging its bets. // [J]
The Act also moved New Year from the twenty-fifth of March to the first of January.
So the year seventeen fifty-one ran for just two hundred and eighty-two days, by our arithmetic. It started late and finished on schedule. //
Russia would wait until nineteen eighteen to switch, and Greece until nineteen twenty-three. Britain was neither the first nor the last.
Parliament passed another Act in nineteen twenty-eight, to fix the date of Easter.
It was never brought into force, and attempts in nineteen forty-eight, nineteen seventy, nineteen eighty-four, and nineteen ninety-nine failed too. // [J]

# chapter: The fine print
Dave reads the Act, looking for his birthday. Instead, he finds the rent. //
The Act says rents, debts, and contracts shall remain due and payable on the same natural days as before. Interest is counted by the true number of natural days. //
Parliament had deleted eleven days, but it was very clear that the landlord was not getting any less. // [J]
It also protected ages. No one would be deemed to have reached twenty-one, or any other age, until the full number of years and days had really passed. //
The clause stopped anyone cashing in on the missing days. Parliament had thought of the teenagers before they did. // [J]
Dave works it out on the wall. He turns forty-one after three hundred and sixty-six real days. In the new calendar, that is the nineteenth of September. //
He tries it anyway. He asks a clerk to record him as forty-one on the eighth. The clerk points to the clause, and then to the door. // [J]
He has lost a birthday, and gained eleven days of waiting. Legally, it is a wash. // [J]
Dave is not satisfied. He has heard that the whole country was furious, and that there was a riot. //

# chapter: The riot
Everyone knows the story. Angry crowds filled the streets, shouting, give us our eleven days. They thought the government had stolen eleven days of their lives.
For generations, historians used it as the standard example of the stupidity of crowds.
Dave finds a piece of wood and some paint. He writes the slogan on it, and sets off to join the mob. //
"Give us our eleven days. Give us our birthday." (@dave) //
He finds an orderly street, a man selling cabbages, and no mob. // [J]
Here is the twist. The riot probably never happened. //
The slogan appears on a banner in a print by William Hogarth, called An Election Entertainment, made in seventeen fifty-five. It refers to the Oxfordshire election of seventeen fifty-four.
The calendar change really was debated in that campaign, between the Whigs and the Tories. The banner was a political slogan, not a riot. //
The other source is a speech. In eighteen twenty, the lawyer James Scarlett mocked the supposed rioters in a treason trial, sixty-eight years later.
A lawyer's joke is not a witness. // [J]
The historian Robert Poole, in nineteen ninety-five, went looking for the riot in the surviving records, and found no contemporary reports of one. The records suggest otherwise.
Dave looks at his banner. He feels robbed, though not of the eleven days. He feels robbed of the riot. // [J]

# chapter: What really happened
So what did happen? The troubles were duller, and closer to home. They were about money. //
A proportionate cut in rents was considered, and dropped, because it would prove more complex than it first appeared. Instead, the Treasury published tables. //
Poole's research suggests that some landlords and employers tried to take advantage of the confusion. The Act's rules about rent were there for a reason. //
Fairs, feasts, and festivals had to move, or did not. Some communities kept their old dates for years, and some went on celebrating Christmas on the fifth of January. //
And the Act left a mark we still pay. The British tax year ends on the fifth of April, which is the old twenty-fifth of March, pushed forward eleven days. // [J]
So the tax man got his eleven days after all. //

# chapter: The nineteenth
So why did Britain lose eleven days in seventeen fifty-two? //
Because the old calendar was a little wrong for a very long time. It was eleven minutes a year, until it was eleven days.
And Parliament finally decided to say so. //
The new calendar is not perfect either. By our arithmetic, it is off by about a day every three thousand years. Dave's descendants will have to deal with that.
The same rule skipped the leap days of eighteen hundred and nineteen hundred in Britain, and almost nobody noticed. //
The story that people lost their minds probably says more about those who told it than about those who lived through it. //
Dave takes the banner down and puts the calendar back on the wall. He draws his ring around the nineteenth. //
He goes to bed on the eighteenth. He wakes up forty-one.
