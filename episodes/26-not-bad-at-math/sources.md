# Episode 26 "You Are Not Bad at Math": sources (verified 2026-10-15)

| "At darts, he counts down from five oh one in his head." / "But darts is math." | In the standard game, each player starts at 501 (or 301). Each turn's points are subtracted from the score, and the first to reach exactly zero, finishing on a double or the bull, wins. So a darts player does running subtraction every turn. Dave and his darts are fiction. | Wikipedia, Darts: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Darts |
| "In two thousand one, Mark Ashcraft measured the mental scratchpad of math dreaders. With words: normal. With sums: it shrank." | "Mental scratchpad" = working memory. Ashcraft & Kirk (2001), Experiments 1 and 3: on two verbal span tasks, "no significant anxiety-group differences at all". On a computation-based span task, "a pronounced decline in assessed working memory capacity" for high-math-anxious people (correlation .40). | Ashcraft M.H., Kirk E.P., "The relationships among working memory, math anxiety, and performance", *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General* 2001;130(2):224-237. Summarised by the same author in Ashcraft M.H., Krause J.A., "Working memory, math performance, and math anxiety", *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review* 2007;14(2):243-248: https://www.andrews.edu/ceis/gpc/faculty-research/montagano-research/working_memory_math.pdf |
| "Something sits on it, only when it smells math." | Ashcraft & Krause 2007: "a math-anxious person's working memory resources are drained ... only when the actual math anxiety is aroused, as in span tasks that involve computations." And: "High math anxiety works much like a dual task setting: Preoccupation with one's math fears and anxieties functions like a resource-demanding secondary task." | Ashcraft & Krause 2007, as above |
| (Supports "Dave isn't bad at math.") | Ashcraft & Krause 2007: on untimed paper-and-pencil tests of whole-number arithmetic, "no math-anxiety differences on accuracy". Achievement-test scores "probably underestimate levels of math achievement among high-anxious participants". After effective anxiety interventions (with no maths teaching), "math achievement scores approach those in the normal range" (citing Hembree 1990). | Ashcraft & Krause 2007, as above |
| "In twenty twelve, Ian Lyons scanned them. As a sum approached, pain areas lit up." | fMRI, 14 high- and 14 low-math-anxious adults. The higher a person's math anxiety, the more activity there was *in anticipation of* a math task in the bilateral dorso-posterior insula and the mid-cingulate cortex, regions the authors link to visceral threat and pain. Title: "When Math Hurts". | Lyons I.M., Beilock S.L., "When Math Hurts: Math Anxiety Predicts Pain Network Activation in Anticipation of Doing Math", *PLoS ONE* 2012;7(10):e48076: https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0048076 |
| "During the math? Gone. It hurt to wait, not to add." | Same paper: "this relation was not seen during math performance". The authors conclude that it is the anticipation of math, not the math itself, that is painful. | Same paper |
| "The thief comes early: worry." | Beilock's account (the Science paper's premise): worries about a test use up working memory, the mental scratch pad needed for the task. Combined with the two rows above (working memory drained when the anxiety is aroused; the pain-network signal comes before the math). | UChicago News, 13 Jan 2011: https://news.uchicago.edu/story/writing-about-worries-eases-anxiety-and-improves-test-performance |
| "In twenty eleven, Gerardo Ramirez tested a fix before a ninth grade final. Anxious students who wrote their worries down for ten minutes averaged B plus. Anxious non-writers: B minus." | Ramirez & Beilock (2011), Studies 3 and 4: ninth-grade biology students before their first high-school final. Test anxiety was measured six weeks earlier. Before the exam, students got envelopes telling them either to write about their feelings about the test (10 minutes) or to think about topics that wouldn't be on the test. Highly test-anxious students who wrote averaged B+; highly test-anxious students who didn't averaged B−. Gerardo Ramirez was Beilock's graduate student and first author. | Ramirez G., Beilock S.L., "Writing About Testing Worries Boosts Exam Performance in the Classroom", *Science* 2011;331(6014):211-213, doi 10.1126/science.1199427 · UChicago News (as above) |
| (Lab result, behind "a fix") | Study 1: 20 college students, two short math tests; before the second, pressure (money, a team counting on them, videotape reviewed by math teachers). The control group sat quietly for 10 minutes and "choked under pressure", with a 12 % accuracy drop. The expressive-writing group showed "a significant 5 percent math accuracy improvement". Study 2: writing about something unrelated did not help, so it was the worry-writing specifically. | Same paper; UChicago News (as above) |
| "Next test, ten minutes: write every worry down." | The one safe action: 10 minutes of expressive writing about your thoughts and feelings on the test, right before it. Beilock: "students can take time to write about their worries and should accordingly improve their performance." No grade or number is promised in the script. | Same paper and press release |

## Checked and NOT used
- **"Math person" genes / born-with-it claims:** not used. The script makes no heritability claim either way; it only says Dave's arithmetic works and the anxiety interferes.
- **Kimball & Smith (2013), "The Myth of 'I'm Bad at Math'" (The Atlantic):** an opinion essay by two economists, not a study. Not cited.
- **Carraher, Carraher & Schliemann (1985), "Mathematics in the streets and in schools":** street-vendor children did far better on sums in the market than on the same sums at school. It would fit the darts beat, but I could not verify the exact percentages from the paper, the sample was 5 children, and the authors explain it by method (oral vs school algorithms), not anxiety. Cut.
- **Lang & Lang (2011) letter in *Science*:** argued that the writing tool may not help, or may harm, students with *low* test anxiety. Ramirez & Beilock replied in the same issue. The script only claims the result for anxious students ("Anxious students who wrote... B plus"), and the B+/B− comparison is between highly anxious students.
- **Lyons & Beilock sample:** 28 adults; the pain-region result is a correlation with anxiety level during anticipation. The script says "pain areas lit up", not "math causes pain", and the next line says the signal was gone during the math itself.
- **Ashcraft & Kirk 2001 dual task (two-column addition with a six-letter memory load):** high-anxious participants made the most errors on carry problems under load. Consistent with the script, left out for length.
- **Beilock et al. 2010 (teachers' math anxiety and girls' achievement):** true but answers no question this story asks, and it would add a gender angle the episode doesn't need.
- **Ep16 material (Einstein, London taxi drivers, the testing effect):** deliberately not reused.
