## Checked and NOT used
- **Peter Tripp's hallucinations** (mice, kittens, "a neurologist mistaken for an undertaker", "nocturnal psychosis"): well reported (Coren 1998; Guinness 2023), but for the last ~66 hours his doctors gave him a stimulant (Ritalin), and Guinness reports that Dement said the drug was more likely than the sleep loss to have caused his paranoia and hallucinations. So they can't be pinned on sleep loss, and the brief says nothing about drugs. The script uses only the booth, the charity and the hours.
- **Tripp's later life** (lost his job, "a changed individual", the payola scandal): anecdotal, contested even in NPR 2017 ("some of that might have been hyperbole"), and unkind. Not used.
- **Tom Rounds, Honolulu, 260 hours** (the record Gardner beat; NPR 2017/2024, Wikipedia): true, cut for length.
- **Maureen Weston, 449 hours in a 1977 rocking-chair marathon** (Guinness 2023): true; McDonald's 453 h is the last record, so we used his.
- **Toimi Silvo, Finland, 276 hours in February 1964**: "some sources report" (Wikipedia via the Guardian); unverified.
- **Tony Wright (2007) and the 2024 livestreamer who stayed up for 12 days**: real, but naming them would advertise attempts at a record Guinness closed for safety.
- **Fatal familial insomnia** (Guinness's third reason): a rare fatal genetic disease. Too grim for the tone, and the series doesn't joke near illness.
- **Matthew Walker's daylight-saving claim** (NPR 2017: a 24 % rise in heart attacks after the spring change, 21 % fall in autumn): contested in later analyses; not used. The same goes for "sleep is not like a bank, you can't catch up": a strong claim the script doesn't need.
- **The Gardner press-conference quote** "I wanted to prove that bad things didn't happen if you went without sleep" (Wikipedia): its sources are a personal homepage and a 2006 magazine. We used his NPR 2017 words instead.
- **Vaccaro 2020's antioxidant rescue** (flies given antioxidants lived a normal lifespan with almost no sleep): true in flies, but on screen it could read as "take supplements instead of sleeping". Not used (no medical advice).
- **Dawson & Reid 1997, *Nature*** (24 h awake ≈ 0.10 % BAC on a tracking task): a classic, but the brief asked for Williamson & Feyer, and two alcohol studies would be a stack.
- **Tamaki et al. 2016, *Current Biology*** (on the first night in a new place, one hemisphere of the human brain sleeps lighter and stays more alert to odd sounds): true and charming, cut for length. A good line for a Short.
- **Gardner's "Coca-Cola, loud music and cold showers"** (Guinness 2023): not used (stimulant).
- **Year Guinness stopped:** NPR 2024 says 1996; Guinness's own 2023 article and Wikipedia say 1997. We use 1997 (the record keeper's own statement).
- **Cause of Gardner's insomnia:** Wikipedia says he "blamed [it] on the 1960s study"; Guinness says he attributed it to his cat's death; the 2017 NPR audio has both (the cat "triggered it" and "karmic payback"); NPR 2024 says the trigger is unclear. The script reports both of his own views and claims no cause.
