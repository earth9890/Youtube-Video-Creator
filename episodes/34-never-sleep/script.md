# chapter: The Record Attempt
This is Dave, and tonight, Dave is not going to sleep. / Not tonight, not tomorrow, not for eleven days. //
He has a scoreboard, a bag of tangerines, and a target: / two hundred and sixty-four hours awake. //
"How hard can it be? I'm just not doing something." // [J]
But in nineteen ninety-seven, the Guinness record book stopped accepting this record. / Dave is about to find out why. //
So, what happens if you never sleep? //
We'll follow Dave hour by hour, with a radio host in a glass box, / a teenager who beat a sleep scientist at pinball, / and a bird that naps while flying. //
And one more contestant, quietly attempting this record for years: you. //

# chapter: Hour Seventeen
By hour seventeen, it's nearly midnight, and Dave feels great. //
In two thousand, Ann Williamson and Anne-Marie Feyer in Sydney tested thirty-nine transport workers and soldiers. //
After seventeen to nineteen hours awake, / some of their scores matched what they got at zero point zero five percent blood alcohol, or worse. //
In Australia, that's the drink-driving limit. / Stay up longer, and it hit zero point one percent. //
Dave is stone-cold sober. His reaction times are not. // [J]
Ask him how he feels. / "Honestly, I feel totally fine." / Remember that word. It comes back. //

# chapter: The Science Fair
Dave studies the record holders. In nineteen fifty-nine, radio host Peter Tripp broadcast from a glass booth in Times Square, without sleeping, for charity. //
He lasted two hundred and one hours, / while a rival in Florida lasted two hundred and twenty-five. //
The Florida host was called Dave. Daves have been at this for a while. // [J]
Then, over the New Year of nineteen sixty-four, a San Diego seventeen-year-old, Randy Gardner, went for it, for a science fair. //
He and two friends flipped a coin to see who would be the experiment. Randy lost. // [J]
By day three, Randy felt sick every morning, and found that tangerines helped. / That's why Dave brought the tangerines. // [J]
At three in the morning, Randy went to visit the local jail. / Why? He's not sure. Probably because it was open. // [J]
And on day ten, the teenager who hadn't slept in ten days / beat a Stanford sleep scientist, William Dement, at pinball. // [J]
So far, it sounds almost easy. / But a navy medical officer, John Ross, was keeping notes. //
By day four, he was moody, forgetful, and unable to concentrate. //
Then came his first hallucination: / he mistook a street sign for a person. //
Next, he became convinced he was a famous football player. / He was, in fact, a science fair project. // [J]
On day eleven, Ross asked him to count down from a hundred in sevens. / (fast) A hundred, ninety-three, eighty-six, seventy-nine, seventy-two, sixty-five. //
Then he stopped, and Ross asked him why. / He'd forgotten what he was doing. // [J]
At two hundred and sixty-four hours, Randy went to bed. / He slept nearly fifteen hours, and woke up, in his words, no groggier than a normal person. //
So that's the answer? Eleven days, one long nap, back to normal? / Not quite. Even Randy's record has holes in it. //

# chapter: The Invisible Naps
Even Dement later accepted that Randy, watched around the clock, probably had microsleeps. //
A microsleep is a few seconds, fifteen at most, / when your brain slips into light sleep without asking you. //
Your eyes can even stay open. / And afterwards, you don't think you slept. You think you lost focus for a second. //
Hour forty, and Dave checks in again. / "Honestly, I feel totally..." //
Four seconds later, he finishes the sentence. / "Fine. Sorry, where was I?" // [J]
It gets stranger. In twenty eleven, a Wisconsin team kept rats awake and listened to single brain cells. //
The rats were up and active. / But small groups of their brain cells kept switching off, the way they do in sleep. //
The longer the rats stayed up, the more it happened, / and the worse they got at reaching for sugar pellets. //
Which, for a rat, is the one job that matters. // [J]
So the brain doesn't wait for permission. / If you won't sleep, it starts sleeping in pieces. //
And that's one reason Guinness quit in nineteen ninety-seven. / You can't prove someone stayed awake / when even they don't know they nodded off. // [J]
The other reason was simpler: it's dangerous. //
The last record they accepted, in nineteen eighty-six, was four hundred and fifty-three hours, / set in a rocking chair, the one piece of furniture designed to put people to sleep. // [J]
So Dave's certificate is off. / But what is sleep for, and can he skip just that part? //

# chapter: The Night Shift
In twenty thirteen, Maiken Nedergaard's lab at the University of Rochester watched the brains of sleeping mice. //
As they slept, the gaps between brain cells widened by about sixty percent, / and fluid rushed through, carrying waste away faster than when awake. //
So sleep looked less like switching off for the night, / and more like a dishwasher that only runs once the kitchen is closed. // [J]
But in twenty twenty-four, a team at Imperial College London measured it a different way, / and in their mice, clearing was slower during sleep, not faster. //
So how the brain cleans up is still argued about. / Whether sleep matters is not. //
That leaves Dave one last hope: sleep like the animals that seem not to. //

# chapter: Half a Brain
Dolphins can't just pass out in the sea, / so they sleep one half of the brain at a time, then swap. //
In a row of sleeping ducks, the ones at the ends keep one eye open more often, / usually the eye facing away from the group. //
The ducks in the middle just trust the ducks at the ends. // [J]
Frigatebirds fly over the ocean for days without landing. / In twenty sixteen, Niels Rattenborg's team put brain recorders on them. / In flight, they slept about forty minutes a day, in ten-second naps, often one half at a time. //
Then they land, and sleep over twelve hours a day. / The champion of not sleeping goes home and sleeps half the day. // [J]
None of them skip sleep. They just found loopholes. //
Dave tries the dolphin method, and closes one eye. / Both halves of his brain stay awake, / and now he's losing at pinball. // [J]
So what happens to an animal with no loophole at all? //
In the nineteen eighties, Allan Rechtschaffen's lab in Chicago kept rats on a platform that moved whenever they dozed. //
Within eleven to thirty-two days, none survived, / and nobody knew exactly why. //
In twenty twenty, a Harvard team found a clue in fruit flies kept awake: / harmful molecules piling up, not in their brains, but in their guts. //
Which is why no lab keeps a person awake for weeks, / and why Dave, finally, goes to bed. //

# chapter: The Slow Version
Dave goes back to normal life, about six hours a night, / and reads the study he should have read first. //
In two thousand three, Hans Van Dongen's team at the University of Pennsylvania / put forty-eight healthy adults in a sleep lab. //
For two weeks, some got eight hours in bed a night, some six, some four. //
By the end, the six-hour group was as impaired as people kept awake for a whole night. / The four-hour group, as if they'd skipped two. //
And how sleepy did they feel? / The researchers' words: only slightly sleepy. //
Their scores got worse every day. Their feelings stayed at fine. // [J]
A whole lab full of Daves at hour seventeen. / "Honestly, I feel totally fine." // [J]
So here's the twist: you don't need a glass booth or a science fair to attempt this record. / Every night you cut short is a tiny version of it. //
Next to that eight-hour group, Dave is two hours short every night. / After a hundred and thirty-two nights, that's two hundred and sixty-four hours. //
Randy's record, broken in instalments, with no reporters and no pinball. / And just like the lab, Dave hasn't noticed a thing. // [J]
So, what happens if you never sleep? / You can't, for long. Your brain takes it anyway, in pieces. / What you can do is borrow it, a little every night, without feeling the cost. //

So here's the one idea worth keeping. / Your feeling of fine is not a measurement, but a clock is. //
Set an alarm for when you go to bed, / not just for when you get up. //
