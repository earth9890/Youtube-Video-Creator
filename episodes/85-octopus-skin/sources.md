# Episode 85 "What If Your Skin Could Change Colour Like an Octopus?": sources (verified 2026-10-22)

Dave, Zoe, Mr Pike, the office party, the suit, the school play and "your skin has the octopus's whole kit" are story devices, not science claims. Everything the narrator or the cast states as fact is below. **Solid** = published source. **Inference** = our reasoning from a solid fact. **Open** = the science itself is unresolved and the script says so.

| Script line | Claim | Exact quote / number | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| "Chromatophores. Little sacs of pigment, each one with tiny muscles around the edge, pulling it open." | Solid. | Hanlon: "chromatophore organs, which are cytoelastic sacs of pigment with radial muscles attached around the periphery." Scitable: "pigment sacs that become more visible as small radial muscles pull the sac open". | Hanlon, "Cephalopod dynamic camouflage", Current Biology 17:R400 (2007): https://www.cell.com/current-biology/fulltext/S0960-9822(07)01138-4 ; Nature Scitable, "Cephalopod Camouflage: Cells and Organs of the Skin": https://www.nature.com/scitable/topicpage/cephalopod-camouflage-cells-and-organs-of-the-144048968/ |
| "Your brain wires straight to them." | Solid. | Hanlon: "Rapidity of visual change is accomplished by direct neural control of chromatophore organs... Each muscle is innervated by motoneurons that originate in the lower motor centers of the brain, and they travel without any synapse to each chromatophore organ." | Hanlon 2007 (above) |
| "An octopus has many thousands [of chromatophores]." | Solid (qualitative). | Smithsonian Ocean: "Many thousands of color-changing cells called chromatophores just below the surface of the skin are responsible for these remarkable transformations." | Smithsonian Ocean, "How Octopuses and Squids Change Color": https://ocean.si.edu/ocean-life/invertebrates/how-octopuses-and-squids-change-color |
| "[It] can change its whole look in under a second." | Solid, approximate. Changes take 200-700 ms; one full camouflaged-to-conspicuous switch in a field film took about two seconds. The script says "under a second" for the skin change and "about a second" in episode 84's sting. | Hanlon & Chiao: "Octopus, squid, and cuttlefish can change their appearance (phenotype) in 200-700 msec due to neural control of chromatophore organs, iridophore cells, and three-dimensional papillae". Hanlon 2007: "the time lapse of 270 milliseconds between images one and two. The total body pattern change as the octopus switched from camouflaged to fully conspicuous took place in 2.02 seconds." | Hanlon & Chiao, Oxford Handbook of Invertebrate Neurobiology (2018): https://doi.org/10.1093/oxfordhb/9780190456757.013.30 ; Hanlon 2007 (above) |
| "The pigment cells only come in red, orange, yellow, brown and black." | Solid. | Smithsonian Ocean: "an elastic sac full of pigment... which may be colored black, brown, orange, red or yellow." Scitable: "chromatophore pigments are typically only red, yellow, or brown". | Smithsonian Ocean; Scitable (above) |
| "Greens and blues come from a second layer. Shiny plates that bounce the light." | Solid. | Smithsonian Ocean: "Iridophores have stacks of reflecting plates that create iridescent greens, blues, silvers and golds, while leucophores mirror back the colors of the environment". | Smithsonian Ocean (above) |
| "Octopuses raise little bumps in their skin so their outline vanishes. Hunters find food by looking for edges." | Solid. | Smithsonian Ocean: "They can change not only their coloring, but also the texture of their skin... by controlling the size of projections on their skin (called papillae), creating textures ranging from small bumps to tall spikes." Scitable: "Many vertebrate predators find their prey by looking for visual edges and breaks in the background... having texture on the skin makes the cephalopod display a less identifiable edge." | Smithsonian Ocean; Scitable (above) |
| "A real octopus is colour-blind. Its eyes have one kind of light sensor. Yet it matches colours, and nobody is sure how." | Solid + **Open**. Single photoreceptor type in shallow-water cephalopods is established; whether and how they match colour is argued. | Stubbs & Stubbs: "cephalopods dramatically change color for both producing chromatically matched camouflage and signaling to conspecifics, despite having a single photoreceptor channel." "only one photoreceptor type exists in the retina of shallow water cephalopods". Scitable: "The most mysterious quality about cephalopod crypsis is that cephalopods are believed to be colorblind". | Stubbs & Stubbs, PNAS 113:8206 (2016): https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4961147/ ; Scitable (above) |
| "One idea is that its skin senses light by itself." | Solid as a finding (excised octopus skin responds to light) and **Open** as an explanation of colour matching. | Ramirez & Oakley: "we found that light causes chromatophores to expand in excised pieces of Octopus bimaculoides skin... octopus skin is intrinsically light sensitive and that this dispersed light sense might contribute to their unique and novel patterning abilities." | Ramirez & Oakley, J Exp Biol 218:1513 (2015): https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/218/10/1513/759/ |
| "Another is that its odd pupil blurs colours differently, and it reads the blur." | Solid as a published hypothesis; **contested** (Gagnon et al. 2016 PNAS reply argues it would not work well in nature, and Stubbs & Stubbs themselves rate the skin-opsin idea as "not currently viable"). The script says "one idea... another". | Stubbs & Stubbs: "An off-axis pupil and the principle of chromatic aberration... can combine to provide 'color-blind' animals with a way to distinguish colors." Gagnon et al.: "Although the Stubbs and Stubbs mechanism works in theory, we identified several factors that would decrease the utility of the suggested signal". | Stubbs & Stubbs (above); Gagnon et al., PNAS 113:E6908: https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1612239113 |
| "In two thousand eighteen, a research team showed that people can read emotions from face colour alone, with no muscle movement." | Solid. | "We show that people successfully decode emotion using these color features, even in the absence of any facial muscle activation." | Benitez-Quiroz, Srinivasan & Martinez, PNAS 115:3581 (2018): https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1716084115 |
| "Blood flow under the skin shows what you feel." | Solid (their hypothesis, supported by their data). | "Blood flow variations in these vessels yield visible color changes on the face." | Benitez-Quiroz et al. (above) |

## Checked and NOT used
- Exact chromatophore count (about 1.5 to 3 million in some species is often quoted): not verified this pass; the script says "many thousands" (Smithsonian).
- Mimic octopus (Thaumoctopus mimicus) impersonating 15+ species: true (Smithsonian) but off the mission.
- Whether octopus camouflage is true colour matching or "colour disruption": argued; not asserted either way.
- "Octopuses can see polarised light": real, not needed.
