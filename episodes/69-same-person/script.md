# chapter: The loophole
This is Dave. Seven years ago, Dave borrowed fifty dollars from a friend, and he has been avoiding the subject ever since.
Today, Dave has found a way out. He read that every seven years, every cell in your body is replaced. So the Dave who borrowed the money no longer exists. // [J]
He has even rehearsed the speech.
"I am a new man, and I would like that noted." (@dave)
The friend would still like the fifty dollars. //
So are you the same person you were seven years ago? And is Dave off the hook? //
The answer involves a bomb, a ship, and the oldest part of Dave's brain. //
Let's start with where the seven years came from. //

# chapter: Where seven came from
No one has shown that every cell in your body is replaced in seven years. The nearest real number comes from Jonas Frisén, a stem cell scientist in Sweden.
In two thousand five, he estimated that the average age of all the cells in an adult's body may be as young as seven to ten years.
An average is a slippery thing. Average a newborn and a seventy-year-old, and you get thirty-five. Nobody in the room is thirty-five. // [J]
So seven is a rounded average, not a rule.
The real question is which of your cells are old, and which are new. //
Dave sets his jaw. He needs the right answer to be most of them. //

# chapter: A clock made by a bomb
How do you find the age of a single cell?
In the nineteen fifties and sixties, nuclear weapons tests sent a spike of carbon-fourteen into the atmosphere. At its peak, the level had roughly doubled.
After a test ban in nineteen sixty-three, it fell, year by year.
Every living thing takes up that carbon. And when a cell divides, it locks that year's carbon-fourteen level into its DNA, like a date stamp.
So to date a cell, you read the level in its DNA, and compare it with the atmosphere's history. A very bad idea accidentally gave us a very good clock. // [J]
Frisén's team used it on cells from real people, and published the first results in two thousand five.
Teeth are the clearest example. Enamel is never replaced, so it carries the date stamp from childhood.
Dave's teeth are the only witnesses that can prove he was born in time to borrow the money. // [J]

# chapter: A body in layers
Dave draws a body outline on the wall, and starts filling it in. The lining of your gut lasts about five days.
Your skin lasts weeks. Red blood cells last around four months.
"But my hand signed the note, not my gut." (@dave)
That hand's skin has been replaced dozens of times since then. The signature, if anything, has improved. // [J]
So the lining of Dave's gut has been replaced since breakfast. It's why it never remembers what you did to it. // [J]
Fat cells change more slowly. In two thousand eight, researchers found that about one in ten is replaced each year, at every weight.
Dave's fat cells are the only part of him on a ten-year lease. // [J]
Bone is rebuilt about every ten years. The muscle cells in your ribs average about fifteen years. They have seen things. // [J]
And the heart barely changes. About one percent of heart muscle cells turn over each year at age twenty-five, and under half a percent at seventy-five.
Fewer than half of the heart muscle cells you were born with are swapped out in a whole lifetime. //
Some things are never swapped. At the centre of the lens in your eye, researchers found no turnover of the fats in the cells for an entire human lifespan.
Dave has been looking at this problem through the same lens since birth. // [J]
His diagram now looks like a quilt, with squares that are five days old, squares that are fifteen years old, and one square as old as Dave. //
So the myth is false. Not every cell is replaced in seven years, and some are never replaced. Dave's friend is quietly pleased. //
But wait. In two thousand twenty-one, two researchers added up the whole body.
They put its turnover at about a third of a trillion cells a day, and nearly nine in ten of those are blood cells.
In weight, it is about eighty grams a day. Dave replaces a small bar of soap's worth of himself daily, and has never noticed. // [J]
You have about thirty trillion human cells. By our arithmetic, that is roughly one percent of them, every day. Dave is, by number, mostly a blood factory with opinions. // [J]
You also carry about as many bacteria as human cells. They are not on the IOU. // [J]
"So the cells that borrowed the money are mostly gone?" (@dave) //
Mostly, yes. Dave's argument is better than the myth. Don't tell him. // [J]
But a company can replace every employee and still owe the bank. So it depends on what did the borrowing. // [J]

# chapter: The ship
This puzzle is very old. In his Life of Theseus, Plutarch describes the ship that Theseus sailed home from Crete.
The Athenians kept it for centuries, taking away the old timbers as they decayed and putting new ones in.
Plutarch says it became a standing illustration for the philosophers. Some said it remained the same ship, and others said it was not.
It spent centuries as a philosophy exhibit, which is a hard job for a boat. // [J]
In sixteen fifty-five, the philosopher Thomas Hobbes added a twist. What if someone had kept the old planks, and built a second ship from them? //
Which one is Theseus's ship? People have argued about that for over three hundred years, which is longer than the ship would have lasted. // [J]
So is Dave his planks, or something else? To find out, look at the one part of him that never swapped planks. //

# chapter: The oldest part of Dave
Here is the twist. In that same two thousand five study, the cells of the visual cortex, at the back of the brain, were as old as the person.
Frisén guessed the reason: they probably need to be wired in a very stable way. Even the balance centre, the cerebellum, was only about three years younger than the person.
A later study checked the neurons across the cerebral cortex, and found the same. They are not made in adulthood at detectable levels. They are made around birth. //
So the part of Dave that remembers borrowing the money is original equipment. // [J]
The memory centre, the hippocampus, may be a partial exception. One study found about seven hundred new neurons a day in each one.
Another reported undetectable levels in adults. That fight is not over. //
But here is the catch. Even old neurons are rebuilt from the inside. The proteins and lipids at a synapse have lifetimes from minutes to days.
A few last months or longer, and researchers think those may help hold memories in place. //
The cells stay. The parts keep changing. And yet a memory can last a lifetime. //

# chapter: Same ship
So here is one way out. A ship is not its planks. It is a pattern that planks are fitted into. //
Now imagine every plank in Dave swapped overnight. He wakes with the same memories, the same habits, and the same debt.
Would his friend refuse the money? Almost certainly not. His friend is not a philosopher. // [J]
Dave is not his cells. He is a pattern the cells keep carrying: his memories, his habits, and his promises.
It is why your friends recognise you after a long weekend, or a long decade. They are recognising the pattern, not the planks. //
So are you the same person you were seven years ago? //
Not the same cells. Most of you is younger than seven, some of it is far older, and even the old parts are rebuilt as they go.
But you are the same pattern. The Dave who borrowed the money had a plan to pay it back. That plan is part of the pattern too. //
Dave looks at the wall. His diagram has a ship on it now, and a thin plain keel in the middle. //
He puts the IOU on the table, and stamps it paid. //
Dave hands over the fifty dollars. Same ship, same debt.
