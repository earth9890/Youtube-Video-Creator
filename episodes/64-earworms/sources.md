# Episode 64 "Why Do Songs Get Stuck in Your Head?": sources (verified 2026-10-22)

Full text read: Jakubowski et al. 2017 (PDF), Beaman, Powell & Rapley 2015 (accepted manuscript), Williamson et al. 2012 (first pages), Ghibellini & Meier 2025 (open access). Abstracts or summaries: Hyman et al. 2013 (Wiley abstract + BPS Research Digest), Liikkanen 2012, Williamson et al. 2014, MacLeod 2020 (abstract snippet). Dave, the supermarket jingle, the nan, the ukulele, the bus stop and the eleven seconds are invented story devices. No song lyrics or titles are used; the experimental song titles in the papers are deliberately not named.

| "The scientific name is involuntary musical imagery… earworm, from the German Ohrwurm. A tune comes into your mind and repeats, without your say-so." | Definition. | Williamson et al. 2012: "Involuntary Musical Imagery (INMI) or 'earworms' describes the experience whereby a tune comes into the mind and repeats without conscious control"; "…most frequently described… as 'earworms' (a direct translation for the German word Ohrwurm)". https://doc.gold.ac.uk/~mas03dm/papers/Williamson_etal_Earworms_POM_2012.pdf |
| "In a study at Goldsmiths in London, three thousand people were asked which tunes got stuck. Songs that had done better in the charts, and left them more recently, were named far more often." | Jakubowski, Finkel, Stewart & Müllensiefen 2017, *Psych of Aesthetics, Creativity, and the Arts* 11:122. | Abstract: "a dataset of tunes that were named as INMI by 3,000 survey participants. It was found that songs that had achieved greater success and more recent runs in the U.K. music charts were reported more frequently as INMI." https://doi.org/10.1037/aca0000090 |
| "They took a hundred tunes that people named, and matched each with a tune nobody named, with the same popularity and style." | Same. | "A set of 100 of these frequently named INMI tunes was then matched to 100 tunes never named as INMI by the survey participants, in terms of popularity and song style." |
| "A computer then scored all two hundred tunes on eighty-three melodic features." | Same (automated analysis, FANTASTIC toolbox). | "These 2 groups of tunes were compared using 83 statistical summary and corpus-based melodic features and automated classification techniques." |
| "The earworms came out faster. They had more common overall shapes, usually an arch… And they were more likely to include unusual leaps." | Same. | "INMI tunes were found to have more common global melodic contours and less common average gradients between melodic turning points than non-INMI tunes… INMI tunes also displayed faster average tempi"; discussion: "the tunes with more common global contours… assume fairly arch-shaped phrases"; "the tunes with less common average contour gradients tend to contain many melodic leaps… or unusually large melodic leaps." (Tempo was "only marginally significant in the logistic regression analysis".) |
| "In two thousand eleven, Victoria Williamson's team at Goldsmiths asked how earworms start, using radio listeners and an online survey. They found four kinds… Exposure… A memory trigger… A particular mood… low attention." | Williamson et al., published online Sep 2011 (Psychology of Music 40:259, 2012). | Abstract: "listeners to the BBC radio station 6 Music and an online survey. Within the two models, four abstract categories were defined… Music exposure, Memory triggers, Affective states, and Low attention states respectively." |
| "Many earworms also turn up during repetitive movement, like walking or running." | Diary studies. | Jakubowski 2017 discussion: "a large proportion of INMI episodes reported by participants in previous diary studies occurred during repetitive movements, such as walking or running (Jakubowski et al., 2015)." |
| "In two thousand twelve, Ira Hyman's team at Western Washington University found the opposite. Songs people knew and liked became intrusive most often." | Hyman et al., *Applied Cognitive Psychology* 27:204 (online Dec 2012). | Abstract: "Contrary to the belief that only obnoxious songs get stuck, we found that songs people know and like frequently became intrusive." https://doi.org/10.1002/acp.2897 |
| "songs from a sixties band were as likely as modern hits." | Experiments used three songs by a 1960s band and by contemporary acts. | BPS Research Digest (Jarrett, 27 Dec 2012): "Beatles songs were just as likely to become earworms as modern hits." https://www.bps.org.uk/research-digest/how-kill-earworm |
| "One study found a tune was more likely to stick after six plays than after two." | Byron & Fowles 2013, cited by Jakubowski. | Jakubowski 2017 discussion: "Byron and Fowles (2013) reported that previously unfamiliar songs were more likely to become INMI if participants were exposed to them six rather than two times." (Primary paper not read.) |
| "a song that kept playing right after it ended was likely to return as an earworm within a day. It tended to come back during easy, low-effort moments." | Hyman 2013. | Abstract: "if a song continued to play in someone's head immediately after listening to it, the song was likely to return as an intrusive song within the next 24 hours… people reported that intrusive songs returned during low cognitive load activities". |
| "People who play or listen to a lot of music get more of them, and the rate falls with age." | Liikkanen 2012, *Psychology of Music* 40:236. | Abstract: "The amount of music practice and listening was positively related to the frequency of involuntary music… In older age-groups the frequency of the incidents decreased among both sexes." https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0305735611406578 |
| "A survey of twelve and a half thousand Finnish internet users found that nearly nine in ten had an earworm at least once a week." | Same. | "N = 12,519… 89.2% of participants reported experiencing this phenomenon at least once a week." (Liikkanen's 2008 survey reported 91.7 % of 12,420; the script uses the 2012 figure.) |
| "Hyman's team had a theory… the Zeigarnik effect… an unfinished tune should nag too." | Hyman's own suggestion. | BPS Digest: "Ira Hyman Jr. and his colleagues believe this last detail may be a manifestation of the classic Zeigarnik Effect, whereby incomplete tasks remain in memory but evaporate once completed. In the case of earworms, the researchers propose that the playing of only part of a song in your head leaves it incomplete and thereby increases the likelihood that it will return against your will as an earworm." |
| "The story goes that a psychologist noticed a waiter who remembered unpaid orders perfectly, and forgot them the moment they were paid. The psychologist Bluma Zeigarnik tested it in nineteen twenty-seven." | Zeigarnik 1927, *Psychologische Forschung* 9:1; the waiter story is the field's origin story (Lewin's observation). | MacLeod 2020, *Memory & Cognition*, "Zeigarnik and von Restorff: The memory effects and the stories behind them": "Zeigarnik's dissertation connected Lewin's field theory to his observation about the waiter's memory." The anecdote's accuracy is itself discussed as a "story" there; the script says "the story goes". https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13421-020-01033-5 |
| "When researchers sorted over a thousand reports of how people react to earworms, one of the most popular and effective responses was to seek out the tune. Another was musical or verbal distraction." | Williamson, Liikkanen, Jakubowski & Stewart 2014, *PLoS ONE* 9:e86170. | Abstract: "The present study classified 1046 reports of how people react to INMI episodes… The most popular and effective behaviours in response to INMI were seeking out the tune in question, and musical or verbal distraction." https://research.gold.ac.uk/id/eprint/10059 |

| "In twenty twenty-five, the psychologists Romain Ghibellini and Beat Meier at the University of Bern pulled together the evidence on the Zeigarnik effect. They found thirty-eight studies." | Ghibellini & Meier 2025, *Humanities and Social Sciences Communications*. | "Of these publications, 38 investigated the Zeigarnik effect, 20 investigated the Ovsiankina effect, and 1 investigated both." https://www.nature.com/articles/s41599-025-05000-w |
| "Interrupted and finished tasks were recalled about equally often, a ratio of point nine nine." | Same. | "a weighted ratio mean(IR)/mean(CR) = 0.99 resulted (N = 38 publications), suggesting that interrupted tasks are recalled about the same as finished tasks." Abstract: "We found no memory advantage for unfinished tasks". |
| "The authors suggest the original result may belong to its time, when people treated experimenters as figures of great authority." | Their explanation (flagged as a suggestion). | "We discuss the Zeigarnik effect's potential dependency on situational influences and individual differences, such as the experimenter's authority, situational demands of task performance, and task involvement. These circumstances were more prevalent historically and are rarer today, which could explain the difficulty in replicating the Zeigarnik effect." |
| "When students heard only part of a song instead of the whole track, they were no more likely to get an earworm." | Hyman's own test. | BPS Digest: "Only playing part of songs to students, as opposed to the whole track, did not increase the risk of earworms." (Digest summary; the paper's full text was not read.) |
| "People resumed an interrupted task about two-thirds of the time, a pull to go back. Whether it applies to tunes is untested." | Ovsiankina effect (Ovsiankina 1928). | "interrupted tasks are resumed roughly 67% of the time (see Fig. 5), well above the chance rate of 50%." I found no study applying this to earworms; the script says "untested". |
| "In two thousand fifteen, Philip Beaman's team at the University of Reading… suspected that hearing a tune in your head leans on the same machinery you use to speak." | Beaman, Powell & Rapley 2015, *QJEP* 68:1049. | Abstract: "Three experiments examine the role of articulatory motor planning in experiencing an involuntary musical recollection (an 'earworm')." https://doi.org/10.1080/17470218.2015.1034142 |
| "Earlier work had shown that chewing gum interferes with remembering words, and with scanning a familiar melody in your head." | Same abstract. | "chewing gum interferes with voluntary processes such as recollections from verbal memory, the interpretation of ambiguous auditory images, and the scanning of familiar melodies". |
| "forty-four students heard the chorus of a pop song twice, then were asked not to think about it for three minutes." | Experiment 1. | Full text: "Forty-four undergraduate students (10 male)"; "Participants were played the first 30s of the song (the chorus) twice… they were asked to try not to think of the music they had just heard for a timed three minute period." |
| "They did it twice, once chewing gum and once not. Chewing reduced the number of times the tune came back, wanted or not." | Same. | "The gum/no-gum conditions were counterbalanced in a repeated-measures design"; abstract: "interfering with articulatory motor programming by chewing gum reduces both the number of voluntary and the number of involuntary-unwanted-musical thoughts." |
| "In two more experiments, gum affected the experience of hearing the tune, not only thinking about it, and tapping your fingers helped less than chewing did." | Experiments 2 (18 students) and 3. | Abstract: "Experiment 2 shows that chewing the gum affects the experience of 'hearing' the music and cannot be ascribed to a general effect on thinking about a tune only in abstract terms."; full text, Exp. 3: "motor activity per se (tapping) is less effective than motor activity which is specifically sub-vocal" (chewing vs tapping t(35) = 2.36, p = .02; tapping vs control n.s.). |
| "It was three small experiments with one song, and a lab tune is not a three-day jingle." | Limitations. | Full text, note 3: "'Earworms' are highly idiosyncratic and individual when encountered in an everyday setting, a situation that is difficult to replicate experimentally". All three experiments used one song, three- or two-minute periods. |
| "Hyman's team had also found that challenging puzzles, like a hard sudoku, raised the rate of earworms rather than cutting it." | Hyman 2013. | Abstract: "we found that overloading the cognitive systems with challenging activities increased intrusive song frequency." BPS Digest: "Overly challenging sudoku or anagram tasks helped breed more earworms (the former more so than the latter)." |
| "For a small number of people, an earworm is distressing, and that deserves real help, not gum. For most, it's a passing tune." / "many were neutral or pleasant, and passive acceptance and enjoyment were among the most common responses." | Williamson 2014. | Abstract: "The majority of INMI episodes are not bothersome, while some cause disruption ranging from distraction to anxiety and distress"; "many INMI episodes were considered neutral or pleasant, with passive acceptance and enjoyment being among the most popular response behaviours." |

## Checked and NOT used
- **Williamson 2014's own recommendation list** (no single recommended technique): the abstract only gives "testable hypotheses for future research on successful INMI coping strategies"; the script therefore calls its toolkit "honest" and ranks it by kind of evidence.
- **Zeigarnik's original "twice as well" figure**: not in any source I read; the meta-analysis says only that the original numbers "are inflated".
- **Kellaris's "cognitive itch" (2001/2003)**: named in Williamson 2012 as a term; the original not read.
- **Beaman's literary-history review (Poe's "The Imp of the Perverse")**: found in the QJEP acknowledgements; cut for length.
- **Tempo-entrainment idea**: Jakubowski suggests earworms might favour tempos easy to step to; speculative, not used.
- **Why a particular tune (Dave's) stuck**: nobody can predict an individual earworm; the script says features and circumstances raise the odds.
