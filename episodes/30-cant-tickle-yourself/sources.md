| "So Dave builds a tickle machine. A feather on a stick, a string and a lever. He pulls the lever. The feather moves, his ribs feel it, and his brain shrugs." | Story device. | "Here's why. Whenever your brain orders a muscle to move, it sends a copy of the order to your senses. Biologists named it in nineteen fifty: the efference copy." | Blakemore 1999: the forward model uses "'efference copy' (Von Holst, 1954) or 'corollary discharge' (Sperry, 1950)". "Your brain copies itself in on every order" is the narrator's email analogy. | Blakemore 1999 · Shergill 2003 reference list (refs 1-2) · Wikipedia, Efference copy: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Efference_copy |
| "What you feel is what happened, minus what the copy predicted. ## Caveats on the evidence (kept off screen, stated here)
- **Small samples, subjective ratings.** The founding tickle studies are small: 6 people in the 1998 scanner, 16 with the robot (1999), 6 pairs plus 12 (2003), 30 (2019). Ratings are self-reports. The core finding (self-produced touch feels weaker) has been reproduced many times with force-matching tasks, e.g. Kilteni & Ehrsson 2017 (*PNAS* 114:8426) and the 2019 eLife study.
- **Not all of tickling is explained.** The prediction account explains why self-touch is weaker. It does not explain why tickling makes us laugh at all, which is still open (reflex vs social play; Harris & Christenfeld vs Panksepp). The script presents the Harris result and the rat-play result side by side and draws only the narrow conclusion ("it just can't be you"). Kilteni's 2026 *Scientific American* piece calls prediction "considered part of why most people can't tickle themselves" (https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/scientists-built-a-tickle-robot-to-solve-one-of-biologys-strangest-mysteries).
- **Blakemore et al. 2000 is one small study.** 15-17 patients with the experiences; the three-group comparison was only marginally significant (chi-square P = 0.09), the planned two-group test P < 0.05. Supporting work: Shergill et al. 2005 (*Am J Psychiatry* 162:2384), "Patients with schizophrenia demonstrated significantly less sensory attenuation than healthy subjects" (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16330607/); Lemaitre, Luyat & Lafargue 2016 (*Consciousness and Cognition* 41:64), healthy students high in schizotypal traits (n = 27 vs 27) found self-tickling more ticklish (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26891191/). The script therefore says "seemed to follow" and "one theory, still debated", talks about experiences rather than a diagnosis, and has no jokes in that stretch.
- **Rat "laughter" is an interpretation.** Panksepp's own title has "(laughter?)"; Burgdorf has called the chirps "a signal of positive affect". The script uses "laugh" for rats in the roadmap ("why rats laugh"), the chapter title and "the laughing mostly stopped", following Panksepp's framing; the facts themselves are said as chirps. Swap to "chirping" if the owner wants zero interpretation.
- **Eye stability.** Corollary-discharge evidence is mostly from monkeys; human evidence is psychophysical. Hence "partly because".

## Checked and NOT used
- **von Holst & Mittelstaedt's fly with its head rotated 180 degrees** (1950): the experiment is real (Wikipedia, Efference copy: "they rotated the head of a fly 180 degrees ... the animal showed a reinforcement of the optokinetic response"), but I could not confirm the often-told detail that it spun in circles from a primary or strong secondary source, and it reads as unkind to the fly. Cut.
- **Van Doorn, Hohwy & Symmons 2014** ("Can you tickle yourself if you swap bodies with someone else?", *Consciousness and Cognition* 23:1-11): a body-swap illusion "had no influence on the ratings of tickliness". A great Dave scene (he tries VR body swap), cut for length. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24270589/
- **Kilteni & Ehrsson 2017** rubber-hand study (attenuation only when the rubber hand felt like one's own): verified, cut for length. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28716932/
- **Hektor, the tickle robot** at Kilteni's Touch & Tickle Lab (Karolinska / Donders), *Scientific American* April 2026: no findings published yet, so not used.
- **Problemata XXXV.1** ("Why do we shudder more when some one else touches us than when we touch ourselves?"): lovely but a second Aristotle quote would be a documentary stack.
- **Darwin on tickled young chimpanzees and orangs** ("a more decided chuckling or laughing sound is uttered"): verified, cut; the rats carry the animal beat.
- **Weiskrantz et al. 1971 details** (sole of the foot; passive arm movement gave an intermediate tickle): read only as described in Blakemore 1999; the 1971 PDF is paywalled. Used only implicitly ("mostly guessed").
- **Helmholtz eye-press demonstration** (press the side of your eye and the world jumps): true and classic, but it would mean telling viewers to press on their eyeballs. Not used.
- **"Your recorded voice sounds strange because your brain turns you down"**: not supported; recordings are explained by bone conduction (Hullar). The script keeps the two facts separate.
