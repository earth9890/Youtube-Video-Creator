# chapter: The hand that does nothing
Right now, try something. / Wiggle your fingertips lightly across the palm of your other hand. //
Nothing, right? Maybe a polite itch. // [J]
Now picture someone else doing exactly the same thing, without warning. //
Same skin, same fingers, same movement. / The only thing that changed is who's doing it. //
So why can't you tickle yourself? / An ancient Greek book of puzzles, credited to Aristotle, asks exactly that. //
For centuries, people mostly guessed. / Then, in the nineteen nineties, somebody built a robot. //
And the answer turned out to be one fifth of a second. //
This is Dave. Dave is the most ticklish man in his family, / and his sister knows it.
She has won every argument since they were six / using two fingers and his left armpit. // [J]
So Dave has a plan. Every night, he'll tickle himself until he's immune. //
Dave is going to fail. / Not because his technique is bad, though it is. / Because his brain sees him coming. // [J]
How it sees him coming explains why fights escalate, why rats laugh, / and what your brain is doing to you right now. //

# chapter: Darwin's guess
Night one. Dave picks up a feather / and goes straight for his own ribs. /
Then the armpits and feet, / the spots that Greek book says tickle most.
Nothing. Just a grown man, alone, touching his own armpit. // [J]
Tickles come in two kinds. / Knismesis, the light itchy kind, which Dave can manage, / and gargalesis, the laughing kind, which he can't. //
In eighteen seventy-two, Charles Darwin made the same guess as that Greek book: / the precise point to be touched must not be known. /
Darwin had field data. / He once tickled the foot of his own week-old baby with a bit of paper. // [J]
He also noted that feet tickle despite being pressed on all day, / with one marked exception: the surface on which we sit. // [J]
So the guess is: tickling needs you not to know where it's coming from. / And you always know where you're coming from. // [J]
Dave tests it anyway, eyes shut, letting his hand wander at random.
Nothing. His brain knew exactly where that hand was going. / It was the one sending it. // [J]
Worse, some tickles are completely predictable, and still work. //

# chapter: The laughing rats
In two thousand, Jaak Panksepp and Jeff Burgdorf, in Ohio, / reported what happens when you tickle a young rat.
It chirps at about fifty kilohertz, too high for human ears, / the same chirps rats make when they play.
The rats chased the hand for more.
Rats housed alone started chirping at the signal that a tickle was coming. / They knew exactly what was next, and it still worked. //
The team even bred extra-ticklish rats. / It took four generations. // [J]
In twenty sixteen, a Berlin lab filmed tickled rats doing little jumps of joy.
But put a rat on a high platform under bright light, and the laughing mostly stopped. //
Darwin noticed the same in children. / Tickled by a stranger, a small child screams. /
So tickling isn't only a feeling. / It's a game, and it needs a second player. //
But does the second player have to be a person? //
In the nineteen nineties, psychologist Christine Harris tickled people's bare feet twice: /
once by hand, and once, they were told, by a tickle machine. / They laughed at the machine just as often. //
The machine was a robotic hand from a toy store. / The tickler was a student, hiding under the table. // [J]
So you don't need to believe a person is there. / It just can't be you. //

# chapter: The copy
So Dave builds a tickle machine. / A feather on a stick, a string and a lever.
He pulls the lever. / The feather moves, his ribs feel it, and his brain shrugs. // [J]
Here's why. Whenever your brain orders a muscle to move, / it sends a copy of the order to your senses.
Biologists named it in nineteen fifty: the efference copy. / Your brain copies itself in on every order. // [J]
What you feel is what happened, minus what the copy predicted. //
For a self-tickle, that comes to almost nothing. //
In nineteen ninety-eight, Sarah-Jayne Blakemore's team at University College London / put six volunteers in a brain scanner.
Soft foam brushed each person's palm, moved by them or by the experimenter.
When they did it themselves, the brain's touch areas went quieter. / So did part of the cerebellum, / the part they think makes the prediction. //
Somewhere in there, a very small voice says: / (low) "Relax, it's just Dave." // [J]

# chapter: One fifth of a second
So how do you beat a prediction? / You make the prediction wrong. //
In nineteen ninety-nine, the same London team built the machine Dave is trying to build. // [J]
Sixteen volunteers moved a robot arm with one hand. / A second robot copied the movement, brushing foam across their other palm.
Then the scientists secretly added a delay. / A tenth of a second, two tenths, three tenths.
The longer the delay, the more it tickled. / From one fifth of a second on, it felt like the robot was doing it alone. //
Only two of the sixteen even noticed a delay. //
So Dave adds a delay box, set to one fifth of a second.
He pulls the lever. / And for the first time in his life, Dave tickles Dave. // [J]
He hasn't beaten his brain. / He's just turned himself into a stranger, / one fifth of a second late. // [J]
In twenty nineteen, a Stockholm team tried this on thirty people. /
After fifty slow strokes, their brains began to expect the delay, / and the trick started to fade. //
But touch with no delay at all began to tickle a little more. / The paper says it plainly: / it is possible to learn to tickle yourself. //
Dave's own fingers now tickle him, slightly. / He is calling it progress. // [J]

# chapter: Two eyes for an eye
And the subtraction doesn't only work on tickles. //
In two thousand three, the London team put Dave's family problem in a lab. // [J]
Six pairs of volunteers took turns pressing on each other's finger. / The rule: press exactly as hard as you were just pressed.
Every pair escalated, on average about thirty-eight percent harder each turn. /
After eight turns, the presses were more than ten times as hard as the first nudge. / Every one of them was trying to be fair. // [J]
Your own push gets the discount too. / In the same study, self-made force felt about half as strong as it was.
So you press harder to make it feel fair, / and the other person feels the full amount. //
That's Dave and his sister. / "You pushed me harder, Dave." / (low) "No, you pushed me harder." / Both of them are right. // [J]
The paper opens with exactly this: / two children, each sure the other hit harder. // [J]

# chapter: Cancelled
So that settles it, right? Your brain cancels your own touch, end of story. //
Not quite. Here's the part nobody talks about. / Your brain is constantly cancelling you out of your own life. //
Your eyes jump about three times a second. / Every jump should make the room lurch, / and it doesn't, partly because your brain subtracts its own order.
Crickets do it with sound. A male cricket chirps at around a hundred decibels, / and its ears are on its front legs.
A single nerve cell turns its hearing down on every chirp, / so the cricket doesn't deafen itself with its own song. // [J]
Your brain does the same to your voice. / In two thousand two, a San Francisco team found your brain's hearing area reacts less to your voice while you speak / than to a recording of it, just as loud. //
A recording of your voice sounds strange partly because your skull normally carries it to your ears through bone. / But while you talk, your brain is turning you down. //
You only notice the cancelling when it slips. //
In two thousand, Blakemore's team tested fifty-six people, / including patients who heard voices, / or felt their movements were controlled from outside.
For most people, their own touch felt weaker. / For the patients with those experiences, / it felt as strong as someone else's.
It seemed to follow the experiences, not the diagnosis. //
One theory, still debated, is that the same cancelling tags your inner voice as yours. / When the tag slips, your own thoughts can sound like someone else's. //
If that's right, it isn't a failure of character. / It's a subtraction that didn't happen. //

# chapter: It's just you
So Dave's brain isn't against him. / It's how he knows which hand is his. //
Dave gives up on immunity. / On Sunday, his sister goes for the armpit, / and Dave laughs until he loses the argument.
Turns out Dave was already immune / to exactly one person. //
(slow) So, one more time. / Wiggle your fingertips lightly across your palm. //
(slow) Nothing. That nothing is your brain, cancelling you out, / the way it has every second of your life. //
(slow) Which is the brain's way of saying: / relax, it's just you.
