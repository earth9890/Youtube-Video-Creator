# chapter: A letter to the future
This is Dave. Dave has written a letter to his descendants, to be opened in one point three million years. / That's about forty-three thousand generations, so the envelope needs to be waterproof. / It also needs a forwarding address. By then the Solar System will have travelled about a thousand light-years around the galaxy. // [J]
"Dear grandchildren, a star is coming. Please bring a telescope and some snacks." (@dave) // [J]
So here is the question. What if a star passed through our Solar System? Not past it. Through it. Would the planets get flung about? Would the sky change? /
Tonight we look at the last one we know of, seventy thousand years ago, and the next one on the schedule, and we ask Dave's great-great-and-so-on grandchildren what they'll see. /

# chapter: The garden
It helps to know how the Solar System is laid out. The nearest star to the Sun, Proxima Centauri, is about four point two light-years away. /
But the Sun's family doesn't stop at Neptune. NASA says the Oort cloud, a theorised shell of icy bodies, starts about two to five thousand times the Earth-Sun distance out. / Its outer edge is somewhere between ten thousand and a hundred thousand. //
A hundred thousand Earth-Sun distances is about one and a half light-years. / Voyager one won't reach the cloud for about three hundred years, and won't leave it for maybe thirty thousand. /
So the Sun's garden is huge, and almost empty. A passing star doesn't hit the planets. It wanders through the far end of the lawn. // [J]
"So it's a very big garden, and mostly empty?" (@dave) //
"Mostly empty, Dave. Very mostly empty." (@brain) // [J]
And it happens a lot. Gaia, ESA's star-mapping satellite, lets us count. A star passes within about six and a half light-years of the Sun roughly every twelve thousand years. /
Most of those are strangers who never come closer. A few are neighbours who really do cut through the garden. /
In twenty twenty-two, Coryn Bailer-Jones counted sixty-one stars in Gaia's third release that formally pass within one parsec, about three light-years. / Only forty-two are high-confidence. The other nineteen are probably ghosts. // [J]

# chapter: Seventy thousand years ago
The last close visitor is a red dwarf and a brown dwarf, together called Scholz's Star, weighing only about fifteen percent as much as the Sun. / In twenty thirteen, Ralf-Dieter Scholz spotted it as a faint nearby object. / In twenty fifteen, Eric Mamajek's team ran its path backwards. //
About seventy thousand years ago, it passed within about fifty-two thousand times the Earth-Sun distance. That's a little under one light-year, about five times closer than Proxima is today. /
There was a ninety-eight percent chance that it passed through the outer Oort cloud, and about a one in ten thousand chance that it reached the inner part. /
Nobody on Earth would have noticed. At its closest it would have been about magnitude eleven point four, far too faint for the naked eye. //
So a star strolled through the Sun's garden, and left the gate open. We only found the footprints in twenty fifteen. // [J]
Later measurements nudge it a bit further away, and a bit older, about eighty thousand years. Either way, nothing fell on anybody. //

# chapter: Gliese seven-ten
The next big visitor is Gliese seven-ten, an orange dwarf about sixty-two light-years away, with about six-tenths of the Sun's mass. It's small, cool and orange. Dave likes it already. // [J]
Before Gaia, nobody could say how close it would come. / Gaia measures how a star moves across the sky, and how fast it moves toward or away from us. Run that motion forward, with the Sun's, and you get a path. /
The old estimate for Gliese seven-ten's distance ran from three trillion to fourteen trillion kilometres. A range of better than four to one. /
In twenty sixteen, the first Gaia data tightened it dramatically: about two point three trillion kilometres, or about sixteen thousand Earth-Sun distances, in about one point three million years. /
It will be slow for a star, nearly fifty thousand kilometres an hour. Dave, naturally, would like to know if it can be made to hurry. // [J]
Even at that speed, a star needs tens of thousands of years to cross the Oort cloud. This is a very slow visit. A very long coffee, as visits go. // [J]
Since then, the estimates keep shuffling. / In twenty twenty-two, Gaia's third release put it somewhere between ten and fourteen thousand Earth-Sun distances, depending on who ran the numbers. Astronomers call that agreement. // [J]

Same date, give or take. The star hasn't changed. Our measurements have. So Dave's letter needs a footnote. // [J]

# chapter: The view from the garden
So what will Dave's grandchildren see? Today, Gliese seven-ten is far too faint to see without a telescope, about magnitude nine point seven. /
At its closest, it will reach about magnitude minus two point seven, as bright as the brightest planets. / That's brighter than Sirius, which is the brightest star in our sky now. / And with it moving so slowly, it should stay brighter than Sirius for several thousand years either side. /
It will be orange, and it will creep. Its motion peaks near one arcminute a year. / The Moon is about thirty-one arcminutes wide, so every thirty years or so, the star slides a whole Moon's width. // [J]
A star that moves a Moon's width in a lifetime is, to an astronomer, practically sprinting. /
And will anyone be there to watch? Our species is about three hundred thousand years old. Dave's letter has to outlast more than four times that. // [J]
As for the planets, studies of the Kuiper belt and the Pluto system find the effects negligible. Earth won't notice at all. //
"That's it? A very bright orange dot?" (@dave) //
"That's the planets, Dave. The comets are another story." (@brain) // [J]

# chapter: The comets
A star's real reach is the Oort cloud. It's where long-period comets come from, and many of them have never been seen by human eyes. /
ESA says a slow passage gives a star plenty of time to act on those icy bodies, potentially sending showers of comets into the Solar System. /
But ESA adds a caution. A passing star doesn't necessarily disturb comets, and Earth might not be in the path of any that do come in. //
A team at the University of Vienna simulated it with three point six million test comets, and tried passes as close as twelve hundred Earth-Sun distances. /
A small number get scattered into the planets' region, and they arrive a long time after the star has gone. / Comets from the Oort cloud fall slowly, because they start so far away. Nobody out there is in a hurry. // [J]
So there's no comet storm on the night. If Dave's grandchildren want one, they'd better wait up. // [J]

# chapter: The real culprit
Here's the part nobody mentions. The star isn't the main thing moving comets. The Milky Way is. /
In nineteen eighty-six, Douglas Heisler and Scott Tremaine worked out that the galaxy's own tidal pull knocks comets out of the cloud / about one and a half to two times as fast as passing stars do. /
A star is a single shove. The tide is a constant, gentle tug. On average, over the long run, the tug wins. // [J]
In twenty fifteen, Fabo Feng and Coryn Bailer-Jones simulated sixty-one known encounters. Past stars, they found, injected at least five percent of the long-period comets we know. /
The tide plays the dominant role. / And Gliese seven-ten, the strongest encounter on the future list, would add at most eight percent to the comet flux. About one extra comet in twelve. //

"It's a very nice envelope, Dave." (@brain) // [J]

# chapter: The postscript
So what if a star passed through our Solar System? For one like Gliese seven-ten, the sky gets a new brightest star, for thousands of years. The planets carry on. /
A few comets are nudged inwards, arriving long after it has left. And the Milky Way keeps doing most of the work, as it always has. //
Nothing on Gaia's list comes anywhere near the planets, and Gliese seven-ten is the closest of them all. It's mostly a very good view. /
