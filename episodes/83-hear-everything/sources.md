# Episode 83 "What If You Could Hear 100 Times Better?": sources (verified 2026-10-22)

Dave, Theo, Ivy, Mr Pike, the leaf blower, the saucepans and the smoke alarm are story devices, not science claims. Everything the narrator or the cast states as fact is below. **Solid** = published source. **Inference** = our reasoning from a solid fact.

| Script line | Claim | Exact quote / number | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| "Zero decibels is the faintest sound a healthy young ear can hear." (Brain) | Solid (zero is the reference level near the best-hearing frequencies; thresholds are higher at other frequencies). | "The decibel scale is a logarithmic scale in which 0 dB approximates the threshold of hearing in the middle frequencies for young adults." NBK207834: "zero (0) dB SPL means that the sound pressure level is 20 micropascals". | CAOHC glossary: https://www.caohc.org/pdfs/REF%20GLOSSARY.pdf ; National Academies, "Basics of Sound, the Ear, and Hearing": https://ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK207834 |
| "Every ten decibels is ten times the energy. A hundred times better is minus twenty." | Solid definition + **our arithmetic**: dB = 10 x log10(I / Iref), so a hundred times the sensitivity in energy (intensity) is 10 x log10(100) = 20 dB lower. In pressure terms that is ten times smaller. The script says "a hundred times fainter" as the rule of the story. | "dB = 10*log10(I/Iref) or 20*log10(p/pref), where I is sound intensity, p is sound pressure". | NBK207834 (above) |
| "The quietest room ever built is about twenty decibels below the faintest sound we can hear." | Solid. Record = a lab chamber, measured -20.35 dBA (Guinness, 2015); script does not name the company. | "an average background noise reading of an unimaginably quiet -20.35 dBA"; CNN: "a measured background noise of -20.35 dBA, 20 decibels below the threshold of human hearing." | Guinness World Records: https://www.guinnessworldrecords.com/news/2015/10/microsoft-lab-sets-new-record-for-the-worlds-quietest-place-399444 ; CNN: https://www.cnn.com/style/article/anechoic-chamber-worlds-quietest-room |
| "You can hear your heartbeat. Your breathing sounds like a gale. When you turn your head, you can hear that too." | Solid (reports from the quietest rooms; "like a gale" is the narrator's exaggeration of "somewhat loud"). | CNN: "If you stand in it for long enough, you start to hear your heartbeat." The chamber's designer, Hundraj Gopal: "When you turn your head, you can hear that motion. You can hear yourself breathing and it sounds somewhat loud". | CNN (above) |
| "When I stand up, my knees sound like a gate." / "Your joints are filing a complaint." | Joke from the fact. | CNN: "When you move, your bones make a grinding noise." | CNN (above) |
| "Some [bat] calls reach a hundred and twenty decibels, louder than a smoke alarm held to your ear." | Solid. Other sources say open-space bats exceed this (up to about 140 dB at 10 cm), so "some calls reach" is conservative. | "bats emit calls as low as 50 dB and as high as 120 dB, which is louder than a smoke detector 10 centimeters from your ear." / "call intensities can reach up to 140 dB SPL at a distance of 0.1 m from the bat's mouth". | Scientific American, "How do bats echolocate...": https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/how-do-bats-echolocate-an/ ; Lattenkamp et al. 2021, Proc R Soc B: https://royalsocietypublishing.org/rspb/article/288/1942/20202600/85993/ |
| "Bat calls are ultrasonic, above twenty kilohertz." | Solid (usually; some species' calls reach audible range, e.g. spotted bat clicks). | "Echolocation calls are usually ultrasonic--ranging in frequency from 20 to 200 kilohertz (kHz), whereas human hearing normally tops out at around 20 kHz." | Scientific American (above) |
| "Your quiet end moved. Your range did not." | Story rule (in the rule card): only sensitivity at the quiet end changes. | n/a | n/a |
| "That hiss is air molecules bumping your eardrum. It sits near minus twenty-three decibels." (doctor) | Solid with a range: estimates run from about -20 to -24 dB SPL depending on bandwidth; -23 is Microsoft's and Howard and Angus's figure. | Microsoft Building 87 page: "Brownian Motion -23dB: The quietest sound theorized by mathematicians is Brownian motion: the movement of particles in a gas or liquid." Howard and Angus (via a physics Q&A): "the pressure amplitude variations caused by the Brownian motion of air molecules, at room temperature and over a critical bandwidth, correspond to a sound pressure level of about -23 dB." Geisler: "pressure fluctuations due to the Brownian motion of air molecules impinging on the eardrum, are about 2 uPa (-20 dB SPL)". | Microsoft News, Inside B87: https://news.microsoft.com/stories/building87/audio-lab.php ; https://physics.stackexchange.com/questions/110540/how-loud-is-the-thermal-motion-of-air-molecules (quoting Howard & Angus, Geisler, Dallos, Harris 1968) |
| "In nineteen thirty-three, two Bell Labs engineers said a better ear would be useless, because that noise would drown it out." | Solid as a historical statement. **Contested** since: Green & Dai (1991) argue human threshold is not limited by Brownian motion (about 5000 times above it at 1 kHz, 9 dB SPL). The script states what Sivian and White said, and Dave at minus twenty would be at the wall, which fits both views. | Yost 2021 quoting Sivian and White (Bell Telephone Laboratories): "For exceptionally good ears, a further increase in physiological sensitivity would be useless in the presence of thermal noise". Green & Dai: "the fundamental limitation is not the thermal agitation of molecules of the air." | Yost, "Do I have a hearing loss?", JASA 150:R7 (2021): https://pubs.aip.org/asa/jasa/article/150/4/R7/997448/ ; Sivian & White, JASA 4:288 (1933): https://doi.org/10.1121/1.1915608 ; Green & Dai: https://doi.org/10.1121/1.2029394 |
| "Eighty-five decibels for eight hours is the limit. Every three decibels more halves the time." (Brain) | Solid (NIOSH recommendation). | "NIOSH established a recommended exposure limit (REL) of 85 A-weighted decibels (dBA) averaged over an eight-hour workday... For each 3 dBA increase in noise level, NIOSH recommends reducing the exposure duration by half." | CDC/NIOSH: https://cdc.gov/niosh/noise/about/noise.html |
| "Real hearing damage is permanent." | Solid. | "Occupational hearing loss is permanent, but it is 100% preventable." | CDC/NIOSH Science Bulletin: https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/bulletin/2022/save-your-hearing-day.html |
| "Your ears are not sturdier. They only hear fainter things." | Story rule (rule card). | n/a | n/a |

| "It is the best silence you have ever heard." (after the battery) | Story. The hiss is the "real silence" for Dave; no claim. | n/a | n/a |

## Checked and NOT used
- "The longest anyone can bear the quietest room is 45 minutes": widely repeated; the New York Times piece treats it as speculation ("wild and terrible speculation"). Not used.
- Orfield Laboratories' later -24.9 dBA measurement (Indian Express): not verified against Guinness; the script says only "about twenty decibels below".
- Barn-owl hearing: used in episode 82; kept out of this one on purpose.
- Baby heart rate in beats per minute: not stated; the script says only "very small and very fast".
