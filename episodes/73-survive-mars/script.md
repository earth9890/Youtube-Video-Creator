# chapter: One Martian year
This is Dave. Dave has stepped out onto Mars in a T-shirt and shorts, holding a stopwatch. / His goal is to stay one full Martian year: six hundred and eighty-seven days. // [J]
"It's just a desert. How bad can it be?" (@dave) //
"It's a desert at fifty-nine below zero, Dave. And the cold isn't even the main problem." (@brain) // [J]
So here is the question. How long could you survive on Mars? / Tonight Dave tries to stay for a year, and each time he fails we hand him one more thing. /
A suit, then oxygen, warmth, water, food, and company. / By the end, there's one thing left that nobody can ship, grow or dig up. This is a thought experiment. Please don't try it in shorts. //

# chapter: The T-shirt
Mars has air, but not much of it. It's about six-tenths of one percent of Earth's, about six millibars. /
There's a line on Earth, around nineteen kilometres up, called the Armstrong limit. Above it, the pressure is so low that water boils at body temperature. / Mars is about ten times thinner than that. /
So the first thing to go in Dave's T-shirt is his spit. As saliva absorbs heat, it boils into vapour and is carried away with the escaping air. // [J]
He does not explode, whatever the films say. He might swell a little. But a person in near-vacuum stays conscious for only ten to fifteen seconds. /
We know roughly how long from a real accident. / In nineteen sixty-five, a man testing a pressure suit in a NASA vacuum chamber lost suit pressure, and passed out after about fourteen seconds. /
His last memory was of the saliva on his tongue starting to boil. He was put back under pressure quickly, and was fine. // [J]
Dave's stopwatch reads fifteen seconds. The cold hadn't even started. //

# chapter: Air
So Dave gets a suit. That fixes the pressure. Now the air. /
NASA's figures say Mars's atmosphere is about ninety-five percent carbon dioxide. Oxygen is sixteen hundredths of one percent. Earth's is about twenty-one, so about a hundred and thirty times less. /
The carbon dioxide is roughly what Dave breathes out, so he's walking round inside a very big, very cold exhale. // [J]
He could try breathing it, but he'd only get the same fifteen seconds again, with more carbon dioxide in them. // [J]

Dave needs to make his own, and we have exactly one machine that has tried. It's called MOXIE, and it rode on the Perseverance rover. /
Between two thousand twenty-one and two thousand twenty-three, it made oxygen from Martian air sixteen times. In total, a hundred and twenty-two grams. /
At its best, it made twelve grams an hour. / NASA says its very first run, five grams, was about ten minutes of breathing for an astronaut. So the grand total is nearly four hours of Dave. // [J]
It's a toaster-sized experiment, and it worked better than planned. But it's a start, not a lungful. / The principal investigator says astronauts spending a year on the surface might use about one metric ton between them. Mostly, the oxygen is needed to launch the rocket home. //
"Four hours of oxygen, and then what happens to me?" (@dave) //
"Then you build a bigger toaster, Dave." (@brain) // [J]

# chapter: Warm and wet
Next: warmth. The average temperature on Mars is about fifty-nine below zero. At night, one lander measured a low of eighty-nine below. /
So Dave doesn't just need a suit. He needs a sealed home, with a heater, and a power supply that works every night. /
That's harder than it sounds. In twenty eighteen, a planet-wide dust storm silenced the solar-powered Opportunity rover for good. The nuclear-powered Curiosity carried on. // [J]
Gravity helps a little. It's about thirty-eight percent of Earth's, so Dave can jump high, and nobody knows what a year of it does to his bones. /
His days are longer, too: a Martian day is about forty minutes longer than ours, so his watch is nearly five hours out within a week. // [J]
Then water. Dave can't carry a year's worth. But Mars has ice. / In twenty eighteen, Colin Dundas's team found deposits more than a hundred metres thick, starting only one or two metres down, in places around fifty-five degrees north and south. /
NASA's Phoenix lander found ice even closer, about five centimetres below the surface, near the north pole. So Dave digs. /
The soil comes with a salt called perchlorate, a few tenths of a percent of it. JPL says it could be useful for rocket fuel. / Dave would rather it wasn't in his tea. // [J]

# chapter: Dinner
Then food. NASA's planning figure is one point eight three kilograms of food per person per day. For Dave's whole year, that's about twelve hundred and fifty kilograms. /
And here's the catch. NASA's food scientists found that only seven out of sixty-five heat-sterilised foods are expected to taste acceptable after five years. // [J]
"I packed sixty-five foods for the trip. Surely they'll be fine." (@dave) /
"Seven of them will be, Dave. Choose carefully." (@brain) // [J]
So Dave plants something instead. In twenty sixteen, a Dutch team at Wageningen grew ten crops in a soil copy that matches Mars's. /
Four of them, radishes, peas, rye and tomatoes, were tested, and none held dangerous levels of metals. /
That's real. But it's a copy of the dirt, not the dirt itself. Nobody has farmed the real thing. //
Dave's checklist now says: suit, oxygen, warmth, water, food. He ticks them proudly. He's alone in a very small house. // [J]

# chapter: The box
Surely the rest is easy? We already know how people cope with small boxes. /
In twenty ten, six volunteers shut themselves in a mock spaceship in Moscow for five hundred and twenty days. / Four of the six had considerable sleep problems, and the crew grew steadily less active. /
In twenty twenty-four, NASA's four volunteers walked out of a mock Mars house in Houston after three hundred and seventy-eight days. They'd grown vegetables, and done pretend Mars walks. /
They came out fine. But a pretend Mars has no Mars on top of it. // [J]
"Four of six couldn't sleep, in Moscow. I'll bring a better pillow." (@dave) // [J]
"So nothing is left? Just me, my toaster, and some radishes?" (@dave) //
"One thing, Dave. It's the easy part." (@brain) // [J]

# chapter: The thing nobody can ship
Mars lost its global magnetic field billions of years ago, and its air is thin. So the rays that hit it from space mostly just arrive. //
Curiosity carried a radiation detector. On the ground it measured about point six four of a thousandth of a sievert, a millisievert, every day. /
On the trip there, the same instrument measured one point eight millisieverts a day. / The researchers worked out that the trip alone, there and back, would cost about two thirds of a sievert. /
NASA's career limit for an astronaut is six hundred millisieverts. That's point six of a sievert. // [J]
For comparison, the average American gets about three millisieverts a year, from everything. One day of Dave's trip is more than seven months of that. //
So Dave hasn't even landed, and he's already overdrawn. //
Add six hundred and eighty-seven days on the ground, about four hundred and forty millisieverts by our arithmetic. / NASA's own template mission, three hundred and sixty days of travel and five hundred on the surface, / comes out at about one sievert, roughly ten times what an astronaut gets in six months on the space station. /
And the limit isn't a cliff. It's built from cancer risk: for the most vulnerable astronauts, it works out to a three percent extra chance of dying of cancer. /
That estimate assumes today's rockets. A faster trip would trim the bill, but nobody has built one yet. //
The air, water and food can be made, dug or grown. The dose has to be dodged. Dave can't grow a magnetic field. //

# chapter: The stopwatch
So how long could you survive on Mars? In a T-shirt, about fifteen seconds. /
With a suit, oxygen, warmth, water and food, as long as they hold, which could be years. /
But the clock that decides how long you should stay isn't on Mars. It started on the way there. /
That doesn't make Mars impossible. It makes it expensive, in a currency nobody can print. //
