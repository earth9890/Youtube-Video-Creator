# Episode 23 "You Are Not Weird": sources (verified 2026-10-15)

| Script line | What the source says | Source |
|---|---|---|
| "At parties, he stands in the corner, sure he's the weird one. Everyone else looks normal. Especially Kevin." | Story frame (Dave and Kevin are fictional). It sets up the situation the 1993 study describes: people's public behaviour looks the same, so each person assumes the others privately feel fine. | Schroeder & Prentice 1998, p. 2152 (see below): "They assume that their peers hold private views that are much more consistent with the drinking norm than are their own." |
| "In nineteen fifty, the Air Force's Gilbert Daniels measured four thousand and sixty-three pilots, to find the normal one." | The data come from the Air Force Anthropometric Survey of 1950; Daniels' technical note uses "the full sample of 4063 men" and asks whether an "average man" exists to design for. Daniels was a first lieutenant at the Aero Medical Laboratory, Wright Air Development Center. | Daniels G.S., "The 'Average Man'?", Technical Note WCRD 53-7, Wright Air Development Center, December 1952: https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/tr/pdf/AD0010203.pdf (text: https://archive.org/stream/DTIC_AD0010203/DTIC_AD0010203_djvu.txt) |
| "Average meant the middle thirty percent. Generous." | Daniels defined "approximately average" as plus or minus 0.3 standard deviations of the mean, "approximately the middle 30% of the total population" on each dimension. | Daniels 1952, as above |
| "Pilots average on all ten body sizes: zero." | Ten dimensions most important for clothing/cockpit design were applied one after another. After step 3 only 143 men were still average; "at the end of 10 steps there was not a single individual remaining who fell within the 'average' range for all measurements." | Daniels 1952, as above; summary: 99% Invisible, "On Average": https://99percentinvisible.org/episode/on-average/ |
| "The cockpit was built for a man who didn't exist." | Daniels' conclusion: designing for the "average man" designs for a person who practically does not exist. Todd Rose's account: cockpits had been standardised to average pilot dimensions; after Daniels, the design fit nobody. | Daniels 1952; Rose T., *The End of Average* (HarperOne, 2016), ch. 1; 99% Invisible, as above |
| "So the Air Force made the seats adjustable." | The Air Force adopted individual fit: adjustable seats, foot pedals, helmet straps and flight suits. | Rose 2016; Kirkus review of *The End of Average*: https://www.kirkusreviews.com/book-reviews/todd-rose/the-end-of-average/ ; WGBH interview with Todd Rose, 2016-02-16: https://www.wgbh.org/news/2016-02-16/standards-grades-and-tests-are-wildly-outdated-argues-end-of-average |
| "In nineteen ninety-three, Deborah Prentice asked Princeton students: how comfortable are you with campus drinking? And the average student?" | Four studies of Princeton undergraduates rated their own comfort with campus alcohol practices and the average student's comfort (11-point scales). The paper is by Deborah Prentice and Dale Miller; the script names the first author only for length. | Prentice D.A. & Miller D.T. (1993), "Pluralistic ignorance and alcohol use on campus: Some consequences of misperceiving the social norm", *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 64(2):243-256, doi:10.1037/0022-3514.64.2.243: https://collaborate.princeton.edu/en/publications/pluralistic-ignorance-and-alcohol-use-on-campus-some-consequences/ |
| "Students said: less comfortable than average. A neat trick. They were the average student." | Abstract: "Students believed that they were more uncomfortable with campus alcohol practices than was the average student." Since the real average student's comfort is the average of the students' own answers, the gap is a misperception, which is the paper's point (pluralistic ignorance). Replicated in Schroeder & Prentice 1998 with freshmen (September means: own comfort 6.98, average student 7.76). | Prentice & Miller 1993; Schroeder & Prentice 1998, p. 2165 |
| "Everyone hid it, so everyone felt weird." | Students' public behaviour is identical while their private attitudes differ; each assumes the others' private views match the norm. In Study 4, students who (wrongly) saw themselves as deviant from the norm showed more alienation from campus. | Prentice & Miller 1993 abstract; Schroeder & Prentice 1998, pp. 2152-2153 |
| "Kevin, laughing too loud, thinks Dave looks relaxed." | Story illustration of the same finding: each person overestimates how comfortable the others are. Kevin is fictional; no claim about any individual. | As above |
| "No normal person. Just a room of people copying someone who isn't there." | Pluralistic ignorance: people conform to a norm that most of them privately do not hold. In Prentice & Miller Study 3, male students shifted their own attitudes over a semester toward the norm they mistakenly perceived. | Prentice & Miller 1993; Schroeder & Prentice 1998, p. 2153 |
| "When Princeton freshmen talked it out, the fake normal lost its grip." | 452 Princeton first-years joined a 1-hour discussion in the first week of term. The peer-oriented discussion exposed pluralistic ignorance (how students' private comfort differs from what they assume of others). Four to six months later (143 follow-ups), they reported drinking significantly less than students given an individual-oriented decision-making discussion (3.1 vs 5.1 drinks per week, Table 1), and "the peer-oriented discussion reduced the prescriptive strength of the drinking norm." | Schroeder C.M. & Prentice D.A. (1998), "Exposing pluralistic ignorance to reduce alcohol use among college students", *Journal of Applied Social Psychology* 28(23):2150-2180: https://sparq.stanford.edu/sites/g/files/sbiybj19021/files/media/file/schroeder_prentice_1998_-_exposing_pluralistic_ignorance.pdf |
| "So Dave turns to Kevin: 'Is it just me?'" | The one safe action: say the private feeling out loud to one person. Backed by the mechanism above (the misperception lives on because private views stay hidden; making them public weakened the false norm). No number is promised, and the script does not claim the other person will always agree. | As above |

## Checked and NOT used
- **"Norma" statue and look-alike contest (1945):** Belskie and Dickinson's 1943 alabaster statue of the "average" young American woman, built from measurement data. The Cleveland Health Museum's Plain Dealer contest drew 3,863 entries (Cabinet magazine) or 3,864 (Todd Rose); the winner was a 23-year-old theatre cashier, and Rose reports none matched all nine dimensions. Good, but it makes the same point as the pilots with weaker primary sourcing. https://cabinetmagazine.org/issues/15/cambers.php
- **"17 pilots crashed in a single day" / "pilot error":** appears in Rose's retelling but I could not find a primary record, so the script does not mention crashes.
- **"Less than 5 % average on 3 dimensions":** the primary number is 143 of 4,063 (3.5 %); left out for length.
- **Miller & McFarland (1987), the confusing-article study:** pluralistic ignorance about not understanding a text. Too close to ep16's "dumb" theme.
- **Spotlight effect and the liking gap:** already used in episode 18. Not used.
- **Exact 1993 means:** I could not read the 1993 tables directly, so the script states only the direction of the gap; the means quoted above are from the 1998 replication.
