# chapter: The doorstep plan
This is Dave. On Friday, Dave has a first date, / and he has already planned the ending. //
At the front door, he will offer his date / a firm, friendly, completely safe handshake. // [J]
Because on Tuesday, Dave read one number, and he hasn't recovered. Eighty million. //
When you think about it, pressing your face against somebody else's face is a strange thing to do. / Yet people everywhere seem to do it. //
So, why do humans kiss? Is it built into us, / or did somebody, somewhere, just make it up? //
Because if it's built in, Dave is going to have to do it. //
The answer goes through chimpanzees, baby food and clay tablets, / and one fact that should make Dave feel much better. //
Let's start with the apes, and a fight at a zoo. //

# chapter: The apes did it first
November, nineteen seventy-five, Burgers Zoo in Arnhem, in the Netherlands. /
The top chimpanzee in the colony attacks a female. / The whole hall erupts, then goes strangely quiet. //
Then hooting and drumming, and in the middle of the noise, two chimps kiss and hug. /
Hours later, a young scientist called Frans de Waal realises they're the same two who were fighting. / In his diary, he writes the Dutch word for reconciliation. //
The kiss that started a whole field of research / wasn't romance. It was an apology. // [J]
In twenty twenty-five, an Oxford team led by Matilda Brindle mapped kissing onto the primate family tree. /
Their best estimate: it began in an ancestor of the great apes, / between twenty-one and seventeen million years ago. //
Chimps kiss, bonobos kiss, orangutans kiss, and Neanderthals probably did too. / Eastern gorillas, so far, have never been caught at it. // [J]
Even Charles Darwin, in eighteen seventy-two, wrote about two chimps meeting / with pushed-out lips, a hug, and a yell of delight. //
Dave hears all this and reaches a conclusion. / "Twenty million years of kissing. So I have to." // [J]
But for an ape, a kiss can mean many things. Making up, saying hello, calming a baby. / So where did the romantic part come from? //

# chapter: Was the first kiss lunch?
Theory one: in the nineteen seventies, the Austrian scientist Irenaus Eibl-Eibesfeldt suggested / that the very first kiss was lunch. // [J]
Before baby food came in jars, mothers chewed food soft / and passed it to the baby, mouth to mouth. //
A Cornell team found it in thirty-eight of a hundred and nineteen cultures with notes on baby feeding. /
At one Beijing university, sixty-three percent of the students asked had been fed pre-chewed food as babies. //
Dave has decided not to ask his mum about this. // [J]
Theory two, from Adriano Lameira at Warwick in twenty twenty-four, is grooming. / When a great ape grooms a friend, it often finishes with a little pucker, / sucking the last bit of dirt off the fur. //
Our ancestors lost the fur. They kept the pucker. // [J]
So which is right? Oxford psychologist Robin Dunbar gave an honest answer. / "I am not sure we have anything close to an explanation." // [J]
But we can at least find out when people started writing it down. //

# chapter: The oldest written kiss
For years, the oldest written romantic kiss was thought to be Indian, about three and a half thousand years old. /
Then in twenty twenty-three, Assyriologist Troels Pank Arboll and zoologist Sophie Lund Rasmussen / pushed it back a thousand years, in the journal Science. //
Mesopotamia, in what is now Iraq and Syria, about four and a half thousand years ago. / Kisses, written into clay tablets in cuneiform. //
Lovers kissed, parents kissed their children, friends kissed. / But in the Akkadian texts, most kisses weren't on anyone's lips. / People kissed the ground before the gods, and the feet of kings. // [J]
In one Sumerian story, the hero Gilgamesh meets a monster called Huwawa, / makes as if to kiss him, / and punches him on the cheek. // [J]
And one old Sumerian proverb may complain about couples kissing on an open street. / Kissing in public has been frowned upon since the Bronze Age. // [J]
Arboll's conclusion: kissing didn't start in one place and spread. / It turned up in many ancient cultures, over thousands of years. //
Dave reads that twice, and stays completely fine. / "So it's ancient, and it's everywhere. I definitely have to." // [J]
So if it's everywhere, what is it for? //

# chapter: The taste test
In twenty thirteen, Rafael Wlodarski and Robin Dunbar asked nine hundred and two adults what kissing is for. /
Two answers came out on top. Checking out a new partner, / and keeping an existing one close. //
So a first kiss isn't only a kiss. It's a taste test. // [J]
In one small survey by Gordon Gallup, / fifty-nine percent of men and sixty-six percent of women / said they'd liked someone, kissed them, and gone right off them. // [J]
Dave is fine. Dave now practises the handshake twice a day. // [J]
Which brings us back to Tuesday's number. / In twenty fourteen, Dutch scientists led by Remco Kort / published a kissing experiment on twenty-one couples visiting Amsterdam's zoo. //
A zoo again. This time, the apes being studied were the visitors. // [J]
One partner drank a yoghurt drink full of easy-to-spot bacteria, then they kissed for ten seconds. /
The scientists worked out what had moved across. / About eighty million bacteria, in one kiss. // [J]
(fast) Also, in the same couples, the men reported about ten kisses a day, / and the women about five. Somebody is counting wrong. // [J]
Then Dave finds a study from Wales. /
The same year, scientists there dipped a gloved hand in harmless bacteria and shook another. / A handshake passed on nearly twice as many as a high five, / and a fist bump the fewest. //
The handshake is not the clean option. Dave switches to a fist bump. // [J]

# chapter: Left or right?
Dave's last hope: if a kiss does happen, he can at least get the angle right. /
In two thousand three, psychologist Onur Gunturkun watched kissing couples / at airports, stations, beaches and parks in the United States, Germany and Turkey. //
Out of a hundred and twenty-four couples, / eighty turned their heads to the right, about two in three. //
His idea: it's the same right turn babies make in their last weeks in the womb, / and in their first six months. //
And the paper, in the journal Nature, contains this exact sentence. / It takes two people to kiss. // [J]
So Dave practises a right turn and a fist bump together, in the mirror. / It looks exactly like the Gilgamesh move. // [J]

# chapter: Only forty-six percent
For decades, scientists repeated the same number. / Romantic kissing happens in more than ninety percent of human cultures. //
In twenty fifteen, anthropologists William Jankowiak, Shelly Volsche and Justin Garcia checked a hundred and sixty-eight cultures. / Romantic kissing turned up in forty-six percent. Less than half. //
The ninety percent seems to have counted every kind of kiss, mostly parents and children. / Most hunter-gatherer groups in the sample showed no sign of it. //
When the Thonga people of southern Africa first saw Europeans kissing, they laughed. / "Look at these people. They eat each other's saliva and dirt." // [J]
In Melanesia, the Trobriand Islanders reportedly called kissing / "a rather insipid and silly form of amusement." // [J]
And remember Darwin's kissing chimps? On the very next page, he wrote this. / "We Europeans are so accustomed to kissing / that it might be thought to be innate in mankind, / but this is not the case." //
Here's what everyone missed. Who writes on clay tablets? / Cities, with kings, priests and scribes. //
And Jankowiak found that the more social classes a society had, the more likely it was to kiss. / So the oldest tablets were written by exactly the kind of people who kiss. //
So, why do humans kiss? Partly because the urge to press close is ancient. / Apes kiss to make up. Parents almost everywhere kiss their children. //
But the romantic kiss looks like a habit about half the world's cultures picked up, / and the other half never bothered with. //

# chapter: The other half was fine
Here's the part that should help Dave. The other half was fine. /
Back in nineteen ninety-two, Jankowiak and Edward Fischer checked a hundred and sixty-six cultures for romantic love itself, / love songs, love stories, the agony of a crush. //
They found it in a hundred and forty-seven. Love, nearly everywhere. / The kiss, in less than half. //
So plenty of couples seem to have fallen in love without a single romantic kiss. / They had other gestures, like the Maori hongi, a gentle pressing of noses. //
Friday. The front door. Dave has a fist bump, a handshake and a right turn ready. /
His date looks at Dave's outstretched hand, / shakes it, and then kisses him on the cheek anyway. //
Dave turns right, like two in three kissing couples. / Not because he has to. Because he'd like to. //
(slow) Half the cultures on Earth would have stopped at the handshake, / and everyone would have been fine. //
(slow) Dave didn't stop. That's fine too.
