# Episode 24 "You Are Not Too Old": sources (verified 2026-10-15)

| "This is Dave. Forty, and he just bought his first guitar. He kept the receipt. Just in case." / "Dave threw away the receipt." | Dave, his guitar and the receipt are invented story devices, not study data. | n/a |
| "Then he read: the brain peaks at twenty-five." | What Dave *read*: a popular belief, which the next beats test. The script does not claim it is a finding; the Hartshorne & Germine data below show there is no single peak age. | See the Hartshorne & Germine rows |
| "Dave's comfort: 'Even Colonel Sanders started KFC at sixty-five.'" / "He didn't. He was selling chicken from a petrol station at forty." | A viral meme says Sanders was a penniless 65-year-old retiree who started KFC with a $105 government cheque. AAP FactCheck rated it **False**: "Sanders started selling hot meals as a side business from a service station in 1930 when he was 40", and "cooking was not something Sanders started after his 'retirement' at age 65". He taught Pete Harman his chicken-frying process in 1952, aged 62. Wikipedia: in 1930 he began serving chicken dishes and other meals at a Shell service station in North Corbin, Kentucky; born 9 September 1890 (so 39-40 in 1930); finalised the pressure-fryer recipe in 1940; sold the Corbin restaurant at 65 after Interstate 75 bypassed it, then franchised full time. | AAP FactCheck, "Barely a nugget of truth in Colonel Sanders rags-to-riches meme", 19 Oct 2021: https://www.aap.com.au/factcheck/barely-a-nugget-of-truth-in-colonel-sanders-rags-to-riches-meme/ · Wikipedia, Colonel Sanders: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colonel_Sanders |
| "Dave's late bloomer started at Dave's age." | Sanders was about 40 in 1930 (above); Dave is 40. | As above |
| "In twenty fifteen, Joshua Hartshorne and Laura Germine tested nearly fifty thousand people." | 48,537 people took online tests (testmybrain.org, gameswithwords.org), checked against Wechsler (WAIS/WMS) norm data. | Hartshorne J.K., Germine L.T., "When does cognitive functioning peak? The asynchronous rise and fall of different cognitive abilities across the life span", *Psychological Science* 2015;26(4):433-443, doi 10.1177/0956797614567339 · MIT News / BCS, "The rise and fall of cognitive skills" (Anne Trafton, March 2015): https://bcs.mit.edu/news/rise-and-fall-cognitive-skills · Scientific American blog (48,537 participants): https://www.scientificamerican.com/blog/beautiful-minds/when-does-intelligence-peak |
| "Speed peaked around eighteen. Faces, early thirties. Reading emotions, forties to fifties. Vocabulary, late sixties." | MIT News: "raw speed in processing information appears to peak around age 18 or 19"; "the ability to recognize faces improves until the early 30s"; "For the ability to evaluate other people's emotional states, the peak occurred much later, in the 40s or 50s"; vocabulary: "the new data showed a later peak, in the late 60s or early 70s". Harvard Gazette summary agrees (faces 30-34; social cognition 40s to 50; vocabulary to 65-70). | MIT News (above) · Harvard Gazette, "Smarter by the minute, sort of", March 2015: https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2015/03/smarter-by-the-minute-sort-of/ |
| "No single peak. Dave's brain peaks in shifts. Some shifts haven't started." | Hartshorne: "we saw there was no single peak for all abilities." For a 40-year-old, the emotion-reading (40s-50s) and vocabulary (late 60s-early 70s) peaks are still ahead. "Shifts" is our image for "asynchronous rise and fall" (the paper's title). | MIT News (above) |
| "In two thousand eight, Hamburg scientists taught forty-four people around sixty to juggle." | 44 trainees with complete data, mean age 59.1 (range 50-67), plus 25 controls (mean 60.2); three months to learn a three-ball cascade. Authors at the Department of Systems Neuroscience, University of Hamburg (Boyke, Driemeyer, Büchel, May) and Jena (Gaser). | Boyke J., Driemeyer J., Gaser C., Büchel C., May A., "Training-induced brain structure changes in the elderly", *Journal of Neuroscience* 2008;28(28):7031-7035, doi 10.1523/JNEUROSCI.0742-08.2008: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6670504 · PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18614670/ |
| "Most never managed a full minute." | "Only 10 of 44 individuals in the present cohort were able to juggle 60 s at time point two." Abstract: they learned "but with less proficiency" than 20-year-olds in the earlier study. | Same paper |
| "But their scans showed new grey matter where the brain tracks motion." | Grey matter increased in hMT/V5 (the visual motion area), plus transient increases in the hippocampus and nucleus accumbens. Caveat: the main comparison used the 25 best jugglers vs 25 controls (most of the 25 still under a minute); the authors say using all 44 "did not change the results, but reduced significance". | Same paper |
| "Clumsy, sixty, and growing." | Narrator's summary of the two rows above (mean age 59). | Same paper |
| "Then they stopped. Three months later, the new grey matter had faded." | Third scan, 3 months after training ended: "This pattern reversed". The hMT/V5 decrease vs baseline was not significant, so "faded" (back toward where it started), not "shrank below". | Same paper |
| "Their brains never checked their age. Only whether they were still juggling." | Narrator's reading of the result: training-induced grey-matter change appeared in people around 60 and receded when practice stopped. The same rise-and-fade happened in 20-year-olds (Draganski et al. 2004, *Nature* 427:311-312: https://www.nature.com/articles/427311a), so practice, not age, decides whether it shows. The older group's effect was slightly smaller, and their juggling was worse; the script says so ("most never managed a full minute"). | Boyke 2008; Draganski 2004 |
| "Tonight, ten minutes, one chord. Tomorrow, the same chord." | The one safe action: a small, repeated practice session. "Ten minutes" and "one chord" are our framing, not a dose from a study; the evidence is that change showed up with ongoing practice and faded without it (Boyke 2008). No claim about memory, dementia or health. | Boyke 2008 |

## Checked and NOT used
- **Taxi drivers (Maguire), Dyson's prototypes, the testing effect (Roediger):** already used in episodes 16 and 20.
- **Grandma Moses, Julia Child, Raymond Chandler, Toni Morrison:** real late starters, but a list of late starters is survivorship bias (episode 20's point) and a fact list; one myth (Sanders) does the story job better.
- **Hartshorne, Tenenbaum & Pinker 2018 (*Cognition*):** grammar learning ability stays high until about 17 and native-like grammar needs an early start. True, and a fair caveat for language learning, but this episode's action is a skill (guitar), and claiming more about languages would need more time than one minute.
- **Park et al. 2014 Synapse Project (*Psychological Science*):** older adults learning digital photography or quilting improved episodic memory more than social or passive groups. Real and relevant, but it is a memory-improvement claim, and the series avoids health claims beyond one source; cut for length.
- **"The brain finishes developing at twenty-five":** a widely repeated simplification of adolescent MRI studies. Not used as a fact; the script only says Dave *read* it.
