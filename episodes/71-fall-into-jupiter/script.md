# chapter: A flag for Jupiter
This is Dave. Dave has bought a flag. It says: Dave was here. He plans to plant it on Jupiter, the biggest planet there is, / and stand beside it for a photo. // [J]
"It's a planet. Planets have ground. How hard can the ground be?" (@dave) // [J]
So here is the question. What would happen if you fell into Jupiter? Would you land, and if you didn't, how far down would you go? /
Tonight Dave drops through the clouds minute by minute, with a gauge on his sleeve, following the one machine that has really done it. /
A small NASA probe called Galileo went first, in nineteen ninety-five. It had a parachute and a thermometer, and it's the reason we know any of this. /
Expect a fireball, a very pleasant hour, and a floor that never turns up. //

# chapter: The way in
Jupiter weighs about three hundred and eighteen times as much as Earth. More than thirteen hundred Earths would fit inside it. /
At the cloud tops, gravity is two point six times Earth's. If Dave weighs seventy kilograms at home, the bathroom scale would say about a hundred and eighty-five. // [J]
Even before the clouds, the probe flew through an intense radiation belt about fifty thousand kilometres up, one we hadn't known was there. Dave was not told. // [J]
Then there's the way in. Galileo's probe hit the top of the atmosphere at about forty-eight kilometres a second. That's a hundred and seventy thousand kilometres an hour. /
Its shield took the worst of it: about two hundred and twenty-eight times Earth's gravity at the peak, and air at about sixteen thousand degrees. /
By the end, the front of the shield was about a third as thick as when it started. / Dave borrowed that shield, and would have liked to hear that figure before, not after. // [J]
A minute or two in, a parachute opens, the shield drops away, and the probe starts to report. //

# chapter: Minute eight: sea level
Eight minutes after entry, just under the visible cloud tops, NASA's timeline says the pressure matches Earth's at sea level. /
This is the nicest layer in the whole planet. The pressure is perfect. Everything else is not. // [J]
It's about a hundred and seven degrees below zero. The air is hydrogen and helium, like the Sun's. And the wind is already about three hundred kilometres an hour. /
NASA thinks Jupiter likely has three cloud layers: ammonia ice on top, and water ice and vapour at the bottom. Dave is aiming for the water. /
"Pressure's lovely. Could somebody close a window?" (@dave) // [J]
It's the last easy bit. From here, every kilometre adds heat and pressure, and nothing ever takes them away. //

# chapter: Minute thirty: room temperature
Half an hour in, the temperature reaches what NASA's timeline calls room temperature on Earth. /
Dave takes off his jacket. It's about twenty degrees, there's no ground in sight, and the sky is still a wall of cloud. // [J]
And the wind keeps climbing. Between the cloud tops and about four times Earth's air pressure, it rises from three hundred kilometres an hour to about six hundred. /
Then it stays there, all the way down. In nineteen ninety-seven, Atkinson's team reported no sign that it weakened with depth. /
Before the probe, many expected it to fade. It never did. Jupiter's weather isn't a coating. It goes down with the planet. // [J]
Dave drifts along with the wind, so he feels nothing at all. Small mercies. // [J]
NASA says the probe also saw less lightning than expected, and about half the helium. /
"Lovely and warm. Any sign of the ground yet?" (@dave) //
"Dave. Shh. Look at the gauge." (@brain) // [J]

# chapter: Hour one: the last signal
After fifty-eight minutes of reporting, the probe's signal ends. NASA says the high temperatures silenced its transmitters. /
Its last reading: about twenty-two times Earth's pressure, a hundred and fifty-six degrees Celsius, and a hundred and thirty-three kilometres below the level where the pressure matched our sea level. /
That pressure is what you'd feel about two hundred metres under the sea, with a heat well above the boiling point of water on top. //
"It stopped. That must be the bottom. I'll plant the flag here." (@dave) //
"It stopped because it was cooked, Dave. And this is not the bottom." (@brain) // [J]
NASA's timeline says the probe was probably completely vaporised by three the next morning. It never landed. It just kept going down, and stopped being a probe. //

# chapter: Not even close
Here's the sum the Brain was doing. From the cloud level to the centre of Jupiter is about seventy-one thousand kilometres. The probe made it a hundred and thirty-three. /
That's less than two-tenths of one percent of the way down. Dave hasn't fallen into Jupiter yet. He's fallen onto it. // [J]
Below the probe, NASA says, pressure and temperature keep rising until the hydrogen gas is squeezed into a liquid. The largest ocean in the solar system, made of hydrogen. /
And there's no shoreline. Hydrogen here is above its critical point, so there is no sharp boundary between gas and liquid. / A dropped penny would never land with a clink. It just gets thicker, kilometre after kilometre. // [J]
Further down, the pressure is so high that the liquid conducts electricity like a metal. Models put that thousands of kilometres down, and they disagree by a lot. /
"Brain, I'm in a metal ocean. Where is the flag supposed to go?" (@dave) //
"Please stop asking about the ground, Dave." (@brain) // [J]
Even the centre won't help. NASA's Juno spacecraft suggests Jupiter's core is large and fuzzy, a slow fade from heavy stuff into hydrogen, not a solid ball to stand on. /
The Great Red Spot is a storm twice as wide as Earth. Juno's gravity data put its roots about five hundred kilometres down, / and the lead scientist thinks it probably fades out gradually below that. /
Juno also found that the wind belts reach about three thousand kilometres down. That's more than twenty times deeper than the probe could tell us. //

# chapter: One dry hole
So at least the one trip we have is solid, right? Well, not quite. /
The probe found about ten times less water than expected. Jupiter was built partly from icy material, so scientists thought it would be wet. /
Then the explanation arrived. The probe had fallen into a clearing in the clouds, a dry spot where deeper, warmer layers show through. /
Its own temperature paper calls the place the edge of a hot spot. / And in nineteen ninety-seven, Galileo's orbiter found the air around a dry spot holds a hundred times more water than the spot itself. /
As planetary scientist Andrew Ingersoll put it: "Jupiter is not as dry overall as we thought." //
In twenty twenty, Juno measured the equator with microwaves that see through clouds. / About a quarter of one percent of the molecules there were water, almost three times the Sun's share. /
The probe's own water reading appeared to be still increasing at the greatest depth it reached. So the one machine we sent in fell into the driest spot around. // [J]
Even that is only the equator. Juno found Jupiter's deep air changes a lot from place to place, so nobody can yet say how much water the whole planet has. /
One hole, one hour, and a lot of Jupiter we haven't seen. //
