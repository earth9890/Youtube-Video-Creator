# chapter: Back by dark

So how did ancient humans raise babies? //
The answer runs through a clay baby bottle in a grave, a toddler lifted up to a cave ceiling, a grandmother, and a very old lullaby about cheese. /
The baby looks at Dave. Dave looks at the baby. Neither is impressed. // [J]

# chapter: The bottle
Start with food. In Bavaria, in southern Germany, archaeologists found three tiny clay vessels in the graves of infants, from the Bronze and Iron Ages. /
They are about five to ten centimetres across, with an extremely narrow spout. //
For years, nobody knew what they were. Some scholars thought they were feeding cups for the sick or the old. /
So in twenty nineteen, a team led by Julie Dunne tested the residue left inside. //
The fats inside came from ruminant milk, the milk of cattle, sheep or goats. These were baby bottles. /
Until then, the only evidence about weaning came from the chemistry of baby bones, which showed roughly when, but not what the babies were drinking. //
Similar vessels appear in Europe from around five thousand BCE. Some of them have little feet, and are shaped like imaginary animals. /
Babies, it seems, have always preferred a bottle with a personality. // [J]
Dunne called them small, evocative vessels, a real connection to mothers and infants in the past. /
Dave picks the bottle shaped like an imaginary animal. The baby accepts it immediately. // [J]
Remember that these were found in very rare graves of children. Most of the babies who used them are lost to us. //
And bottles were the side dish. Among the Kung, a modern group of foragers in southern Africa, the main course was the mother. /
In the nineteen seventies, researchers counted about four feeds an hour, a few minutes each. The average gap between feeds was fourteen minutes. //
Dave does the maths for a whole afternoon, and sits down. // [J]

# chapter: The sling
Which raises the next problem. A baby that feeds every fifteen minutes can't be left in a cave. It has to come with you. //
No ancient sling has survived. Leather and plant fibre rot. So the best clue comes from living foragers. /
Among the Kung, newborns were carried in a sling, upright and pressed against the mother's side. No clothing separated the baby's skin from the mother's. /
Physical contact with someone peaked at about ninety percent of the time at ten to twenty weeks. //
Among the Hadza of Tanzania, babies were carried in a sling for the majority of the time. A sling and no instruction manual. // [J]
Does it work? In nineteen eighty-six, researchers split ninety-nine mothers and babies into two groups. /
Half of them were asked to carry their babies more. At six weeks, those babies cried and fussed forty-three percent less. //
Dave straps the baby on, and the crying stops. Dave decides this is the greatest invention in history. // [J]
There is one more clue, and it is in a French cave. In Rouffignac, finger lines cover a ceiling, high above anyone's head. /
Researchers Kevin Sharpe and Leslie Van Gelder measured the lines and argued that children aged between two and five made most of them. //
The ceiling is too high for a toddler to reach. So adults, or teenagers, must have hoisted the children up onto their shoulders. /
It happened sometime between thirteen and twenty-seven thousand years ago. //
The oldest evidence of a toddler's art comes with the oldest evidence of somebody carrying them to it. // [J]
Dave tries to lift the baby onto his shoulders. The baby has views about this. // [J]

# chapter: Many hands
Dave is tired. He has been a carrying device for an hour. // [J]
Here, the evidence says he was never meant to do this alone. Among the Efe, forest foragers in Central Africa, babies were cared for by five to twenty-four different people. /
The average was fourteen. Other women often breastfed a newborn in the first days, until the mother's milk came in. /
By eighteen weeks, other people accounted for about sixty percent of the baby's physical contact. // [J]
The mother, in other words, had a team. Dave is offered the Efe approach to feeding, and politely declines. // [J]
And there's a theory about one carer in particular. In nineteen ninety-eight, Kristen Hawkes and her colleagues published the grandmother hypothesis. /
Humans live long after menopause, unlike other primates. They suggested that this evolved because older women fed their grandchildren, which freed up their daughters to have more babies. //
That's a hypothesis, not a settled fact. But there's a number that fits it. //
Rachel Caspari and Sang-Hee Lee compared the teeth of ancient adults. For every ten young Neanderthal adults who died, only four older adults had survived past thirty. /
In the European Upper Paleolithic, for every ten, there were twenty. Twenty potential grandparents. //
They argued that this helped drive population growth and cultural innovation. //
Dave looks around for a grandmother. The nearest candidate is a goat. // [J]

# chapter: The oldest lullaby
Soon the baby is tired, and furious about it. Dave needs it to sleep, urgently. //
The oldest lullaby we can read is Sumerian, from the reign of King Shulgi of Ur, about four thousand years ago. /
It was written for one of his sons, though nobody knows which. //
Scholars say it is unique in Sumerian literature. A mother sings it to a child who seems to be ill. /
She promises him healing cheeses and well-watered lettuce. And she calls on sleep itself to come, as if Sleep were a neighbour who owed her a favour. // [J]
Cheese that heals, and lettuce that has been properly watered. The mother is clearly doing her best. // [J]
She imagines her son grown up, with a wife and a child of his own, watched over by a goddess. //
It isn't a happy-go-lucky tune. It's a worried parent, singing hope into the room. //
Dave doesn't know the Sumerian. He sings the only song he knows, which is mostly about cheese. The baby sleeps anyway. //

# chapter: The missing toys
The baby has slept, and woken, and wants something to do. Dave wants a toy. //
And archaeologists have a problem. Almost none have been found. //
Biblical scholar Kristine Garroway describes why with a cartoon. Two men in suits are staring at a round object on a table. /
"Hey, what's this?" asks one. "I dunno," says the other. "Probably a toy. Or a religious object." //
Archaeologists, she says, have long tended to choose the religious object. Small pots and figurines get filed as ritual. // [J]
In archaeology, if you can't explain it, it's ritual. And if it's tiny, it's for very small gods. // [J]
But some objects give themselves away. At Tel Nagila, in Israel, archaeologists dug up forty-eight miniature clay pots from houses about thirty-six hundred to four thousand years old. /
Ten of them had fingerprints the size of a child's, pressed into the clay before it was fired. //
Children made those pots, copying adult craftwork. //
Garroway sees more of it in rounded clay disks, about the size of a button, with two holes. /
Thread a string through, swing it to wind it up, pull, and it spins. The sloppy ones, with rough edges, were probably made by novices. //
She suspects the sloppy finish came from the children's impatience to play with the final product. //
The same trick goes back much further. In France and Spain, people made spinning disks with an animal on each side, fourteen to twenty-one thousand years ago. /
Twirl the string and the animal seems to move. /
Archaeologist John Shea adds that nearly every stone-tool site holds tiny, rough tools made from poor rock, which children may have made. //
The oldest cartoon, and the oldest craft kit. //
Dave watches the baby chew a clay pot. A religious object, obviously. //
