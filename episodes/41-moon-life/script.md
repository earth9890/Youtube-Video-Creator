# chapter: One suitcase
This is Dave. Dave has packed one suitcase, because Dave is moving to the Moon. //
He wants somewhere quiet, with a great view, where nobody knocks on the door.
So what if you tried to live on the Moon? Not visit for an afternoon. Live there. //
Twelve people have walked on the Moon, and the longest anyone has stayed is about three days. Dave has packed for longer.
Earplugs, sunscreen, a warm jumper, one bottle of water, and a packet of seeds, because every home needs a garden. // [J]
"One suitcase. Honestly, how hard can the Moon be?" (@dave) //
By hour one, he can't hear a thing. By day four, he's broken a record. And by week two, the sun goes down for two weeks. //

# chapter: Hour one
Dave steps outside and feels lighter. The Moon's gravity is about one sixth of Earth's, so in theory, he can jump about six times higher. /
He bounces like a kangaroo. NASA's notes say no Apollo astronaut used that hop on flat ground, except in play. Dave is playing. // [J]
Then he notices the quiet. Sound needs air to travel through, and the Moon has almost none. /
A sugar cube of Earth's air holds about a hundred trillion times more molecules than the same space just above the Moon. //
So Dave has brought earplugs to a place where sound can't even travel. // [J]
The sky is black, even at midday, because there's no air to scatter the sunlight. And hanging in that black sky is the Earth. /
On the side of the Moon that faces us, Earth stays in almost the same spot. It never rises, and it never sets. /
Dave wanted a home with a view. He got a view that never moves. // [J]

# chapter: Day one
But a day on the Moon isn't really a day. From one sunrise to the next takes about twenty-nine and a half Earth days. /
That's about two weeks of sun, then two weeks of night. Dave's first day on the Moon will last a month. // [J]
With no air to spread the heat around, the ground near the equator heats up to over a hundred and twenty degrees Celsius. Hotter than boiling water. /
Dave puts on his sunscreen. He is very thorough about it. // [J]
But sunscreen is for sunburn. The real danger is invisible: cosmic rays, particles from deep space. /
On Earth, our thick air and our magnetic field protect us. The Moon has neither. /
In twenty nineteen, a Chinese lander took the first proper radiation measurements on the Moon's surface. /
That's about two hundred times the dose you'd get on the ground on Earth, and nearly twice the dose inside the Space Station. /
You can't feel it. But over a long stay, it raises the risk of cancer. /
The scientists who built the detector had a tip: cover your home with a thick layer of Moon soil. Dave's home is a suitcase. // [J]

# chapter: Day four: the dust
On day four, Dave breaks the record set by Apollo seventeen's crew: about seventy-five hours. //
"Breaking news. A man on the Moon has set a new record, mostly by not leaving." (@news) // [J]
But Dave now has a problem that wasn't on his packing list. The dust. //
On Earth, wind and water wear grains of dust smooth. The Moon has neither, so its dust stays as fine as flour, and as sharp as broken glass. /
Sunlight gives it an electric charge, so it clings to everything, like static on a balloon. /
On Apollo, it jammed latches, scratched visors, wore through layers of boots, and broke the seals on boxes of Moon rock. /
One commander said his suit looked more worn after eight hours on the Moon than his training suit after a hundred. // [J]
Back inside their lander, it smelled like burnt gunpowder, and all twelve moonwalkers reacted to it, from sneezing to a blocked nose. //
"Mission Control here. Dave, you have what one Apollo astronaut called lunar hay fever." (@doc) // [J]
The last commander to leave the Moon said they could beat every other problem up there, except the dust. //
Dave didn't pack a dustpan. It wouldn't have helped. On Apollo, brushing worked far better in tests than on the Moon. // [J]

# chapter: Week two: the night
Around day fifteen, the sun sets. With no air to hold in the heat, the ground near the equator drops to about minus a hundred and thirty degrees Celsius. /
Dave puts on his warm jumper. It is not warm enough. // [J]
Dave's water bottle is now a block of ice. And that, it turns out, is how the Moon keeps its water. // [J]
Near the poles, the Sun stays so low that some crater floors never see sunlight. Down there, the warmest it ever gets is about minus a hundred and fifty-seven degrees. /
In two thousand nine, NASA crashed part of a rocket into one of those craters on purpose, and found water ice in the debris. //
Away from the poles, it's far drier. In twenty twenty, a telescope flying in a jumbo jet found about one small bottle of water per cubic metre of sunlit soil. /
The Sahara has about a hundred times more. So Dave's one bottle of water is worth about a cubic metre of Moon. // [J]

# chapter: Month one: air and salad
By the end of the first month, Dave needs more air. The good news is, he's standing on it. // [J]
By weight, Moon soil is about forty to forty-five percent oxygen. It's just locked inside the minerals. /
In twenty nineteen, a team in Glasgow, working with the European Space Agency, cooked fake Moon soil in molten salt and ran a current through it. /
In fifty hours, they pulled out ninety-six percent of its oxygen. /
"Air from rocks. Brilliant. Now, what's for dinner?" (@dave) //
Dave opens his seeds. In twenty twenty-two, a University of Florida team tried that on Earth, with just twelve grams of real Apollo soil. /
They planted thale cress, a small weed from the mustard family. /
After two days, everything sprouted. After about day six, the Moon plants fell behind: slower, with stunted roots, and some turned reddish. /
Their genes showed the same stress as plants grown in too much salt or metal. //
So you can grow a salad on the Moon. It just won't be a happy salad. // [J]
In orbit, astronauts' bones lose about one to one and a half percent of their density a month. Whether one sixth of Earth's gravity helps, nobody knows yet. //

# chapter: Under the Moon
So that settles it, right? Air, water and food. Dave can live on the Moon. //
Well, not quite. Not on it, anyway. Look at Dave's problems: the heat, the cold, the radiation, the dust. They all live on the surface. /
So the best home on the Moon might not be on the Moon. It might be in it. //
"I came all this way to live in a hole?" (@dave) // [J]
Yes, Dave. A very good hole. In two thousand nine, a Japanese orbiter confirmed the first pit in the Moon. Today we know of more than two hundred. /
Some are probably skylights into lava tubes, tunnels where rivers of molten rock once flowed. /
In twenty twenty-four, a team in Italy reread old radar data and found a cave stretching tens of metres beyond the bottom of one pit. /
Nobody knows how far it goes. It could run for kilometres under the Sea of Tranquillity. //
Two years earlier, a University of California team studied that same pit. /
In the shade under its overhang, it stays at about seventeen degrees Celsius. All day, all night, all month. //
The university called it sweater weather. Dave would call it jumper weather. // [J]
A roof of rock also gives some protection from cosmic rays and tiny meteorites. Remember the tip, to cover your home with Moon soil? The Moon already did it. // [J]

# chapter: Unpacking
So, what if you tried to live on the Moon? On the surface, it's a fight with the heat, the cold, the radiation and, worst of all, the dust. //
But underneath, you might just manage, with air from rock, ice from the craters, and a stressed salad. //
One of those scientists put it simply: humans evolved living in caves, and to caves we might return when we live on the Moon. //
(slow) Dave carries his suitcase down into the pit. Out come the earplugs, the sunscreen, the empty bottle and the seeds. /
(slow) Then the one thing he packed that works exactly as planned. He puts on his jumper. //
(slow) Above the pit, the Earth hangs in the same spot it has hung all month. //
(slow) And for the first time, Dave unpacks.
