# chapter: The first bubble
This is Dave. This morning, Dave opened his curtains and saw a small white bubble above his neighbour's head. //
It said: did I leave the oven on? // [J]
Every head Dave can see now has a bubble showing what that person is thinking. //
Every head except one. Dave can't see above his own. //
So what would really happen if you could see everyone's thoughts? For about an hour, it's a superpower. /
By hour six, he'll know what his friends really think of him. By day two, a crowded train will nearly drown him in other people's heads. /
And by day four, he'll be scared of his own. //
Inside Dave's head, a tired little librarian looks up from her desk. That's his brain. /
"Heads have lids for a reason, Dave. You'll see." (@brain) //

# chapter: Hour one: a superpower
Hour one, and Dave is a genius. It's his sister's birthday, and her bubble holds a picture: a yellow umbrella, covered in ducks. /
Dave buys the umbrella. She cries. Best brother ever. // [J]
At his job interview, the interviewer's bubble says: please, just say teamwork. Dave says teamwork. He gets the job. // [J]
But most of the bubbles don't have any words in them at all. //
In two thousand eight, Christopher Heavey and Russell Hurlburt gave thirty students a beeper that went off at random, and asked what was in their heads at each beep. /
The inner voice turned up in only about a quarter of those moments, and pictures in about a third. /
And about one moment in five held a clear thought with no words or pictures at all. //
So some bubbles are empty, and Dave still somehow knows they mean: maybe soup. // [J]
Five of the thirty students reported no inner voice at all. So the interviewer has a radio station in his head, and Dave's sister has a silent picture book. // [J]
Normally, we're bad at this. In nineteen ninety-two, Linda Stinson and William Ickes filmed pairs of young men chatting, then had each one guess the other's thoughts. /
Strangers got about twenty percent right. Friends, about thirty. And Dave is now on a hundred. //

# chapter: Hour six: six thousand thoughts
By hour six, the bubbles won't stay still. They keep changing. //
You may have heard we have sixty thousand thoughts a day. It's often credited to the National Science Foundation, which can't find it either. // [J]
In twenty twenty, Julie Tseng and Jordan Poppenk in Canada studied brain scans of a hundred and eighty-four people watching films and resting. /
They counted the moments when brain activity jumps to a new pattern, which they think marks a new thought. /
About six and a half a minute, or, as a tentative estimate, a little over six thousand a day. /
So Dave isn't reading one book per person. He's reading six thousand very short ones. // [J]
At lunch, his date's bubble says: he's sweet. Is that spinach? Dave checks. It is spinach. He has never loved a bubble more. // [J]
Then his friends arrive. Sam's bubble says: his hair looks weird today. /
Jo nods along to Dave's story. Her bubble says: he's talking about his goldfish again. // [J]
And when Sam says, love the haircut, his bubble says: it's, well, a haircut. //
That's a white lie. In nineteen ninety-six, Bella DePaulo's team had a hundred and forty-seven people log every lie they told for a week. /
Students told about two a day, other adults about one, and one lie in four was told to protect someone else. /
In Dave's world, every white lie comes with subtitles. // [J]
"Brain, I don't want to know what people think anymore." (@dave) /
"I did mention the lids, Dave." (@brain) // [J]

# chapter: Day two: the train
Day two. Rush hour. Dave squeezes onto a train with about a hundred other people, and a hundred bubbles. //
Shopping lists, old arguments, a song stuck on repeat, a dog someone saw in two thousand twelve. // [J]
Above a calm man in a suit: what if I licked that pole? // [J]
Above a sweet old lady in the quiet carriage: what if I shouted a rude word, right now? //
Is this train full of secret villains? No. These are intrusive thoughts, and almost everybody has them. /
In nineteen seventy-eight, Stanley Rachman and Padmal de Silva asked a hundred and twenty-four people with no diagnosis about unwanted thoughts. /
Ninety-nine of them said they had them, and most found them easy to brush off. /
Even clinicians found them surprisingly hard to tell apart from patients' obsessions. //
In twenty fourteen, a survey of students in thirteen countries found that ninety-four percent had had at least one in the past three months. //
An intrusive thought isn't a plan. It's your brain checking a door it has no intention of opening. //
The man in the suit gets off at his stop. He has not licked anything. // [J]
But there's no off switch, and nobody gets a private moment. Not the man practising an apology, not the woman quietly missing her dad. /
It feels like reading everyone's diary without asking. //

# chapter: Day four: his own bubble
By day four, Dave stops going out. Not because of other people's bubbles. Because of his own. //
"What if it works both ways, Brain? What if everyone can see mine?" (@dave) /
"Then they'll see what I put up with all day." (@brain) // [J]
On the way to the shops, his own head offers: what if you hugged the bus driver? What if you sang at the checkout? // [J]
So Dave wears a big woolly hat, just in case. // [J]
That feeling, that your thoughts are leaking out, has a name: the illusion of transparency. /
In nineteen ninety-eight, Thomas Gilovich's team at Cornell had students tell lies in small groups, then guess how many would catch them. /
The liars expected about half the group to see through them. Only about a quarter did, no better than guessing. //
Everyone walks around feeling like Dave in his woolly hat. // [J]

# chapter: Can anyone really read minds?
So Dave visits a lab. Can anyone really read minds? //
"Brain decoders are real, Dave. They're just very slow and very picky." (@doc) /
In twenty twenty-three, Jerry Tang and Alexander Huth's team in Texas had three volunteers listen to sixteen hours of stories inside a brain scanner. /
Then a computer turned new brain activity back into words. Well, the gist. /
When one volunteer heard, I don't have my driver's license yet, it wrote: she has not even started to learn to drive yet. //
Trained on one person, it barely worked on anyone else. And when volunteers silently named animals, it mostly stopped working. /
So one of the best mind readers on Earth needs sixteen hours of your help, and you can beat it by thinking about giraffes. // [J]
For now, the lid stays on. Your thoughts are still your own. //

# chapter: The twist
So Dave takes off the hat, goes back out, and this time, he reads slowly. //
Sam's bubble still says: his hair looks weird today. But the next one says: I'm really glad he came. /
And Jo's says: he gets so happy about that goldfish. I love that. //
The rude thoughts were real. They were just the loudest, not the last. //
In twenty eighteen, Erica Boothby's team asked people after a chat how much they liked their partner, and how much they thought they were liked. /
Again and again, people underestimated it. The researchers called it the liking gap, and among students sharing a dorm, it lasted for months. /
So Dave spent four days scared of what people really think. Mostly, they think: oven, lunch, a song on repeat, and, very quietly, I like Dave. // [J]
Every head is as messy as his: half-finished sentences, empty bubbles, an urge to lick a pole, and underneath it all, a lot of kindness nobody says out loud. //
And there's a better superpower than bubbles. In twenty eighteen, Tal Eyal and Nicholas Epley ran twenty-five experiments on reading minds. /
Imagining someone else's point of view didn't reliably make anyone more accurate. What worked was much less magic. You ask them. //

# chapter: The oven
On day five, the bubbles start to fade, and Dave lets them. /
So what would really happen if you could see everyone's thoughts? An hour of superpower, then a flood. /
And then, if you read slowly, a relief. Other heads are as messy as yours, and they like you more than you think. //
At the bus stop, his neighbour looks worried. Dave doesn't read his bubble. He asks. /
"Everything okay? You look like you're thinking hard." (@dave) /
His neighbour laughs. He's wondering if he left the oven on. //
Dave knows the feeling. He walks home to check his own.
