# chapter: Three hundred grams
This is Dave. It's seven in the morning, and he has stacked four dozen eggs on the kitchen counter. //
Today, Dave is going to eat three hundred grams of protein. By tomorrow, he plans to have enormous arms. // [J]
"Protein is muscle. More protein, more muscle. It's simple." (@dave) //
So what would happen if you ate three hundred grams of protein in a day? Where would it all go? /
By mid-morning, his brain begs him to stop. By the afternoon, his liver hits a speed limit, and an Arctic explorer has a warning for him. //
This is a thought experiment, so please don't try it. Let's watch Dave do it instead. // [J]
On the fridge, he's drawn a scoreboard. It says zero out of three hundred. /
Three hundred grams is about forty-eight large eggs, or a kilo of roast chicken breast. /
America's National Academies set the recommended amount at zero point eight grams of protein a day for every kilo you weigh. /
Dave weighs eighty kilos, so that's about sixty-four grams. He's going for almost five times that. // [J]

# chapter: Breakfast: twelve eggs
Seven fifteen. Twelve eggs, about seventy-five grams. The scoreboard says seventy-five. /
By egg number nine, a small, tired voice speaks up inside Dave's head. His brain. /
"Dave, we're full. We've been full since egg number seven." (@brain) // [J]
Protein is the most filling of the big three nutrients. Protein first, then carbs, then fat. /
In a two thousand five Seattle study, people who doubled their share of protein ate about four hundred and forty fewer calories a day, without being told to. //
So Dave is trying to eat more, using the one food that's best at making you eat less. // [J]
And there's a second problem. Your body burns energy just processing food: up to three percent of the energy in fat, five to ten in carbs. /
For protein, it's twenty to thirty percent. So of Dave's twelve hundred protein calories, roughly three hundred leave him as heat. /
Dave has become a small radiator that runs on eggs. // [J]

# chapter: Lunch: the nitrogen problem
Twelve noon. A quarter of a kilo of chicken breast. The scoreboard says a hundred and fifty. /
"Halfway there, and my arms already feel bigger." (@dave) // [J]
Here's the part nobody puts on the label. Carbs and fat are made of carbon, hydrogen and oxygen, so when you burn them, you mostly breathe them out. //
Protein has an extra ingredient. About sixteen percent of it is nitrogen, and when you burn protein, that nitrogen comes off as ammonia, which is toxic. /
So your liver runs a little factory that turns ammonia into urea, and your kidneys flush the urea out in your urine. /
By one kidney specialist's sums, Dave's three hundred grams end up as roughly ninety grams of urea. /
Dave wanted to build muscle. So far, he's mostly building urea. // [J]
And urea needs water to carry it out. For Dave, that mostly means a very busy bathroom door. // [J]
His kidneys speed up too, filtering faster after big protein meals. We'll come back to whether that matters. //

# chapter: The liver's speed limit
Four in the afternoon. Three scoops of protein powder, twenty-five grams each. The scoreboard says two hundred and twenty-five. /
But Dave's liver can only make urea so fast. In two thousand six, two Australian researchers worked out the ceiling. /
For an eighty kilo person, they called about two hundred and eighty-five grams a day the absolute maximum. Roughly a kilo of lean meat. //
Dave weighs eighty kilos. Dave is aiming for three hundred. // [J]
"Fifteen grams over the limit? That's hardly anything." (@dave) //
So what happens near the limit? In nineteen twenty-eight, the Arctic explorer Stefansson checked into a New York hospital to start living on nothing but meat. /
At his doctors' request, he started with lean meat only, about two hundred and sixty grams of protein a day. Within three days, he had nausea and diarrhoea. //
Then they added fat. He recovered in two days, and went on eating only meat for a year, with no obvious harm. /
His doctors thought the high protein may have been part of the problem. And Stefansson had seen this before. //

# chapter: Rabbit starvation
Up north, it was called rabbit starvation. Rabbits are very lean. Live on nothing but rabbit, Stefansson wrote, and you eat bigger and bigger meals, /
but you feel hungry at the end of each one. Within about a week, you're eating three or four times as much. /
Then comes diarrhoea, and without fat, he wrote, death after several weeks. //
But wait. Protein is the most filling nutrient there is. How can it leave you starving? /
Because the body can only turn so much protein into fuel, and a rabbit has almost nothing else to offer. Your stomach is stuffed. Your engine is empty. //
"Lean meat fills your belly and starves you anyway, lad. Find the fat." (@old) //
Dave checks his scoreboard. It has one column. Protein. // [J]

# chapter: The twenty-five gram rule
Ten at night. One last quarter-kilo of chicken. The scoreboard says three hundred. Dave has done it. //
"I'm hot, I'm full, and I live in the bathroom now. But at least my muscles got all of it." (@dave) // [J]
Not according to the gym. The old rule says one meal can only build muscle with about twenty to twenty-five grams of protein. /
Anything more, it says, gets burned for energy or turned into urea. /
In two thousand twenty-three, a team at Maastricht University tested it. / Thirty-six young men did a hard workout, then drank a shake with no protein, twenty-five grams of milk protein, or a hundred. /
A hundred grams was the most the scientists thought anyone could eat in one meal. Dave has done that three times over today. // [J]
And the hundred grams won. Their muscles kept building for more than twelve hours, and by then, only about half of the protein had even reached their blood. // [J]
So, at least after a hard workout, the old rule was wrong. Muscles can use much more from one meal than people thought. They just take their time. /
"So I was right all along. Protein is muscle." (@dave) //

# chapter: The twist
And here's the twist. Somebody has already run Dave's experiment, for eight weeks. //
In two thousand fourteen, a Florida university asked trained weightlifters to eat four point four grams of protein per kilo, every day. /
The researchers put extra people into the high-protein group, because they expected some to give up. // [J]
The twenty who finished ate an average of three hundred and seven grams a day, and about eight hundred extra calories, for eight weeks. /
No change in weight. No change in fat. And no change in muscle. //
A few of them did complain of stomach trouble, and of feeling hot. // [J]
Muscle isn't built from protein alone. A two thousand eighteen review of forty-nine studies found that in weightlifters, /
extra protein stopped adding muscle at around one point six grams per kilo a day. The lifting, they wrote, was the much more potent stimulus. //
For Dave, that's about a hundred and thirty grams. He ate that, plus another hundred and seventy his muscles likely had no use for. // [J]
So what would happen if you ate three hundred grams of protein in a day? You'd be very full, a little hot, and on first-name terms with your bathroom. // [J]
Your muscles would take what your training asks for. The rest gets burned as fuel, and its nitrogen leaves as urea. //

# chapter: What nobody knows yet
And if you did it every day, for years? Here, scientists still argue. /
A two thousand eighteen review of twenty-eight trials found no harm to kidney function in healthy people. But those diets had about twice the usual protein, not five times. /
One of the longest trials at very high intakes followed fourteen men for a year, and found no harm either. That's not many men, or many years. //
In twenty twenty, kidney specialists warned that lots of protein over many years might strain the kidneys' tiny filters. /
And most people who already have kidney disease are advised to eat less protein, not more. /
Europe's food safety agency says twice the recommended amount is considered safe. Dave's day was more than four times it. /
"So please, don't copy Dave. This was a thought experiment, not a plan." (@doc) //
