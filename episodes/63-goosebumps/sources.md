# Episode 63 "Why Do We Get Goosebumps?": sources (verified 2026-10-22)

Read in full text: Darwin 1872 ch. IV (Project Gutenberg), Shwartz et al. 2020 (PMC), Benedek & Kaernbach 2011 (PDF), Sachs et al. 2016 (PMC), de Fleurian & Pearce 2021 (open text). Others from abstracts. Dave, the wedding, the freezer, Priya and the toast are invented story devices; the script claims nothing from them. No song lyrics or titles are used.

| "Almost every hair on your body has a tiny muscle attached to its base, called the arrector pili. Darwin described it as one of the minute, unstriped, involuntary muscles." | Anatomy ("almost" because eyebrows/eyelashes lack it; standard anatomy, not sourced here). | Darwin 1872, ch. IV: "The movement is effected, as we know from Kolliker's interesting discovery, by the contraction of minute, unstriped, involuntary muscles, often called arrectores pili, which are attached to the capsules of the separate hairs". https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/1227 |
| "When a nerve tells the muscle to squeeze, it pulls the hair upright and dimples the skin around it. That dimple is the goosebump. Doctors call it piloerection." / "The nerve belongs to the sympathetic system, the one that runs your fight-or-flight settings." | Mechanism. | Shwartz et al. 2020, *Cell* 182:578: "elevated impulses from sympathetic nerves trigger the contraction of APM bundles, pulling the hair erect, a phenomenon known as piloerection or goosebumps." https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7540726/ ("fight-or-flight" is a standard gloss for the sympathetic system, not in the paper.) |
| "In eighteen seventy-two, Darwin called it a reflex action, independent of the will. He noted that it fires in the cold, in terror and in rage, and even when a neighbouring patch of skin is tickled." | Darwin ch. IV. | "The arrectores pili contract not only under the above emotions, but from the application of cold to the surface… Mr. Lister has also found, that tickling a neighbouring part of the skin causes the erection and protrusion of the hairs. From these facts it is manifest that the erection of the dermal appendages is a reflex action, independent of the will". |
| "In furry animals, standing hair does two jobs. It traps a layer of air… keeps the animal warm." | Insulation. | Shwartz 2020: "The erected hair traps air to create a layer of insulation for thermoregulation." |
| "when the animal is angry or afraid, it makes the animal look bigger. Darwin wrote that it serves to make the animal appear larger and more frightful to its enemies." | Darwin ch. IV. | "These appendages are erected under the excitement of anger or terror… The action serves to make the animal appear larger and more frightful to its enemies or rivals". |
| "He took a stuffed snake into the monkey house, and the hair on several species instantly stood up, especially on their tails." | Darwin ch. IV (checked against the Gutenberg text). | "I took a stuffed snake into the monkey-house, and the hair on several of the species instantly became erect; especially on their tails". |
| "He also remembered his mules and dogs after a night on the bleak Andes. Their hair was as erect as under the greatest terror." | Darwin. ("Andes" for "Cordillera".) | "I remember that my mules and dogs, brought from a lower and warmer country, after spending a night on the bleak Cordillera, had the hair all over their bodies as erect as under the greatest terror." |
| "Humans lost most of the fur and kept the wiring. Researchers describe our goosebumps as a relic of the thermoregulation of our furred ancestors." | Benedek & Kaernbach 2011, citing Campbell 1996. | "In humans, piloerection in response to cold is considered as a relic of the thermoregulatory response of our furred ancestors in evolution (Campbell, 1996)." https://scottbarrykaufman.com///wp-content/uploads/2017/07/Benedek-Kaernbach-2011.pdf |
| "Researchers call the music version frisson, which is French for shiver." | Term. | de Fleurian & Pearce 2021: "L. Harrison and Loui (2014) recommended the use of frisson, a term first used in the context of research on MECs by Huron (2006)". (The French meaning, "shiver", is dictionary knowledge.) https://doi.org/10.31234/osf.io/yc6d8 |
| "In nineteen ninety-one, the psychologist John Sloboda asked eighty-three music lovers… More than eighty percent reported shivers down the spine." / "Shivers were most reliably set off by new or unexpected harmonies." | Sloboda 1991, *Psychology of Music* 19:110. | Abstract: "Eighty-three music listeners completed a questionnaire… Shivers down the spine, laughter, tears and lump in the throat were reported by over 80% of respondents… shivers were most reliably evoked by passages containing new or unexpected harmonies." https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0305735691192002 |
| "Later work added crescendos, and the entry of a solo voice, a choir, or an extra instrument." | Review in Benedek & Kaernbach 2011. | "These structures involve crescendos, the violation of expectations (e.g., by unexpected harmonies), and the entry of a solo voice, a choir or an additional instrument (Grewe et al., 2007b; Guhn et al., 2007; Nagel et al., 2008; Panksepp, 1995; Sloboda, 1991)." |
| "at most nine in ten people can get music chills at all. So at least one in ten never does." | Upper-limit estimate from surveys. | de Fleurian & Pearce 2021, *Psychological Bulletin*: "from these results, it is reasonable to assume that 90% is an upper limit for the proportion of the population that has the ability to experience MECs." |
| "In one week of sampling, about eight in ten people had at least one music chill, and chills turned up in about one in seven listening sessions." | Experience sampling. | Same review: "during a week of experience sampling, 81% of respondents reported having at least one experience of MECs, and reported MECs in 14% of the occurrences of listening to music (Nusbaum et al., 2014)." |
| "In two thousand eleven, Valorie Salimpoor's team scanned listeners and found dopamine released in the striatum at the chills, and in a different spot during the build-up just before." | Salimpoor et al. 2011, *Nature Neuroscience* 14:257. | Abstract: "we found endogenous dopamine release in the striatum at peak emotional arousal during music listening"; "The caudate was more involved during the anticipation and the nucleus accumbens was more involved during the experience of peak emotional responses to music." (read in abstract coverage, BRAMS summary; the paper itself not read) https://brams.org/2011/01/09/salimpoor/ |
| "In two thousand eleven, Mathias Benedek and Christian Kaernbach filmed the forearms of fifty students while they listened to film soundtracks and music. Forty percent grew visible goosebumps. Film set them off in about a quarter of trials, and music in about one in nine." | Benedek & Kaernbach 2011, *Biol Psychol* 86:320. | Full text: "Fifty psychology students (43 females, 7 males)"; "The stimuli elicited visible piloerection in 40% of the participants"; "Visible piloerection was obtained in 70 out of 400 trials"; "film audio tracks significantly more often elicited piloerection (24%) than music tracks (11%, χ2 = 11.71, p < .001)". Flagged: the 7 men showed none ("none of the small subsample of male participants"), so the sex difference is not claimed in the script; piloerection was measured with an optical forearm device. |
| "They tested two ideas. Goosebumps as a marker of peak arousal didn't fit. Goosebumps as a social, being-moved feeling fit better, and film is more social than a song." | Their discussion (the "peak arousal" vs "separation call" hypotheses). | Abstract: "Consideration of all available evidence suggests that emotional piloerection represents a valuable indicator of the state of being moved or touched." The preference for the separation-call hypothesis over peak arousal is from the paper's discussion (seen via the PMC summary: "The separation call hypothesis is favored over the peak arousal hypothesis."). The script's "social" gloss paraphrases that. |
| "In about a third of the trials where the camera saw goosebumps, the students said they hadn't felt any." | Subjective vs objective mismatch. | Full text: "in 6% of trials (i.e., 34% of piloerection trials), participants reported not to have experienced piloerection, although it was detected." |
| "In two thousand sixteen, Matthew Sachs and colleagues surveyed two hundred and thirty-seven people. They picked ten who often get music chills and ten who rarely do, matched for personality and musical training." | Sachs, Ellis, Schlaug & Loui 2016, *SCAN* 11:884. | Full text: "A total of 237 people completed an online survey"; "we identified the 10 participants who reported perceiving chills to music… consistently, and the 10 participants who reported perceiving chills to music… rarely or never. Importantly, we matched the two groups for gender, age, personality factors and musical training." https://ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4884308/ |
| "Scans showed more nerve fibres linking the hearing area to the regions that handle feelings, about twice the volume on the right side." | Same. | "Chill responders showed higher volume in these tracts in both hemispheres" (median 1141.5 vs 541.5 voxels, right hemisphere; 2334 vs 1286.5 left); tracts from "superior temporal gyrus" to "insula and medial prefrontal cortex", "emotional and social processing areas". |
| "Ten against ten… and the authors call some of the links preliminary." | Flag. | "Because the sample size is relatively low, i.e. only 20 total, these correlations are considered preliminary and are interpreted with caution." |
| "Another study had a hundred students listen to five pieces chosen to give chills… People high in openness to experience… got more of them." | Colver & El-Alayli 2016, *Psychology of Music* 44:413. | Abstract: "One hundred college students completed the NEO-PI-R… and then listened to five musical selections that were likely to elicit frisson. Frisson was assessed via a combined self-report and physiological (galvanic skin response) measure. As predicted, frequency of frisson was positively correlated with overall Openness to Experience". https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0305735615572358 |
| "If the reflex is a leftover, why is it still here? Evolution tends to trim what nothing uses." | General principle (the question the Hsu paper raises). | Shwartz 2020: "this tri-lineage configuration is highly conserved across mammals including in humans, where piloerection has lost its role in thermoregulation, raising the possibility of additional functions." |
| "In twenty twenty, a team led by Ya-Chieh Hsu at Harvard studied exactly that muscle in mice, and published it in the journal Cell." | Shwartz…Hsu 2020. | Cell 182(3):578–593, "Cell Types Promoting Goosebumps Form a Niche to Regulate Hair Follicle Stem Cells". https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32679029/ |
| "the muscle and the sympathetic nerve are a team. The nerve forms synapse-like contacts with the hair's stem cells, and the muscle keeps the nerve in place." | Same abstract. | "Sympathetic nerves form synapse-like structures with HFSCs and regulate HFSCs through norepinephrine, whereas APMs maintain sympathetic innervation to HFSCs." |
| "In ordinary conditions, the nerve keeps the stem cells gently primed. Without its signal, they fall into a deeper sleep." | Same. | "Under steady state, this nerve-APM-HFSC connection primes HFSCs for activation by lowering the expression of quiescence regulators Foxp1 and Fgf18"; "Without norepinephrine signaling, HFSCs enter deep quiescence". |
| "During development, the hair follicle builds this team itself, using a signal called Sonic Hedgehog." | Same. | "During development, HFSC progeny secretes Sonic Hedgehog (SHH) to direct the formation of this APM-sympathetic nerve niche". |
| "Mice kept cold for two weeks began growing new hair ahead of schedule." | Cold experiment. | "mice exposed to cold entered anagen precociously to produce new hairs within less than 2 weeks"; "2 weeks of cold exposure in 2nd telogen results in premature anagen entry". |
| "Remove the muscle, and the nerve contact to the stem cells is lost, and the new coat arrives late." | APM ablation. | "we found that the sympathetic innervation to HFSCs was lost concomitantly following APM ablation… these APM-ablated mice also displayed a delay in anagen entry". |
| "The authors add that the same arrangement exists across mammals, including humans, where goosebumps no longer keep us warm. They say that raises the possibility of additional functions." | Same. | (quote in the row above on "highly conserved across mammals including in humans"). |
| "That is a mouse result. Nobody has shown the same in people, and it doesn't explain the choir." | Our flag; the paper is in mice (and uses mouse genetics, ablation, 3D electron microscopy). | Shwartz 2020 (mice throughout). We found no human study of the effect. |
| "the same sympathetic nerve that fires in the cold also fires when you're moved." | Inference: emotional piloerection comes with sympathetic markers (skin conductance); the Cell paper names the sympathetic nerve as the piloerection driver. | Benedek & Kaernbach 2011: "The occurrence of piloerection was primarily accompanied by an increase of phasic electrodermal activity"; a later systematic review of emotional piloerection found sympathetic indices "abundant" (seen only in a search summary). Flagged as an inference in the script's wording. |

## Checked and NOT used
- **"Goosebumps" etymology (goose skin)**: Darwin uses "goose-skin" ("our own goose-skin during the chill before a fever-fit"); the origin of the English word beyond that is not in a source I read. Not used.
- **Darwin's peccary and the stuffed snake** ("the hair rose in a wonderful manner along its back"): verified, a second snake line would be a documentary stack.
- **Darwin's chimpanzee alarmed at a coalheaver**: verified text, cut because Darwin describes the man in racial terms.
- **Harvard press-release quote from Hsu ("dual-component niche")**: only seen in a reprint; not needed.
- **Blood & Zatorre 2001 (PET, chills and reward regions)**: cited inside Benedek and Sachs; not read directly.
- **Craig 2005 ("shivers down the spine… a necessary but not sufficient concomitant of visible piloerection")**: quoted in Benedek's introduction; used only implicitly (the camera-versus-report fact instead).
- **Gender difference in goosebumps (47 % of women, no men)**: only seven men; not claimed.
- **Baldness treatment speculation** (press coverage of the Cell paper): the paper does not claim it; not used.
