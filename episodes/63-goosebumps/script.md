# chapter: Thirty-one degrees
This is Dave. It is thirty-one degrees, and Dave is at his sister's wedding, in a suit, sweating gently into a hymn sheet. // [J]
Then the choir comes in, and every hair on Dave's forearm stands to attention. //
Dave is not cold. Dave is not scared. Dave is, if anything, slightly overcooked. // [J]
So why do we get goosebumps? Why does your skin run a cold-weather emergency routine for a song? //
The answer involves a muscle on every hair, a stuffed snake in a monkey house, and a Harvard result that rescues the useless part. Let's start with the muscle. //

# chapter: A muscle for every hair
Almost every hair on your body has a tiny muscle attached to its base, called the arrector pili. / Darwin described it as one of the minute, unstriped, involuntary muscles. //
When a nerve tells the muscle to squeeze, it pulls the hair upright and dimples the skin around it. / That dimple is the goosebump. Doctors call it piloerection. //
The nerve belongs to the sympathetic system, the one that runs your fight-or-flight settings. You don't get a vote. // [J]
In eighteen seventy-two, Darwin called it a reflex action, independent of the will. / He noted that it fires in the cold, in terror and in rage, and even when a neighbouring patch of skin is tickled. //
Dave tries to do it on purpose. He stares at his own forearm and orders it to stand up. //
Nothing. The arm is unmoved. The choir had better luck. // [J]
Dave then holds the arm in front of the open freezer. The hairs rise at once. Same bumps, different trigger. He considers this a controlled experiment. // [J]
So what is this reflex for? //

# chapter: The fur we lost
In furry animals, standing hair does two jobs. It traps a layer of air against the skin, which keeps the animal warm. //
And when the animal is angry or afraid, it makes the animal look bigger. / Darwin wrote that it serves to make the animal appear larger and more frightful to its enemies. //
He tested this the way Darwin tested things. He took a stuffed snake into the monkey house, and the hair on several species instantly stood up, especially on their tails. // [J]
He also remembered his mules and dogs after a night on the bleak Andes. / Their hair was as erect as under the greatest terror. Cold and terror look identical on a mule. // [J]
Humans lost most of the fur and kept the wiring. Researchers describe our goosebumps as a relic of the thermoregulation of our furred ancestors. //
Dave's raised arm hairs now trap roughly no air at all. It's a fur coat with no fur. // [J]
"It's thirty-one degrees. I'm not cold. And that choir is not a snake." (@dave) //
So cold and fear are covered. A wedding choir is a third thing. //

# chapter: Chills without cold
Researchers call the music version frisson, which is French for shiver. They've been mapping it for decades. //
In nineteen ninety-one, the psychologist John Sloboda asked eighty-three music lovers about their body's reactions. More than eighty percent reported shivers down the spine. //
Then he had them find the passages that reliably did it. Shivers were most reliably set off by new or unexpected harmonies. //
Not the loudest part, and not the saddest. / The part where the music does something you didn't see coming, and it turns out you wanted it to. //
Later work added crescendos, and the entry of a solo voice, a choir, or an extra instrument. A choir is on the list. Dave's arm had read the research. // [J]
A big review of this field concludes that at most nine in ten people can get music chills at all. / So at least one in ten never does. //
For those who can, it's frequent. / In one week of sampling, about eight in ten people had at least one music chill, and chills turned up in about one in seven listening sessions. //
Dave checks his playlist. Every goosebump moment is a chord he didn't see coming. His arm is an unexpected-chord detector. //
And your brain pays you in advance. / In two thousand eleven, Valorie Salimpoor's team scanned listeners and found dopamine released in the striatum at the chills, and in a different spot during the build-up just before. //
"I release the good stuff before the chorus, Dave. You're welcome." (@brain) // [J]
It isn't only music. In two thousand eleven, Mathias Benedek and Christian Kaernbach filmed the forearms of fifty students while they listened to film soundtracks and music. //
Forty percent grew visible goosebumps. Film set them off in about a quarter of trials, and music in about one in nine. //
They tested two ideas. Goosebumps as a marker of peak arousal didn't fit. / Goosebumps as a social, being-moved feeling fit better, and film is more social than a song. //
So goosebumps may be a marker of being moved. And your arm doesn't always tell you. / In about a third of the trials where the camera saw goosebumps, the students said they hadn't felt any. The camera noticed. They hadn't. // [J]

# chapter: Who gets them
So why not everybody? Dave's friend Priya has heard the same choir every Sunday for years. Not a hair. //
In two thousand sixteen, Matthew Sachs and colleagues surveyed two hundred and thirty-seven people. / They picked ten who often get music chills and ten who rarely do, matched for personality and musical training. //
Scans showed more nerve fibres linking the hearing area to the regions that handle feelings, about twice the volume on the right side. //
Ten against ten is a crowd you can seat at one table, and the authors call some of the links preliminary. / But it fits: Priya's ears and Priya's feelings are connected, just by a quieter road. // [J]
Another study had a hundred students listen to five pieces chosen to give chills, and counted the chills by what they said and by skin sensors. / People high in openness to experience, the curious and imaginative sort, got more of them. //
Priya's skin works fine, by the way. She gets goosebumps at the cold end of the lake. / None of which makes her less moved. It may only mean that her moved looks different. // [J]
But there's a bigger puzzle than the choir. //

# chapter: The leftover that wasn't
If the reflex is a leftover, why is it still here? / Evolution tends to trim what nothing uses. Yet every hair on Dave's arm still has its own employee. // [J]
In twenty twenty, a team led by Ya-Chieh Hsu at Harvard studied exactly that muscle in mice, and published it in the journal Cell. //
They found that the muscle and the sympathetic nerve are a team. / The nerve forms synapse-like contacts with the hair's stem cells, and the muscle keeps the nerve in place. //
"So the goosebump muscle is really a coat hook for a nerve?" (@dave) // [J]
Sort of. In ordinary conditions, the nerve keeps the stem cells gently primed. Without its signal, they fall into a deeper sleep. //
During development, the hair follicle builds this team itself, using a signal called Sonic Hedgehog. Yes, that is the real name. // [J]
Cold doesn't only give a mouse goosebumps. Mice kept cold for two weeks began growing new hair ahead of schedule. //
Remove the muscle, and the nerve contact to the stem cells is lost, and the new coat arrives late. // [J]
So the goosebump isn't a leftover with no job. It's the part of a working system that you can see. //
The authors add that the same arrangement exists across mammals, including humans, where goosebumps no longer keep us warm. / They say that raises the possibility of additional functions. //
That is a mouse result. Nobody has shown the same in people, and it doesn't explain the choir. //
But the same sympathetic nerve that fires in the cold also fires when you're moved. / At thirty-one degrees, Dave's skin ran the winter programme for a song. // [J]
His stem cells, we can safely say, stood to attention. //

# chapter: Stood down
So Dave stops treating the ripple as a malfunction. / It's a reflex for a coat he doesn't have, wired into a system that still has a job, and it goes off when something moves him. //
Dave rolls his sleeve down. The hairs lie flat. //
At the reception, he stands up for the toast and gets as far as the words my little sister before both forearms rise together. This time, he lets them. //
The choir finishes the verse and begins another. Dave doesn't look at his arm. //
(slow) The hair stands up again. It stands down, later.
