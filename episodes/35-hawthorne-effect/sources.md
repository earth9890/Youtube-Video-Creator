## Checked and NOT used
- **Donald Chipman, Western Electric supervisor, 1931:** "They say figures don't lie, but we have shown that we can take a set of figures and prove anything we want to." (HBS 03.html). A perfect line for the twist, cut for length: it pushed the twist below 70 %.
- **Elton Mayo "proved" attention raises output / "productivity continued to rise" when poor lighting returned (Blalock & Blalock 1982):** this is the legend Levitt & List tested. The script tells it as the textbook version and then corrects it, instead of stating it as fact.
- **"Output rose about 30 %" in the relay room:** no single figure in R&D 1939. The book gives Operator 4's rate as up ~20 relays an hour, "approximately 40 per cent" by Period XIII, and Brannigan & Zwerman give ~50 → 60–70 relays an hour. The script uses "the line went up" plus the Period XII nuance instead of a headline percentage.
- **The replacement operator's family story** (her mother had died and her father and brother faced unemployment; Brannigan & Zwerman; Carey 1967): real and relevant to the money explanation, but personal hardship of a named real person. Left out under the "nothing mean / no suffering" rule.
- **Alex Carey 1967 ("conclusions… almost wholly unsupported") and Stephen Jones 1992 ("essentially no evidence of Hawthorne effects")** in the relay data: support the twist; the script uses the more concrete Levitt & List findings plus the pay change in their place. Bloombaum (1983) disputed Franke & Kaul, which is one more reason to keep the twist at "hints of something real" rather than "zero".
- **Bank wiring room (14 men, 1931–32, informal output quota, no productivity change):** a good counter-example, cut for length.
- **Interview programme (~21,000 employees):** true (Brannigan & Zwerman), not part of the story's question.
- **Izawa, French & Hedge 2011 (*Human Factors*), a second re-analysis of the illumination archive at Cornell:** found in search, not read in full; not used.
- **Eastland disaster (1915, Hawthorne Works employee picnic):** a real Hawthorne tragedy, off-topic and grim.
- **"The Hawthorne effect was coined by Henry Landsberger in 1958":** widely repeated, but McCambridge and Wikipedia trace the term to a 1953 methods text by J. R. P. French. Script: "In the nineteen fifties, the idea got a name, and sociologist Henry Landsberger helped make it famous."
