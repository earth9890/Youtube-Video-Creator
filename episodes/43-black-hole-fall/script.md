# chapter: Feet first
This is Dave. Dave has decided to fall into a black hole, feet first, like getting into a cold pool. // [J]
He has even bought a postcard. On the back: greetings from inside a black hole. //
Small problem. Nothing has ever come back out of a black hole. Not light, not rockets, and probably not the post. // [J]
So what would happen if you fell into a black hole? Would you turn into spaghetti? Would time stop? And what is inside? /
Tonight we follow Dave all the way down. Far out, the sky bends. Near the edge, his friend watches him freeze. And inside, every direction points the same way. //
His friend is staying on the ship, to report live from a sensible distance. /
"Good evening. A local man is falling into a black hole. On purpose." (@news) // [J]

# chapter: The one in the photo
Dave isn't falling into just any black hole. He has picked the one in the photo. /
In twenty twenty-two, the Event Horizon Telescope, radio dishes across the Earth working as one, / revealed the first picture of the black hole at the centre of our galaxy, Sagittarius A star. /
A dark shadow in a glowing ring. Four million times heavier than the Sun, and twenty-seven thousand light-years away. /
From Earth, it looks about as big as a doughnut on the Moon. So Dave is going for a closer look. // [J]
In twenty twenty-four, NASA's Jeremy Schnittman used a supercomputer to fly a virtual camera into a black hole just like this one, from six hundred and forty million kilometres out. /
The first strange thing is the sky. Gravity this strong bends light, so the stars behind the black hole get smeared into arcs. /
Line a star up exactly behind it, and its light becomes a ring round the black hole, called an Einstein ring. /
Even the shadow is partly a trick of the light. It looks about twice as big as the black hole really is. //
Closer in, light itself can go round in circles. That happens at one and a half times the radius of the edge, a place called the photon sphere. /
Inside it, nothing can stay in orbit, not even light. You keep your rockets firing, or you fall. Dave brought a postcard. // [J]
In NASA's simulation, the fall to the edge takes about three hours, with nearly two laps on the way. //

# chapter: The tug-of-war
All the way down, Dave is worried about one thing. Everyone knows what black holes do to people. They turn them into spaghetti. //
Gravity gets stronger the closer you get. Dave is falling feet first, so his feet get pulled a little harder than his head. /
His feet and his head are in a tug-of-war, and the feet have a black hole on their side. // [J]
The Moon does the same to Earth. It pulls hardest on the side facing it, and the oceans bulge. That's the tides. /
And the stretch grows fast. Halve your distance from the centre, and the stretch gets eight times stronger. /
It squeezes you from the sides as well. Long and thin. Astrophysicists really do call this spaghettification. // [J]
And it isn't just a cartoon. In twenty twenty, the European Southern Observatory watched a black hole do it to a star, / two hundred and fifteen million light-years away. It shredded the star into thin streams. //
Even small black holes do it. One of about ten suns would stretch you into spaghetti long before you reached its edge. /
Dave's black hole is four hundred thousand times heavier than that. Dave does the maths, and goes very quiet. // [J]
"Excuse me. Is it too late to pick a smaller black hole?" (@dave) // [J]
"We'll be right back, assuming Dave is still in one piece." (@news) // [J]

# chapter: The edge
Dave checks his feet every few minutes. Still attached, still the usual distance from his head. So far. / [J]
Ahead is the edge, the event horizon. For Dave's black hole, it's about twenty-five million kilometres across, a sixth of the way from Earth to the Sun. /
Inside it, you'd need to go faster than light to get back out, and nothing goes faster than light. //
From here, the story splits in two: what Dave's friend sees from the ship, and what Dave feels. /
Gravity slows time. Even on Earth, clocks on GPS satellites tick faster than ours, by about thirty-eight millionths of a second a day. /
Mostly, that's because gravity is weaker up there. If nobody corrected for it, satnav would drift by about ten kilometres a day. /
Near a black hole, it's far stronger. Dave flashes his torch once a second. On the ship, the flashes arrive further and further apart, / and his light turns redder and dimmer. /
"Breaking news. Dave has stopped moving, and he's gone a little red." (@news) // [J]
His friend never sees him cross. In theory, Dave hangs at the edge forever, fading until there's nothing left to see. //
Dave was counting on this. He has a plan. /
"If time stops out here, I can watch the whole future of the universe go by." (@dave) //
And time near a black hole really does stretch. NASA worked out that an astronaut who swooped close to this one and flew back out, a six-hour trip, /
would come home thirty-six minutes younger than the friends she left behind. But Dave isn't coming back out. //
Which leaves one last worry. In twenty twelve, four physicists argued that quantum physics might put a wall of fire at the edge. /
Their paper said perhaps the infalling observer burns up at the horizon. Others pushed back hard, and it's still argued about. //
Dave's own watch ticks perfectly normally. He braces his feet. A thousand kilometres to go. A hundred. Ten. //

# chapter: The giant is the gentle one
Then he crosses the event horizon, and feels absolutely nothing. //
"Was that it? I didn't feel a thing. Where's the edge?" (@dave) /
That was it. Andrew Hamilton at the University of Colorado animated the view as you cross. There's no sign of the edge at all. / His own caption says: yes, the animation is boring. // [J]
So where's the spaghetti? Here's the twist: the giant is the gentle one. //
The stretch depends on how fast gravity changes from your feet to your head. / At a giant's edge, millions of kilometres from the centre, it barely changes across a body. /
Hamilton did the sums. Above about thirty thousand suns, the stretch at the edge is weaker than Earth's own gravity. Dave's has four million. //
It still shreds stars, because stars are huge. Dave is under two metres. For once, being short helps. // [J]
NASA's Schnittman has some advice. If you have the choice, you want to fall into a supermassive black hole. / Good thing nobody let Dave swap. // [J]
As for the fire, nobody has checked. Except Dave, and Dave can't tell anyone. // [J]

# chapter: Every way is down
Inside, it isn't even dark. Dave can still see the stars behind him, but there's no fast-forward through the future. So much for the plan. // [J]
When he turns round to head back, the centre is still in front of him. /
The physicist Sean Carroll puts it this way: you can no more stop moving toward the singularity than you can stop getting older. /
In NASA's simulation, the camera lasts about thirteen seconds past the edge. Dave spends them on the postcard. /
"Nothing special at the door. Lovely view. Wish you were here." (@dave) //
Then the stretch finally catches up with him. The spaghetti was just running late. // [J]
And the very centre? Our best physics stops working there, so nobody knows. //

# chapter: The postcard
So what would happen if you fell into a black hole? In a small one, spaghetti before the door. / In a giant one, a quiet crossing, a fading torch, and thirteen strange seconds. //
And the postcard? In nineteen seventy-four, Stephen Hawking showed that black holes aren't completely black. They glow faintly, and slowly shrink away. /
Dave's would take about ten to the power of eighty-seven years to vanish. Does his postcard come out in that glow? It's one of the most famous puzzles in physics. /
Hawking first said no. In two thousand four, he lost a bet on it, and paid with an encyclopedia, from which information can be freely retrieved. // [J]
Later he said it does come back out, in a chaotic and useless form. So the postcard will probably arrive. Scrambled, and very late. //
Hawking put it kindly: if you feel you are in a black hole, don't give up. There's a way out. //
(slow) Greetings from inside a black hole. Wish you were here.
