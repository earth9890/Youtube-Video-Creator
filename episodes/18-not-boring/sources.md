# Episode 18 "You Are Not Boring": sources (verified 2026-10-14)

Main paper (read in full): Boothby E.J., Cooney G., Sandstrom G.M., Clark M.S., "The Liking Gap in Conversations: Do People Like Us More Than We Think?", *Psychological Science* 2018;29(11):1742-1756, doi 10.1177/0956797618783714. Authors at Cornell, Harvard, Essex and Yale. PDF: https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2020/05/The-Liking-Gap-in-Conversations.pdf · Record: https://repository.essex.ac.uk/23025/ · APS summary: https://www.psychologicalscience.org/?p=148177

| Script line | Claim | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Dave, Maya, the party, the printer, "replayed it nine times" | Story illustration, not a study result. The worry itself is the paper's own example: after chats people wonder "Did I talk too much?" "Did they think I was boring?" and replay them self-critically ("My banter wasn't witty enough"). | Paper, introduction (p. 1742-1743) |
| "But chats are polite. Nobody says the real score." | The paper: "conversations are conspiracies of politeness in which people do not reveal their true feelings" (citing Blumberg 1972; Brown & Levinson 1987; and others), so it is hard to know what partners really think. | Paper, introduction (p. 1742) |
| "In twenty eighteen, Erica Boothby's team asked strangers after a five-minute chat…" | Five studies. Study 1a: pairs of strangers chatted ~5 min, then rated how much they liked the partner and how much the partner liked them (7-point scales). Actual liking 5.82 vs perceived 5.17, p < .001. The authors: since it "cannot logically be true" that people on average like their partners more than their partners like them, the gap is a mistake. | Paper, Study 1a, Fig. 1 |
| "How interesting was your partner? Four point three, out of five. How interesting did they find you? Three point six." | Study 4: 100 members of the public at UK "How to Talk to Strangers" workshops, 5-point scale (not at all to extremely interesting), after a ~5-minute introductory chat with a stranger. Partner rated as interesting: M = 4.30. Guess of how interesting the partner found them: M = 3.61 (difference 0.68, p < .001). Before the chat the gap was 0.37; after talking it grew. | Paper, Study 4, Fig. 4 |
| "Same chat, two scores." | Each person's own rating of the partner vs their guess about themselves, from the same conversation. | As above |
| "Coders watching films of chats could see who liked whom." | Study 1b: two trained coders, blind to the hypothesis, watched 15 videotaped Study 1a conversations. Their ratings predicted how much people actually liked each other (b = 0.71, p < .001) but not how much people thought they were liked. People did signal liking; partners neglected the signals ("neglected-signal account"). | Paper, Study 1b |
| "The talkers missed it, busy replaying their own printer stories." | Study 2 (84 Yale students and recent graduates, free 5-min chats): people's top thoughts about how the partner saw them were more negative than their thoughts about the partner, and this negativity partly explained (mediated) the gap. Authors: people are "too focused on their own self-critical thoughts to notice". The printer is Dave's illustration. | Paper, Study 2, Fig. 2; overview p. 1743 |
| "So I just need longer chats? No. From two minutes to forty-five, same gap." | Study 3 (102 people, Harvard Decision Science Lab): pairs talked as long as they liked, 2 to 45 minutes (mean 23). Longer chats meant more liking overall, but conversation length did not change the size of the liking gap (interaction p = .37) or of the enjoyment gap (p = .33). | Paper, Study 3, Fig. 3 |
| "Across town, Maya is replaying it too, sure she was the boring one." | Story illustration of the finding that the gap is an average across *both* members of each pair: each person, on average, underestimates how the other sees them. Not a claim about any individual. | Paper, Studies 1a-4 (both partners rate each other) |
| "After your next chat, assume they liked you more than you think. Then talk to them again." | The one safe action, drawn from the paper's conclusion ("after people have conversations, they are liked more than they know"). No miracle claim and no number promised. | Paper, abstract and discussion |

## Checked and NOT used
- **Study 5 (Yale suite mates):** 102 first-years surveyed in Sept, Oct, Dec, Feb and May; the gap was significant at the first four time points and gone by May. Real and strong, but cut for length (the "longer chats" beat makes the same point).
- **Shyness:** in Study 1a the gap was biggest for shy people (1.14) and not significant for the least shy third (0.25). This is why the script never says "everyone".
- **Enjoyment gap:** Study 3 also found people think partners enjoyed the chat less than they did. Implied, not stated separately.
- **Mastroianni, Cooney, Boothby & Reece (2021)**, "The liking gap in groups and teams", *Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes* 162:109-122: the gap also appears in groups and work teams. Supports generality; not needed. https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jobhdp/v162y2021icp109-122.html
- **Liking gap in children:** a secondary source says it appears from about age 5; I could not verify the primary citation, so it is not used.
- **Rating scales:** 4.3 and 3.6 are means on a 1-5 scale, not percentages; the script says "out of five".
