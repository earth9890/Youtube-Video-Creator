# Solid vs inference, as said in the script: Weisman's timeline is a scholar-journalist's projection built on interviews with engineers, NOT a measurement; the script says so ("Weisman's projection", "he expected"). The real-world check is Chernobyl: a human-free landscape since 1986, with a census of its wildlife. Weisman's claim that nuclear plants would fail is DELIBERATELY NOT used (contested and unsettling). What a lone survivor would eat or how they would survive is not claimed. The ending (the dog) is the story's, not a prediction about any person.
# SAFETY: gentle, not grim: no death on screen, nobody "disappears" in a way that hurts anyone; Dave is a story device. The loneliness is played for warmth and comedy; the ending is a companion and a tree. The script says plainly that Weisman himself did not want everyone gone: it is a way of looking at what we do to the world.

# chapter: Hour one
This is Dave. Dave woke up this morning, and everyone is gone. Eight billion people. All of them. Except Dave. //
"Dear diary. Hour one. I walked into the shop. Nobody has asked me to leave." (@dave) // [J]
So what would happen if you were the last person on Earth? / Not to you. To the world. Who runs it when nobody is running it? //
The writer Alan Weisman asked this in a book in two thousand seven. He said he removed everyone at the start, so readers could stop worrying and watch what happens next. // [J]
Here is the plan. Dave keeps a diary, and every entry jumps further ahead: hours, days, months, decades, centuries. / For the purposes of the diary, Dave does not age. His only light is a lamp in his window. // [J]

# chapter: The first two days
Hour six. Everything still looks fine. Buildings stand, shops are full, and the lights are on. But the lights are only on because people keep them on. //
Weisman's projection: much of our power comes from plants with automatic fail-safe switches, so they don't run out of control if nobody is watching. / The power goes off. And once it does, he says, the pumps stop working. //
Take New York. Even on a clear sunny day, the people who run the subway pump about thirteen million gallons of groundwater out of it. / Nearly eight hundred pumps do the work. //
When the power goes off, they go silent. Within forty-eight hours, he says, you have a lot of flooding. //
"Day two. The subway is a river. I am, technically, near a river." (@dave) // [J]
That is less strange than it sounds. / Manhattan used to be hilly, with about forty streams. The hills were flattened and the streams pushed underground. They are only waiting to come back. //
Sewers clog quickly, first with all the plastic bags blowing around, later with leaf litter. // [J]
Weisman's lesson from the engineers was a kind one. / Our huge, imposing infrastructure seems indestructible, but it is a fairly fragile idea that keeps working thanks to a few people on whom the rest of us depend. //
"Day three. I tried every light switch in the building. The building and I are no longer speaking." (@dave) // [J]

# chapter: The first year
Month three. Weisman expected grand boulevards and superhighways to crack and buckle within months. Water gets in, freezes, and nobody patches it. //
The first species to leave are not the ones you would expect. / Cockroaches in northern cities depend on the heat inside buildings to get through winter. With the heat off, they have nowhere to go. // [J]
In warm places like Florida, they stay. //
Rats that live on our leftovers hunt for food, and become food. Hawks and other raptors move in to the cities. //
"Month five. A hawk is watching me. It looks at me the way I look at lunch." (@dave) // [J]
"Month seven. The cockroaches left. I miss them. That's how bad it has got." (@dave) // [J]
The crops go wild. Carrots, left alone, revert to Queen Anne's lace, which is a flower, not a root. // [J]
Dave looks at his garden. Dave has no idea what to do with a flower. //

# chapter: The pets
Now, the question everyone is asking. What about the pets? //
Cats have the best odds. In the Australian bush alone, there are between one point four and five point six million feral cats, depending on the rain. / And they manage without anyone feeding them. //
Dogs are harder to call. About three in four of the world's dogs already roam free, some owned and some not. / But roaming free is not the same as living without people, and nobody knows how many would manage. //
The cats, if asked, would say: finally. // [J]

# chapter: The decades
Fast forward. Year twenty. Steel columns hold up the streets of New York. They corrode, and the streets begin to crater. / Trees take root in the leaf litter in the gutters, and crumbling concrete adds lime to the soil, so more plants can move in. //
Buildings that were never designed to be waterlogged topple, and take a couple of neighbours down with them, clearing space for seeds to blow in. / A city starts to grow its own little ecosystem. //
Bridges need painting and cleaning constantly. Without it, debris and rust jam the expansion joints, bolts shear, and bridges begin to fall into the water beneath them. //
Dams and dikes need people too. The dams along the Panama Canal need their floodgates opened from time to time, or they erode. / And Weisman says the dikes of Holland will eventually flood again, because we have built nothing permanent that holds back the sea. //
"Year twenty. I've stopped going out. The bridge went in the river. Honestly, I think it needed a rest." (@dave) //

# chapter: The real tests
This is a projection. But the world has run a few small tests. / In Cyprus, a seaside resort of hotels and big balconies was fenced off after a war in nineteen seventy-four. //
When Weisman visited, thirty-three years later, the buildings were beyond saving, and the beach was full of sea turtles. The resort is fully booked. // [J]
In the Korean demilitarized zone, two of the world's biggest armies face each other. Soldiers shout through loudspeakers, and in the middle, flocks of cranes spend the winter. // [J]
In Europe, a forest on the border of Poland and Belarus was set aside as a game reserve in the thirteen hundreds, and became a park after the Second World War. / Weisman found oaks and ashes nearly a hundred and fifty feet tall, with wolves, elk and the last wild herd of European bison. //
And in April nineteen eighty-six, the city of Pripyat was evacuated after the Chernobyl accident. By twenty seventeen, a reporter described it as having turned into a pine wood. //
Only small patches of cracked asphalt and concrete still recall the human presence. //

# chapter: The twist
Here is the twist. The empty places are not empty. In twenty fifteen, scientists counted the large mammals in the Chernobyl exclusion zone. //
Elk, roe deer, red deer and wild boar were about as common as in nearby nature reserves. And wolves were more than seven times as common. //
One of the team was careful. / It doesn't mean radiation is good for wildlife. It means the effects of people, including hunting, farming and forestry, are a lot worse. //
So a world without us is not quiet. It is crowded. // [J]
"Year thirty. I thought I was alone. I have counted fourteen deer." (@dave) //
Weisman put it simply: without humans, North America would probably become a giant deer habitat. //
Dave was never the last one standing. He was just the last one who looked up. //

# chapter: What lasts
So what would be left of us? Weisman has a list. //
More than a billion tons of plastic have been made, and nearly all of it is still here, in smaller and smaller pieces. //
Stainless-steel pots could last for millennia. Certain common plastics might last for hundreds of thousands of years, until microbes learn to eat them. // [J]
The longest-lasting thing Dave owns is a pot. // [J]
Concrete and steel do not last. Stone does. Machu Picchu's stones fit so well that gravity holds them, not mortar. //
Weisman thinks a pyramid, left to erode like any mountain, could stand for a million years or more, getting steadily lower. // [J]
And in time there will be another ice age, he says, and the ice will scrape Manhattan clean again. //
And it would take around a hundred thousand years for all the carbon dioxide we have added to be absorbed again, though most of that happens in the first thousand. //
Dave writes: so I will be remembered by a pot and a lot of plastic bags. // [J]

# chapter: The dog
Weisman said something kind about all of this. He never wanted us gone. He called humans a valuable species, as valuable as any other. //
Dave blows out the lamp, and lights a small fire in the window instead. //
Something is standing at the edge of the light. Four legs, a long nose, and a very good sense of when someone has food. //
It sits down next to him. It is, probably, not waiting for anyone. It is just here. //
"To whoever reads this: the world is fine. Plant a tree. P.S. The dog is staying." (@dave) //
Everyone is gone, and Dave leaves the light on in the window.
