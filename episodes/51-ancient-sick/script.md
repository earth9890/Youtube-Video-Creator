# chapter: Left behind
This is Dave. Dave has a fever, a pounding head, and no idea why he is lying in a cave about fifty thousand years ago. //
He does have one firm belief. In the Stone Age, if you got sick, you got left behind. //
"Survival of the fittest. I'm the least fit person here. This is where it ends." (@dave) // [J]
His plan is to look as healthy as possible. The fever is not cooperating. // [J]
So what did ancient humans really do when someone got sick? //
The answer runs through a man with one arm, a skull with one tooth, / an operation older than farming, and a famous quote that may never have been said. //
But first, a problem. Fevers leave no marks on bones. Only broken, worn and healed things do, so that's where the evidence has to come from. //
Dave, who would like it noted that he is only a little bit sick, asks to start with the worst case. // [J]

# chapter: The man with one arm
In nineteen fifty-seven, the archaeologist Ralph Solecki dug up a Neanderthal skeleton in Shanidar Cave, in Iraqi Kurdistan. /
Scientists call him Shanidar One, and he lived about fifty thousand years ago. //
His list of injuries is long. A crushing blow to the left eye socket, probably leaving him with poor vision on that side. /
A right arm that withered and was most likely amputated above the elbow. A damaged right leg and foot, and a limp. //
"Okay. Mine is just a cold." (@dave) // [J]
In twenty seventeen, Erik Trinkaus and Sébastien Villotte added one more. /
Bony growths had grown across both his ear canals, enough to cause serious hearing loss. //
Trinkaus said his deafness "would have made him easy prey for the ubiquitous carnivores in his environment." /
That's a man who couldn't hunt, couldn't hear the lion, and couldn't keep up. And he lived into his forties. //
Someone, apparently, was listening for the lion on his behalf. // [J]
Those injuries had all healed. And healing a bone takes weeks of rest, and weeks of somebody else doing your jobs. //

# chapter: One tooth
But Shanidar One is a Neanderthal, and Dave suspects people back then were softer. So the story goes back much further. //
At Dmanisi, in the country of Georgia, a team led by David Lordkipanidze found a skull about one point seven seven million years old. /
In two thousand five, they reported that this man had lost every tooth but one, years before he died, because the sockets had filled in with bone. //
He couldn't chew. To survive he'd need soft food: bone marrow, brains, soft plants. /
The authors thought he might have managed alone, but that other people may have helped, because of his age or illness. //
A diet of marrow and brains. The original soft food menu. // [J]
Not everyone is sure that tooth loss proves care. Wild apes survive without an eye, without a hand, even with most of their teeth gone. /
Apes, it turns out, are tougher than Dave. // [J]
Trinkaus and Villotte replied that the Dmanisi jaw wasn't only missing teeth. It was badly infected too. /
And a deaf man with a bad arm and a bad leg, all at once, is a different problem from one missing eye. //
That's a debate, not a verdict. //

# chapter: The leg
In twenty twenty-two, researchers reported a skeleton from Liang Tebo, a limestone cave in Borneo. /
A young person, whose left leg and foot were gone from partway down the shin. //
The bones said the leg had been cut off at least thirty-one thousand years ago, probably when they were a child. /
The cut was clean and straight, not the crushed mess of an accident or an animal attack. //
Dave asks if it was a crocodile. The paper says no. A crocodile bite would have gone badly infected, and this leg showed no sign of infection. // [J]
The child lived another six to nine years in a rugged mountain landscape, then was carefully buried in the same cave. //
The oldest earlier case of amputation was a French farmer, about seven thousand years ago. Borneo beat it by twenty-four thousand years. /
That's not breaking a record. That's lapping it. // [J]
The authors write that the challenges of life without that leg were probably "overcome by a high degree of community care." //
And long care has a record too. At Man Bac, in Vietnam, a man was buried about four thousand years ago. /
He had been paralysed from the waist down, with almost no upper body movement, for at least a decade. //
The researcher Lorna Tilley said he needed intensive nursing: food, water, washing, and preventing bed sores. /
A hospital with no building, no budget and no shifts. // [J]
Dave has quietly stopped mentioning his cold. // [J]

# chapter: The medicine cabinet
But Dave doesn't have a broken leg. He has a fever. Is there any sign of ancient medicine? //
In twenty seventeen, Laura Weyrich's team read the DNA trapped in the tooth plaque of five Neanderthals. /
One, from El Sidrón in Spain, had a dental abscess and a gut parasite. //
His plaque also held DNA from poplar, a tree whose bark contains the painkiller chemical in aspirin, and from Penicillium, a mould that can make an antibiotic. //
The authors suggest he may have been treating himself. That's a suggestion, not proof. Plaque can't tell you whether he chewed it on purpose. //
Or whether he just had a bad day near a tree. // [J]
Dave offers to go and find some poplar bark. Dave does not know what a poplar looks like. // [J]

# chapter: The famous femur
Dave remembers a quote, and says it with great confidence. /
"The first sign of civilization is a healed femur. Margaret Mead said so." (@dave) //
You've probably seen it. A student asks the anthropologist what the first sign of civilization is. She says it isn't a pot or a tool. It's a broken thighbone that healed. //
No wild animal survives a broken leg long enough to mend, so someone must have stayed with that person. /
It's been in Forbes, in The Economist, and in a university commencement speech. It has been quoted far more often than it has been checked. // [J]
There is just one problem. Nobody can find Margaret Mead saying it. //
The earliest version anyone has found is in a book from nineteen eighty by the surgeon Paul Brand. /
He remembered a lecture he'd heard years earlier. Mead had died two years before the book came out. //
Nobody has found a recording or a transcript. // [J]
And in a nineteen sixty-eight interview, a student asked Mead directly when a culture becomes a civilization. /
Her answer was great cities, a division of labour, and some form of keeping records. //
So her documented definition of civilization is a city with a tax office. Less touching. // [J]
A twenty twelve book even gives the femur a fifteen-thousand-year-old site. Which site? It doesn't say. /
The story got older. The source never turned up. // [J]
"So it's all made up? Then they really would have left me." (@dave) //
No. The quote is shaky. The bones are not. //

# chapter: What a bone can't say
Healed bones turn up in other primates, and chimpanzees have been seen treating each other's wounds with insects. //
And a healed bone doesn't prove kindness. It proves time. Someone stayed alive long enough for bone to grow back, and the bone can't say why. /
Tilley thinks care needed no special reason. Her view is that being part of the community was enough. No résumé required. //
There's one more catch, and it's ours. A healed bone only records someone who got looked after. A person who was left behind leaves no healed bone at all. //
So the skeletons can't count the abandoned. They can only show us the ones who weren't. //
Here's what the evidence honestly says. Where we can see it, in Georgia, Iraq, Borneo and Vietnam, people were kept alive for months, years, and once for at least a decade. //

# chapter: Dave stays
By morning, Dave's fever has broken. Nobody left him. Nobody even brought it up. //
So what did ancient humans do when someone got sick? When we can see it at all, they stayed. They fed them, kept them warm, and did their jobs for them. //
And then, in the cave, at breakfast, the person next to Dave starts coughing. //
Dave, who spent all night sure he'd be left behind, looks at the exit. Then he looks at the cough, and picks up the soup. //
(slow) A healed bone is just time, and time is the one thing nobody gives away. //
(slow) Nobody gets left behind. Dave stays.
