# Episode 61 "Why Does Time Speed Up As You Get Older?": sources (verified 2026-10-22)

Abstracts and full text read where marked; everything else from the named abstract or secondary coverage. Dave, the seaside week, the dentist's waiting room, the juggling and the sticker calendar are invented story devices; the script claims nothing from them.

| Script line | Claim | Source (exact quote) |
|---|---|---|
| "At eight, a year was an eighth of his whole life. Now it's a thirty-ninth… about five times thinner." | Arithmetic: 39 / 8 = 4.9. Dave's own sum, not a finding. | n/a |
| "In eighteen seventy-seven, the French philosopher Paul Janet wrote a short paper called 'An Internal Optical Illusion.' His idea: how fast time feels is proportional to how old you are." | Janet 1877, *Revue Philosophique* 3, 497–502, "Une illusion d'optique interne" (the English title in the script is our translation of the French; Wearden's English translation of most of it exists). | Wearden 2021, *Timing & Time Perception* 10(3): "it is proposed that the rate of passage of subjective time is proportional to the age of the person making the judgement." https://brill.com/view/journals/time/10/3/article-p279_006.pdf (open abstract) |
| "A year feels like a twentieth of your life when you're twenty, and a sixtieth when you're sixty." | The ratio model. | Friedman & Janssen 2010, full text: "Janet (1877, cited in Fraisse (1963)) suggested a ratio model, in which the past year was subjectively 1/20 for a 20-year-old but 1/60 for a 60-year old." https://researchmap.jp/read0153473/published_papers/1402627/attachment_file.pdf |
| "In nineteen seventy-five, Robert Lemlich tried a gentler version, using a square root. He polled students and older adults… and found generally good agreement." | Lemlich 1975, *Perceptual and Motor Skills* 41:235. | Abstract: "the subjective duration of an interval of real time varies inversely with the square root of the total real time (age). This result was tested by polling college students and older adults as to their recollections regarding the relative apparent rate of the passage of time at various ages. Generally good agreement was obtained." https://doi.org/10.2466/pms.1975.41.1.235 |
| "Remembering a feeling from thirty years back is not the same as having had it." | Our reasoning, backed by the reviewers. | Friedman & Janssen 2010: "few developmental psychologists would trust people's ability to remember with accuracy their experiences from so long ago." |
| "The psychologist Claudia Hammond calls this the holiday paradox." | Name and claim. | Scientific American, July 2016: "This phenomenon, which Hammond has dubbed the holiday paradox, seems to present one of the best clues as to why, in retrospect, time seems to pass more quickly the older we get." https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/why-does-time-seem-to-speed-up-with-age/ ; Telegraph, 21 Apr 2012 ("'Holiday paradox' explains why time flies", BPS conference talk): https://www.telegraph.co.uk/news/science/science-news/9217313/Holiday-paradox-explains-why-time-flies.html |
| "Doing something new makes time pass quickly while you're doing it. But when you remember it later, it seems to have lasted longer than ordinary days." | Prospective vs retrospective time. | Scientific American (same page): "Engaging in a novel exploit makes time appear to pass more quickly in the moment. But if we remember that activity later on, it will seem to have lasted longer than more mundane experiences." |
| "In lab experiments, the more events people remember from a stretch of time, the longer they judge it to have been." | Retrospective duration follows remembered events. | Friedman & Janssen 2010: "laboratory studies have shown that the more events or changes that are remembered from an interval, the greater the retrospective estimates of its duration." |
| "People over thirty remember a disproportionate number of events from ages ten to thirty." | The reminiscence bump. | Munawar, Kuhn & Haque 2018 (systematic review, 68 studies): "the reminiscence bump… enhances memory recall from approximately 10-30 years of age by people over the age of 30"; "the cognitive account postulates that it is simply the novelty of many events occurring in the second and third decades of life". https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6289446/ (the novelty account is one of four; the script says only "nearly everything happens for the first time", a gloss) |
| "On that one, busy is fast, and boredom is what crawls." / "In one French study, people were pinged on their phones several times a day… faster when happy or excited, slower when sad. Twenty-year-olds and seventy-year-olds gave about the same answers." | Droit-Volet & Wearden 2015 (experience sampling; young 20–23, older 69–75), described in Droit-Volet 2016. Telegraph/Hammond: "time appears to go more quickly than when we are bored or anxious". | Droit-Volet 2016, *Time & Society* 28(1): "the present passage-of-time judgment (PoTj) did not change with age, being similar in both the young and the older participants… The participants did indeed experience a slowing down of the passage of time when they felt sad. Conversely, they experienced an acceleration when they felt happy." https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0961463X16656852 (open access, read in full) |
| "In two thousand five, Marc Wittmann and Sandra Lehnhoff asked four hundred and ninety-nine people, aged fourteen to ninety-four… Age explained at most ten percent." | Wittmann & Lehnhoff 2005, *Psychological Reports* 97(3):921. | Abstract: "499 subjects, ages 14 to 94 years… small-to-moderate effects accounted for at most 10% of the variance. Results generally support the widespread perception that the passage of time speeds up with age." https://doi.org/10.2466/pr0.97.3.921-935 |
| "People over forty said childhood moved slowly, then sped up through the teens and early twenties. For the last ten years, older people said time had gone faster." | Same study, via Scientific American. | Scientific American: "For shorter durations—a week, a month, even a year—the subjects' perception of time did not appear to increase with age. Most participants felt that the clock ticked by quickly. But for longer durations, such as a decade, a pattern emerged: older people tended to perceive time as moving faster. When asked to reflect on their lives, the participants older than 40 felt that time elapsed slowly in their childhood but then accelerated steadily through their teenage years into early adulthood." |
| "In two thousand ten, William Friedman and Steve Janssen asked eighteen hundred and sixty-five adults, aged sixteen to eighty." / "respondents of all ages reported that time seems to pass quickly. Age differences were 'very small,' except for… the last ten years." | Friedman & Janssen 2010, *Acta Psychologica* 134:130. | Abstract: "A total of 1865 adults from two countries, ranging in age from 16 to 80, reported how fast time appears to pass over different spans of time… Respondents of all ages reported that time seems to pass quickly. In contrast to widely held beliefs, age differences in reports of the subjective speed of time were very small, except for the question about how fast the last 10 years had passed. Findings support a theory based on the experience of time pressure." https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20163781/ |
| "Janet's fraction predicts that time should feel four times faster. People said about one point six." | Pooled earlier polls, quarter of present age. | Friedman & Janssen (full text): "the mean rated ratios between one's current age and an earlier age are about 1.58 for one-quarter one's present age and 1.46 for half one's age… (The predictions are 4.0 and 2.0, respectively, for the ratio model and 2.0 and 1.4 for the square-root model.)" "The subset of studies involving quantitative comparisons… fails to support either Janet's (1877) or Lemlich's (1975) model." |
| "Janet himself said the effect would show most when people judge the distant past, years or decades." | Janet's own prediction. | Wearden 2021 abstract: "Janet further proposes that this proportionality will be most marked when judging time intervals remote from the present, such as past years or decades." |
| "In one sample, age explained about one percent… and rushing explained ten." | Experiment 2 of Friedman & Janssen. | Full text: "age differences accounted for only about 1% of variance on the composite speed-of-time scale"; "The rushing scale predicted 10% of the variance in the speed-of-time composite". Rushing correlated within each age group "except for the 16-to-20-year-old group", hence "almost every age group". |
| "The same paper tested a memory idea by reminding people of the news from the last two years. Reminded years should have felt slower. If anything, they felt a little faster." | Recall-difficulty model, Experiment 2. | Full text: "participants who gave their impressions of the speed of time after being reminded of a substantial number of news events from the last two years actually tended to report time as passing more rapidly than those who had not yet been reminded." (The script's gloss "should have felt slower" is the model's prediction.) |
| "Sylvie Droit-Volet's team in France tested older people, up to the age of ninety-eight. After seventy-five, on the clock they feel now, time did not speed up. It slowed down. The best predictor of that slowing was sadness." | Droit-Volet 2016, n = 54, ages 64–98.5. | Abstract: "the judgment of the current passage of time changed after 75 years. However, the perception was not one of acceleration but of slowing down. The best predictors of this slowing down of time were the negative affects, namely sadness". Contested/limited: 54 people, 44 women; the script says "this is still argued". |
| "Not a fixed fraction, a habit of repeating yourself." / "the memory story is the favourite, not the winner." | Our summary; the field has no consensus on mechanism. | Friedman & Janssen: "Two memory based theories do not appear to be good candidates for supplemental explanations"; SciAm / Hammond favour memory. Flagged in the script as unresolved. |
| "Is it a cure? Nobody has tested it. It's a hypothesis with stickers on it." | The novelty advice is a suggestion, not a trial result. | SciAm: "We can alter our perceptions by keeping our brain active, continually learning skills and ideas, and exploring new places." (advice, no trial cited) |

## Checked and NOT used
- **Hammond's "six to nine new memories in a fortnight"** (Telegraph 2012, BPS talk): a number from a conference talk, no study cited. Not used.
- **Siffre cave experiments / "time perception under stress slows"** (Arstila 2012; Eagleman): different phenomenon (fear), cut for focus.
- **Internal-clock / dopamine slowing with age**: mentioned as a theory in Friedman & Janssen ("the speed of biological clocks"); not given its own beat (no clean evidence in the sources read).
- **Lockdown time-distortion studies (2020)**: real but a different question.
- **Janet's reversal guess for old people thinking about time left**: in Wearden's abstract; cut for length (the Droit-Volet result carries the "after seventy-five" beat).
- **"Dave is thirty-nine" and the 1/8 vs 1/39 sum**: invented; the sum is the textbook proportion model, flagged as Dave's own.
