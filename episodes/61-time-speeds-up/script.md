# chapter: The summer that lasted forever
This is Dave. Dave is thirty-nine, and he would like to report that this entire year took about a weekend. // [J]
When Dave was eight, one summer lasted long enough to build a den, lose a shoe, and fall out with the same friend four times. / Nothing much happened. It still took roughly forever. //
"Is it September yet? It has been summer for about a hundred years." (@kid) //
So why does time speed up as you get older? //
Dave has done the sum. At eight, a year was an eighth of his whole life. / Now it's a thirty-ninth. By his arithmetic, a year is about five times thinner than it used to be. // [J]
So Dave has a plan: to make this year feel as long as that summer. //
The answer involves a French philosopher, a holiday that went too fast and lasted too long, and one survey that should make Dave feel better. //
Let's start with the philosopher, and a very old piece of arithmetic. //

# chapter: The fraction
In eighteen seventy-seven, the French philosopher Paul Janet wrote a short paper called "An Internal Optical Illusion." His idea: how fast time feels is proportional to how old you are. //
A year feels like a twentieth of your life when you're twenty, and a sixtieth when you're sixty. Same year, thinner slice. //
In other words, a year hasn't shrunk. It just looks smaller from further away. [J] //
That's Dave's sum, a hundred and fifty years early. // [J]
In nineteen seventy-five, Robert Lemlich tried a gentler version, using a square root. / He polled students and older adults about how fast time felt at different ages, and found generally good agreement. //
So that's it? Time speeds up because each year is a smaller fraction of you? There's a catch. //
Those polls asked people to remember how fast time felt decades ago. Remembering a feeling from thirty years back is not the same as having had it. // [J]
Dave needs something he can test, and he has a calendar. It has fifty-two weeks on it, and from where he's standing, they all look the same. // [J]

# chapter: The holiday paradox
So Dave takes a week at the seaside, to find out what a different kind of week does. //
It's over in about a minute. // [J]
"Wait. Where did the whole week go?" (@dave) //
Then he comes home and thinks back over it, and now the week is enormous. / There was a pier, a wrong turning, and a seagull that took his chips and his dignity. // [J]
The psychologist Claudia Hammond calls this the holiday paradox. //
Scientific American puts it plainly. Doing something new makes time pass quickly while you're doing it. / But when you remember it later, it seems to have lasted longer than ordinary days. //
So you have two clocks. One runs now, and it follows your attention. The other runs afterwards, and it counts memories. //
In lab experiments, the more events people remember from a stretch of time, the longer they judge it to have been. Memory counts events, not minutes. // [J]
A week of firsts races by on the first clock and stretches out on the second. //
Now look at Dave's year. The same commute, the same desk, the same sandwich, three hundred and sixty-five times. / That leaves very little for the second clock to count. // [J]
It also fits a famous oddity of memory. People over thirty remember a disproportionate number of events from ages ten to thirty. / Those are the years when nearly everything happens for the first time. //
So Dave goes looking for firsts. //

# chapter: Dave tries firsts
He takes a different route to work, learns to juggle three oranges, and eats a vegetable he can't pronounce. It's a busy week. It's over by Wednesday. // [J]
"Wait. Where did the whole week go?" (@dave) //
Dave is confused. Isn't a week of new things supposed to feel long? //
Not on the first clock. On that one, busy is fast, and boredom is what crawls. / Dave tests this in a dentist's waiting room. The hour drags by, and by dinner he can't remember a thing from it. // [J]
In one French study, people were pinged on their phones several times a day and asked how fast time felt right then. / It felt faster when they were happy or excited, and slower when they were sad. Twenty-year-olds and seventy-year-olds gave about the same answers. //
So the clock you feel follows attention and mood, and the clock you remember follows memory. They don't have to agree. //
So which clock is the one that speeds up with age? To find out, you have to ask a lot of people. //

# chapter: Asking everyone
In two thousand five, Marc Wittmann and Sandra Lehnhoff asked four hundred and ninety-nine people, aged fourteen to ninety-four, how fast time felt. / People over forty said childhood moved slowly, then sped up through the teens and early twenties. //
For the last ten years, older people said time had gone faster. Dave feels vindicated. His calendar does not care. // [J]
Then in two thousand ten, the psychologists William Friedman and Steve Janssen asked eighteen hundred and sixty-five adults, aged sixteen to eighty, the same kind of question. //
Dave expected the sixty-year-olds to say it flew. He did not expect the sixteen-year-olds to say it too. They'd had sixteen years, and already wanted a refund. // [J]
In the paper's words, respondents of all ages reported that time seems to pass quickly. / Age differences were "very small," except for one question: how fast had the last ten years gone? //
For the last week, the last month, even the last year, the age effect all but vanished. //
Now Dave checks his fraction. In the same paper, the authors pooled earlier polls that asked people to compare time now with time at a quarter of their age. //
Janet's fraction predicts that time should feel four times faster. People said about one point six. // [J]
"Wait. So the whole fraction is just wrong?" (@dave) //
Not entirely. Janet himself said the effect would show most when people judge the distant past, years or decades, not the present. On that point, he was right. //
So what does predict it? Wittmann's team had found that age explained at most ten percent of how fast time felt. / Friedman and Janssen found a better predictor: how rushed you feel. In one sample, age explained about one percent, and rushing explained ten. // [J]
People who are busy, behind and short of time say it goes faster, in almost every age group. Dave is behind on everything. His time goes extremely fast. // [J]
So maybe time doesn't speed up as you get older. Maybe your calendar just fills up with things that rush you. //

# chapter: Two clocks
So, why does time speed up as you get older? //
Partly, because of the clock you remember. Years that are copies of each other collapse into one. Not a fixed fraction, a habit of repeating yourself. // [J]
On the clock you feel, age barely matters. Attention and rush do. //
Here's the honest caveat. This is still argued, and the effects are small. / The same paper tested a memory idea by reminding people of the news from the last two years. / Reminded years should have felt slower. If anything, they felt a little faster. //
So the memory story is the favourite, not the winner. //
There's one more finding. In two thousand sixteen, Sylvie Droit-Volet's team in France tested older people, up to the age of ninety-eight. / After seventy-five, on the clock they feel now, time did not speed up. It slowed down. //
The best predictor of that slowing was sadness. //
Which is a reminder that a slow day is not always a good one. //
So what can Dave do about the second clock? //

# chapter: Six weeks of firsts
Dave doesn't try to become eight again. He just stops copying his weeks. //
Over the year, he sets aside six weeks: a trip, a language, a bicycle, and a hobby he's bad at. //
Every first gets a sticker. By December, his calendar has forty-six plain weeks and six that look like a stamp collection. //
Is it a cure? Nobody has tested it. It's a hypothesis with stickers on it. //
But when Dave looks back now, those six weeks fill most of the wall. The plain weeks shrink to a thin grey strip along the bottom. //
Ask him how the year felt while it was happening, and he'll still say a weekend. / But it was a long weekend, and it had a pier in it. //
(slow) Dave wonders where the week went. / Then he looks at the wall, and finds it.
