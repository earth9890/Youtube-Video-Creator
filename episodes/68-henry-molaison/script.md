# chapter: The star in the mirror
This is Dave. Dave has been given a pencil, a sheet of paper with a double-outlined star, and a mirror.
He must trace the star while looking only at the mirror.
Scientists call this a test of motor learning. Dave has other words for it. // [J]
Dave's first star looks like a seismograph. Every instinct he owns is now pointing the wrong way. The mirror isn't broken. His hand is just loyal to the old rules. // [J]
Meanwhile, in another lab, a man is doing the same task. Every morning, the scientists have to introduce themselves again. He doesn't remember them, or the pencil, or the star.
But somehow, he is getting better. //
Dave puts on a name tag that says hello, I'm Dave, in case anyone has forgotten. Nobody has. He keeps it on anyway. // [J]
So what can a person still learn if he can't remember learning it? And where in the brain do our days really live? //
His name was Henry Molaison. Let's start with how he got there. //

# chapter: Henry
Henry Molaison was born in Connecticut in nineteen twenty-six. At seven, he was knocked over by a bicycle. Some early reports said nine, until his mother corrected them. Mothers remember. // [J]
Minor seizures began at ten, and major seizures after sixteen.
He worked for a while on an assembly line. By twenty-seven, even very high doses of medicine could not control the seizures, and he could not work or live normally. //
In nineteen fifty-three, the surgeon William Scoville offered him an experimental operation, one he had previously carried out on patients with psychosis.
It was done with the approval of Henry and his family.
Scoville removed tissue from both sides of the brain, in a region called the medial temporal lobe, which includes the hippocampus. His drawings showed the cut reaching eight centimetres.
The hippocampus is named after the seahorse, which an early anatomist thought it resembled. A small organ with a very large reputation. // [J]
The operation worked, partly. The seizures were reduced. But something else had gone.
Henry could not remember what had happened in the hospital, and he did not recognise the staff who cared for him.
He lost most of the year or two before the operation, though his childhood stayed.
He would apologise for forgetting the names of people he had just met. He described it as being like waking from a dream, where every day is alone in itself. //

# chapter: What was still there
The strange part was what stayed. His intelligence was normal, and in tests months later it was above average. His language was fine. He could hold a conversation.
He could hold a number, too.
Researchers found that he could keep a three-digit number in mind for as long as fifteen minutes, by repeating it over and over, using an elaborate mnemonic scheme.
That is the most carefully organised set of three digits in Connecticut. // [J]
But the moment his attention moved to something new, the whole event was gone. As if it had never been. //
A young researcher named Brenda Milner was invited to Hartford to meet him.
She had been a doctoral student at McGill, and had already met two other patients who lost their memory after brain surgery.
She wrote up his case with Scoville in nineteen fifty-seven. It is still one of the most cited papers in neuroscience. //
Before Henry, following the influential Karl Lashley, scientists thought memory was spread across the cortex, tangled up with thinking and seeing.
Henry showed that it was a separate job, done in a particular place. //
Dave listens, pencil in hand, and goes over his first star again. //

# chapter: The star
Then came Milner's discovery. She gave Henry the mirror task: trace a five-pointed star with a double outline, seeing only your hand and the star in a mirror.
A mirror turns your lifetime of hand training upside down. Left is right, forward is backward. Dave has been demonstrating this for twenty minutes. // [J]
Henry learned the task over ten trials, and he kept the skill across three days.
And yet at the end, he had no memory of ever doing it before. //
Dave comes back on day two. "I remember this. I hated it." (@dave) // [J]
His star is cleaner than day one. By day three, it is recognisably a star. Possibly a cross spider, but a star. // [J]
Dave remembers all of it, every wobble, and still can't explain how his hand got better.
Henry's hand got better too. His hands kept a record that his mind could not read. //

# chapter: Two kinds of remembering
This was the first hint that memory is not one thing. There is remembering that, and there is remembering how. //
Remembering that is facts and days: what you ate, who you met. Remembering how is skills, such as riding a bike, typing, or tracing a star in a mirror.
Dave remembers how to ride a bike. He does not remember what he ate. In this respect, Dave is normal. // [J]
Henry kept the second kind. He could learn new movements, and later he learned to use a walker. But the first kind, the days and the faces, would not stick. //
The Nobel laureate Eric Kandel won his prize for work on memory in sea slugs. He called Milner's work one of the great milestones in the history of modern neuroscience. // [J]
He said it opened the way for the study of two memory systems, explicit and implicit. //
And surely, we think, nothing new got in. Not quite. Over the years, a few facts did.
He could tell you about some celebrities, and he drew a map of the house he had moved into after surgery.
He could also update old knowledge, adding Jonas Salk to what he knew about polio.
In a crossword, he could answer questions about the world before nineteen fifty-three, which put him ahead of Dave. // [J]
Henry once told someone in the lab that it was a funny thing: you just live and learn. He was living, and they were learning. //
Suzanne Corkin, who studied him for decades, said he had a sense of self, and values, and a wonderful sense of humour. //
That left one question. Where, exactly, do our days live? For that, scientists needed Henry's brain. //

# chapter: The brain
Henry died on the second of December, two thousand eight, at eighty-two. Until then, the world knew him only as H.M., to protect his privacy.
Probably the most famous patient in the history of neuroscience, and almost nobody knew his name. // [J]
A year later, his brain was cut into two thousand four hundred and one slices, each seventy micrometres thick. It took fifty-three hours, and it was streamed live online.
Dave watches for the first hour, then asks if there will be a test. // [J]
Here is the twist. In two thousand fourteen, Jacopo Annese's team reported that a significant amount of hippocampal tissue had survived the operation. //
They also found a small lesion on the front of the left side, in the orbitofrontal cortex.
The textbook picture of Henry, the man with no hippocampus, was tidier than his brain.
The textbooks had written off his hippocampus. Some of it had not read the textbooks. // [J]
Today, scientists say the days are made by a system, the hippocampus and its neighbours, rather than one tidy spot.
Scoville's drawings had said eight centimetres. Brain scans in the nineteen nineties had already shown a little over five. //
Even Milner had been careful. She wrote that she never claimed the memory loss was caused by the hippocampal lesions alone.
Her paper titles said hippocampal anyway. Titles run ahead of evidence. // [J]

# chapter: What he gave
So what did Henry teach us? That memory is separate from intelligence. That it has more than one system. That one man can lose his days and keep his hands. //
And that science can be corrected by the very patient it made famous. The new brain atlas did not make Henry's lesson smaller. It made it more careful. //
Questions about consent and records were raised after his death, and they are disputed by others, including MIT.
They are worth knowing, and worth reading about, and we have not found an answer to them here. //
Dave sets down the pencil and the mirror. His star is clean now. He ticks the box that says he has done this before. //
Next to it, Henry's line stays blank. But his star is clean too.
