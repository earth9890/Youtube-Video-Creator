# chapter: Something green
This is Dave. In ten minutes, Dave meets his new neighbours, and he is fairly sure there is something green in his front tooth. //
There's a problem. It's thirty thousand years ago, and as far as anyone knows, nobody has made a mirror yet. //
"I can't meet people like this. How do I even check?" (@dave) //
So what did ancient humans do before mirrors? //
Our species is about three hundred thousand years old. The oldest manufactured mirrors anyone has found are about eight thousand. /
So for roughly ninety-seven percent of our history, nobody owned a mirror. That is a lot of green teeth. // [J]
The answer runs through a puddle, a rock from a volcano, a philosopher with advice, / and a test for chimpanzees that went strangely wrong in Kenya. //
Dave, who has been smiling at a cave wall for a full minute, goes to find some water. // [J]

# chapter: The puddle
The first mirror was almost certainly still water, or maybe a smooth shiny stone. A pool that is calm enough will do, and a pool that isn't won't. //
Dave kneels at a pond and a face wobbles back at him, with teeth made of ripples. / Whether he has something in his tooth is now a question about the ripples. // [J]
About two thousand years ago, the poet Ovid wrote the most famous meeting between a person and a reflection. /
A young man called Narcissus sees a beautiful stranger in a pool. He stretches out his arms, and the stranger stretches out theirs. //
He smiles, and the stranger smiles back. Then, slowly, he works it out. "I am he. I sense it, and I am not deceived by my own image." //
So in the oldest famous story about a reflection, the hard part isn't seeing the face. It's realising it's yours. //
Dave waves at the pond for ten minutes before he is sure. // [J]
Water works. But a pond doesn't come with you, and it never holds still when you need it to. /
What people really wanted was a pond they could carry. // [J]

# chapter: A rock from a volcano
So about eight thousand years ago, at a town called Çatalhöyük, in what is now Turkey, people started grinding stone into mirrors. /
They used obsidian, a black volcanic glass. A modern curator said these mirrors have surfaces that "are highly polished and reflect a sharp image." //
A reader wrote to Archaeology magazine to say that wasn't easy. "To grind a flat surface and then achieve a true polish," they said, "is not easy." /
So in nineteen ninety-nine, a man called James Vedder tried to make one by hand. //
His first grinding tool was a piece of concrete sidewalk. // [J]
The biggest mirror he managed was about four centimetres by six. Every one gave a good image, / and every one came out slightly curved, which is what you'd expect from rubbing by hand. //
A mirror the size of a postage stamp, so Dave checks one tooth at a time. // [J]
Dave grinds for an hour, and ends up with a very smooth rock and the same tooth. // [J]
And the black glass kept its job for thousands of years. In Aztec Mexico, obsidian mirrors were tied to the god Tezcatlipoca, whose name means smoking mirror. /
One ended up in the British Museum. It belonged to the Elizabethan magician John Dee, who used it to talk to spirits. //
A note kept with it calls it "The Devil's Looking-glass." // [J]

# chapter: A mirror you had to keep polishing
Metal came next. Polished copper mirrors appear in Mesopotamia from about four thousand BCE, and in Egypt from about three thousand. /
In China, bronze mirrors go back about four thousand years. //
In the tomb of a woman named Hatnefer, an Egyptian mirror was found at the bottom of her coffin. Its handle carries the emblem of Hathor, goddess of beauty. /
Beside it lay a second mirror. She was taking two. // [J]
An Egyptian bronze mirror gave a warm, softly blurred reflection. Flattering, but no use at all for teeth. // [J]
But metal mirrors tarnished, and needed constant polishing. They were dim. And only the wealthy could afford them. /
One reading of the Bible's famous line, seeing "as in a mirror, darkly", is that it describes exactly this. //
The Greek philosopher Socrates, according to Diogenes Laertius, told young men to use a mirror often. /
If they were handsome, they should live up to it. If they were ugly, they should make up for it with education. //
Dave decides to go with the second option. // [J]

# chapter: A farm for a mirror
Glass was the next upgrade. The Roman writer Pliny says craftsmen in Sidon backed glass with lead or gold leaf. /
But archaeologists haven't found a glass mirror from before the third century, so for now we only have his word. // [J]
Real glass mirrors came from Venice. By the sixteenth century, Venetian workshops coated glass with a tin and mercury mixture, and kept the secret for about a century. /
Their mirrors were up to a metre square, and they were expensive luxuries for palaces. /
In the late seventeenth century, a countess is reported to have traded an entire wheat farm for one, and called it a bargain. //
So the farm's wheat could not tell her what she looked like, and the mirror could. // [J]
Then the secret leaked. French workshops industrialised the process. Mirrors finally became cheap. //
So now Dave understands the pattern. For most of history, most people got a quick look at a pond, and not much else. /
And he draws a conclusion. "So ancient people didn't know what their own faces looked like. They didn't even know who they were." (@dave) //

# chapter: The test
Scientists do have a test for knowing who you are. /
In nineteen seventy, the psychologist Gordon Gallup marked chimpanzees with red dye, on an eyebrow and the top of an ear. /
At first, the chimpanzees threatened the stranger in the glass. Eventually they used it to groom themselves, make faces and pick their noses. // [J]
And when the dye was there, they touched the marks. The chimpanzees had recognised themselves. //
Human children do it too. In Western studies, about half of eighteen-month-olds touch a mark on their own face, rising to about seventy percent by twenty-four months. //
In a study published in twenty ten, Tanya Broesch and her colleagues took a sticky note to Kenya and tested eighty-two children, aged from eighteen months to six years. / They didn't even ask the usual question, who's that in the mirror? //
Just two of them touched it. The other eighty froze. // [J]
In Fiji, none of the children touched the mark. In Canada, seventy-seven percent did, and in the United States eighty-eight. //
Did the children not know their own faces? The researchers don't think so. /
Their impression was that the children understood it was themselves and that the mark was unexpected, / but were unsure what the right response was, and didn't dare touch it. //
So the test isn't only about knowing your face. It's also about whether, in your world, you'd reach up and touch a stranger's sticky note on your forehead. // [J]
Dave, who was about to say something clever about prehistory, goes quiet. //

# chapter: A mirror made of people
So what's the other mirror? In nineteen oh two, the sociologist Charles Cooley wrote about what he called the looking-glass self. / "Each to each a looking-glass," he wrote, "reflects the other that doth pass." //
His point was that what moves us to pride or shame is not the mere mechanical reflection of ourselves. / It is the imagined effect of that reflection on another person's mind. //
So what did ancient humans do before mirrors? For about ninety-seven percent of our history, / they used water, then polished stone and metal for the few. And for everyone else, a mirror made of people. //
That's our summary, not Cooley's. It fits. A friend's face tells you more about yours than a pond ever will. /
Cooley said the self has three parts: how we imagine we look, how we imagine someone judges it, and a feeling such as pride or mortification. //
Dave walks up to the neighbours with no idea what his tooth looks like. / A small child looks up and says, very clearly, that he has a leaf in his tooth. //
It is the first accurate mirror Dave has seen all day. He feels the third part immediately. //
(slow) He says thank you, and smiles anyway. Carefully.
