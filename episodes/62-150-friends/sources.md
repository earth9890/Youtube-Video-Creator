# Episode 62 "Why You Can Only Have 150 Friends": sources (verified 2026-10-22)

Primary papers read in full text: Dunbar 1993 (preprint), Dunbar 2016, Hill & Dunbar 2003, Lindenfors et al. 2021; the rest from abstracts plus named secondary coverage. Dave, his phone, the party, the hall, his Christmas cards, the neighbour and the calling week are invented story devices; the script claims nothing from them. "Dave's Brain" is a recurring character (episode 38).

| "Humans have the largest neocortex ratio of any primate, about fifty percent above the next highest." | Neocortex ratio CR = 4.1. | Dunbar 1993 (full text): "the neocortex ratio for humans is CR=4.1. This is about 50% larger than the maximum value for any other primate species". |
| "a prediction: a hundred and forty-eight, with a likely range of a hundred to two hundred and thirty-one." | 147.8; 95 % limits 100.2–231.1. | Dunbar 1993: "Equation (1) yields a predicted group size for humans of 147.8… the 95% confidence limits around this prediction… are moderately wide (100.2- 231.1)." |
| "He pointed out himself that extending a line past your data is frowned upon. | "villages or clans, averaged a hundred and forty-eight people across nine societies." | Ethnographic census data. | Dunbar 1993: "The average size of the intermediate level groups for those societies for which accurate census data are available is 148.4 (range 90-221.5, N=9). | "The Hutterites… say a colony should split at about a hundred and fifty. Past that, they explain, peer pressure stops working. Rather than hire police, they split the village." | Hutterites via Hardin 1988, in Dunbar 1993. | Dunbar 1993: "According to Hardin (1988), the Hutterites regard 150 individuals as the limiting size for their farming communities: once a community reaches this size, steps are taken to split it into two daughter communities… They explicitly state that when the number of individuals is much larger than this, it becomes difficult to control their behaviour by means of peer pressure alone (Hardin 1988). Rather than create a police force, they prefer to split the community." (The same paper gives the Schmedenleut mean as 106.9.) |
| "The Roman army's basic unit, the maniple, held about a hundred and twenty to a hundred and thirty men. A modern army's smallest independent unit, the company, runs a hundred to two hundred." | Armies. | Dunbar 1993: "the basic unit was the maniple… which normally consisted of 120-130 men"; "The smallest independent unit in modern armies (the company) invariably contains 100-200 men". |
| "Armies have had two thousand years to make that number bigger. They keep landing here." | Our gloss on Dunbar's argument. | Dunbar 1993: "Given that the fighting power of a unit is a function of its size, we might expect there to be considerable selection pressure in favour of units that are as large as possible. That the smallest independent unit should turn out to have a maximum size of about 200 even in modern armies… suggests that this upper limit is set by the number of individuals who can work effectively together". (Dunbar's reading; not independently verified.) |
| "in two thousand three, Russell Hill and Dunbar asked forty-three households to list everyone they sent Christmas cards to. The average came to a hundred and fifty-three… a hundred and twenty-five." | Hill & Dunbar 2003, Human Nature 14:53. | Abstract: "Maximum network size averaged 153.5 individuals, with a mean network size of 124.9 for those individuals explicitly contacted"; text: "Forty-three questionnaires were returned, between them involving a total of 2,984 Christmas cards." https://rahill.webspace.durham.ac.uk/wp-content/uploads/sites/207/2021/04/Hill-and-Dunbar-2003.pdf |
| "camps of thirty to fifty, clans of a hundred to two hundred, and tribes of five hundred and up." | Three size classes. | Dunbar 1993: "group sizes fall into three quite distinct size classes: small living groups of 30-50 individuals… a large population unit… that typically numbers between 500 and 2500 individuals and an intermediate level of grouping… that typically contains 100-200 people." |
| "layers of about five, fifteen, fifty and a hundred and fifty, each roughly three times the last. They run on to about five hundred, and fifteen hundred." | The layers. | Dunbar 2016, R Soc Open Sci: "natural scaling ratio of approximately 3… In humans, these layers have values that approximate 5, 15, 50 and 150, and extend beyond this in at least two further layers to 500 and 1500". Zhou et al. 2005, Proc R Soc B: "a discrete hierarchy of group sizes with a preferred scaling ratio close to three… approximating 3-5, 9-15, 30-45". https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15734699/ ; https://ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4736918/ |
| "Five are the people you would turn to in a crisis. Fifteen are close friends… Five hundred are acquaintances… Fifteen hundred is about how many faces you can put a name to." | Layer definitions. | Dunbar 2016: support clique "(friends on whom you would depend for emotional/social support in times of crisis)", sympathy group "(close friends)"; outer layers: "The two outermost layers (at 500 and 1500) correspond, respectively, to acquaintances (people we would not consider as personal friends or family, but know well enough to have a conversation with) and to the number of faces we can put names to." "A hundred and fifty are everyone you have a real relationship with": "an individual's social network (defined as all meaningful relationships)… typically in the order of 100–200". Note: the 50 and 150 layers are less well established than 5 and 15; the script says "researchers find layers" and stays qualitative. |
| "In twenty sixteen, Dunbar tested this on two big surveys of British adults. In practical terms, it may reflect the fact that real (as opposed to casual) relationships require at least occasional face-to-face interaction to maintain them." |

| "In twenty twenty-one, three Swedish researchers, Patrik Lindenfors, Andreas Wartel and Johan Lind… Different methods gave average group sizes anywhere from sixteen to a hundred and nine. | "Dunbar's side replied that they had used the wrong kind of regression. ## Checked and NOT used
- **"40 % of social time to the inner five, 20 % to the next fifteen"**: widely quoted, but I saw it only in a secondary summary (not the Sutcliffe et al. 2012 text). Not used.
- **Killworth's "reverse small world" network estimate (134 / about 250)**: in Dunbar 1993; cut for length.
- **Phone-call evidence of 5 and 15 layers (Dunbar 2015)**: not read in full; not used.
- **Dunbar's own written comment on Lindenfors et al.**: not read; the dispute is told only as the critics report it, and the script says so by attributing it ("Dunbar's side replied…").
- **"Companies above 150 can't function without sub-structuring" (Dunbar 1993, personal communication)**: an informal business rule; not used.
- **Neolithic Mesopotamian village size (Oates 1977: 150–200)**: in Dunbar 1993; cut.
- **Hutterite colony mean of 106.9**: in the paper, not in the script (it would complicate the "split at 150" beat without adding a clear idea).
