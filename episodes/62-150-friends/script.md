# chapter: A thousand and twelve
This is Dave. Dave is turning forty, and he has decided to invite everybody he knows to the party. / That is one thousand and twelve people, which is how many contacts are in his phone. // [J]
The village hall holds a hundred and fifty. Dave calls this a minor logistical issue. // [J]
He calls every one of those contacts a friend, because anything else feels rude. //
But a famous scientist once suggested that this is impossible. Not rude. Impossible. / Nobody, the popular version goes, can keep more than about a hundred and fifty friends. //
So why can you only have a hundred and fifty friends? And is that even true? //
The answer runs through monkeys, Roman soldiers, a Christmas card list, and a recent paper that says the number may not be real. Let's start with the monkeys. //

# chapter: The monkey sum
In nineteen ninety-two, the British anthropologist Robin Dunbar noticed a pattern across primates. / The bigger a species' neocortex, the thinking part of the brain, the bigger the group it lives in. //
Humans have the largest neocortex ratio of any primate, about fifty percent above the next highest. We win this one by a brain. // [J]
So Dunbar ran the line out to humans, and it gave him a prediction: a hundred and forty-eight, with a likely range of a hundred to two hundred and thirty-one. //
He pointed out himself that extending a line past your data is frowned upon. He did it anyway, as a first guess. // [J]
"So you're saying a monkey told you how many friends I'm allowed to have?" (@dave) // [J]
More or less. But a prediction is not a head count. To check it, somebody has to go and count actual people. //

# chapter: Counting real people
Dunbar went looking for groups of about that size. / In traditional societies with good census data, the middle level of grouping, villages or clans, averaged a hundred and forty-eight people across nine societies. //
The Hutterites, who farm in communal colonies, say a colony should split at about a hundred and fifty. / Past that, they explain, peer pressure stops working. Rather than hire police, they split the village. // [J]
The Roman army's basic unit, the maniple, held about a hundred and twenty to a hundred and thirty men. / A modern army's smallest independent unit, the company, runs a hundred to two hundred. //
Armies have had two thousand years to make that number bigger. They keep landing here. // [J]
And in two thousand three, Russell Hill and Dunbar asked forty-three households to list everyone they sent Christmas cards to. / The average came to a hundred and fifty-three. If you count only the people they meant to keep in touch with, it was a hundred and twenty-five. //
Dave counts his own cards. Eleven, and four are from the dentist. // [J]
"Honestly, I think that one is just a contact." (@dave) //
Dunbar's tables showed three typical sizes: camps of thirty to fifty, clans of a hundred to two hundred, and tribes of five hundred and up. A ladder, not a rung. // [J]
So there are real clusters near a hundred and fifty. / But Dave's thousand and twelve is still in his pocket, and nobody has explained where the other eight hundred and sixty-two go. //

# chapter: Five, fifteen, fifty
Here's the part the headline leaves out. A hundred and fifty is not one circle. It's the third ring of an onion. //
Researchers who study personal networks find layers of about five, fifteen, fifty and a hundred and fifty, each roughly three times the last. / They run on to about five hundred, and fifteen hundred. //
Five are the people you would turn to in a crisis. Fifteen are close friends. / A hundred and fifty are everyone you have a real relationship with. //
Five hundred are acquaintances, people you'd stop and talk to. Fifteen hundred is about how many faces you can put a name to. //
Dave redraws his seating plan as rings. His thousand and twelve contacts sit somewhere between the acquaintances and the names. // [J]
In twenty sixteen, Dunbar tested this on two big surveys of British adults. / People's social media friends averaged a hundred and fifty-five in one, and a hundred and eighty-three in the other. //
But only about twenty-eight percent of those friends were described as genuinely close, and the most common answer was between none and ten percent. //
Dave does the sum. Twenty-eight percent of a thousand and twelve is about two hundred and eighty real friends. / Dave's Brain asks him to name five. He gets to three. // [J]
The innermost layers came out at four point one and thirteen point six. Almost exactly five and fifteen. //
His conclusion: social media doesn't break the limit. Real relationships, he suggests, need at least occasional face-to-face contact. Dave's Brain clears its throat. //
"I can put a name to fifteen hundred faces, Dave. I cannot hold a conversation with them." (@brain) // [J]

# chapter: What it costs
So why can't Dave simply keep more? Dunbar's answer is time. //
Monkeys hold their groups together by grooming, and the bigger the group, the more they groom. / By the monkey formula, a human group of a hundred and fifty would spend about forty-two percent of the day grooming each other. //
That is more than double any other primate manages. / Dunbar suggests that language evolved as cheap grooming: you can talk to several people at once while doing something else. //
In his sample of human conversations, about sixty percent of the talk was about relationships and personal experiences. / Gossip is grooming for people who can't reach each other's fur. // [J]
Even so, friendship has a running cost. / In one study of twenty-five students followed for eighteen months, friendships lost closeness when contact fell, much more than family ties did. //
And in a study of twenty-four students' phone records, people seemed to run a one-in, one-out policy. As new friends arrived, old ones received fewer calls. //
Dave's new neighbour is lovely. Dave's brother has noticed that he's getting fewer calls. // [J]
So Dave tries the opposite. He sets out to phone all one thousand and twelve contacts, ten minutes each. //
That is about seven days of non-stop calling, with no sleep. By Thursday, Dave is calling people to say that he's calling people. // [J]
"Honestly, I think that one is just a contact." (@dave) //
So the number looks like a time budget. But then the plot turns on the number itself. //

# chapter: Deconstructed
In twenty twenty-one, three Swedish researchers, Patrik Lindenfors, Andreas Wartel and Johan Lind, redid Dunbar's monkey sum with newer data and newer statistics. //
Different methods gave average group sizes anywhere from sixteen to a hundred and nine. / And the ranges around them ran from about two all the way up to five hundred and twenty. //
In their words, specifying any one number is futile. A cognitive limit on human group size cannot be derived this way. //
They hoped, "though perhaps futile," that the paper would end the use of Dunbar's number in science and the media. // [J]
They also point out that Dunbar's method gives an average group size, yet people use it as a maximum. / Those are two different things. A hundred and fifty is an average. Nobody is asked to leave at a hundred and fifty-one. // [J]
Dunbar's side replied that they had used the wrong kind of regression. / The Swedes replied that his kind over-corrects, and that other methods agreed with theirs. Science, at its most polite. //
So the monkey sum is shaky. But look at what's left standing. / The clans, the Christmas cards, the layers, and the social media counts are measurements of real people. None of them depends on the monkey line. //
Some are small samples, and several come from Dunbar's own group. That's worth knowing. //
So here is the honest answer. A hundred and fifty isn't a wall in your skull. / It's roughly where people tend to land, because friendship costs time, and time runs out. //

Dave still has one thousand and twelve contacts. He keeps them all. / Some are people he'd stop and talk to in the street, and that was always enough. //
"Honestly, I think that one is just a contact. And that's fine." (@dave) //
