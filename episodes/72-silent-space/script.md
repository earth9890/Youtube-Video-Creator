# chapter: A bell for the universe
This is Dave. Dave has brought a brass bell on a spacewalk. It says HELLO on the side. He plans to ring it so loudly that the whole universe hears. // [J]
"One good swing, and every star out there will know I exist." (@dave) //
He swings. The bell shakes in his hand. It makes absolutely no sound. //
"Shh, Dave. It's a vacuum. Nobody out here can hear you, including me." (@brain) // [J]
So here is the question. Why is space completely silent? And is it silent everywhere, or only wherever Dave happens to be standing? /
Tonight Dave tries his bell inside a spaceship, on the Moon, on Mars and between the stars, to find out where a sound can live. /
And at the end there is a bell much bigger than his, ringing somewhere right now, once every ten million years. //

# chapter: The loudest quiet place
Back inside the space station, Dave rings it again. Everyone hears it, and nobody is pleased. Not because of the bell. The station is already loud. /
NASA measures the background noise in its modules at fifty-six to sixty-nine decibels, from fans and pumps that never switch off. / Crews wear ear protection, and NASA says the noise limit has been exceeded in forty-one percent of its checks since two thousand eight. // [J]
So behind a thin wall, it's loud. Outside it, nothing. The bell is the same. The difference is what's around it. /
The quietest place in the universe turns out to be just outside a very noisy house. // [J]

# chapter: What a sound needs
A sound is a push, passed along. The bell shoves the air next to it, those molecules shove the next ones, and the shove travels to your ear. /
As Scientific American puts it: "There just aren't enough particles to bump into each other to transport the acoustic wave." //
In one cubic centimetre of air, about the size of a sugar cube, there are roughly twenty-seven billion billion molecules. /
Between the stars, Voyager one's instruments found about one tenth of a particle in the same space. /
That is two hundred and seventy billion billion times thinner. It isn't empty. It's a push with nobody to hand it to. // [J]
Between galaxies it gets thinner still: about one particle in every cubic metre, roughly the size of a wardrobe. Dave's shout would need a lot of luck. // [J]
"So the bell shakes the nothing, and the nothing doesn't shake back." (@dave) //

# chapter: The Moon rings
But sound doesn't need air. It can travel through a solid, and space has plenty of rock. /
In nineteen sixty-nine, after Apollo twelve's astronauts left, NASA controllers fired the empty lander's thrusters and crashed it into the Moon, about forty miles from their seismometer. /
The seismometer recorded signals for nearly one hour after the impact. Scientists said the Moon rang like a bell. /
The reason, as Popular Science puts it, is that the Moon is dry, cool and rigid, so there is nothing to deaden the vibrations. Earth's wet rock soaks them up. //
So the sound that can't cross the sky can cross the ground. Dave can't hear his bell on the Moon. In principle, the Moon can. // [J]

# chapter: A bell on Mars
Mars has air. It's thin, and mostly carbon dioxide, but it's there. So Dave carries his bell to Mars. /
NASA's Perseverance rover recorded Mars with a microphone on its mast, and researchers analysed four hours and forty minutes of it. /
The result, published in Nature in twenty twenty-two: sound on Mars is slow. / Low notes move at about two hundred and forty metres a second, compared with about three hundred and forty on Earth. /
And Mars has two speeds of sound. Higher notes travel slightly faster, at about two hundred and fifty. / So a bell's high notes would arrive just ahead of its low ones. // [J]
Worse, the thin air swallows high notes quickly. On Mars they fade within about eight metres. On Earth, it takes about sixty-five. /
So on Mars, Dave's bell is heard, but only the low part, and only near Dave. His grand HELLO becomes a dull clunk for the nearest rock. // [J]

# chapter: Sounds that aren't sounds
Now, you've heard recordings of space in NASA videos. They are real data. They are not microphone recordings. /
The Chandra X-ray team puts it plainly: "Sonification is the process that translates data into sound." / Someone decides which part of the data becomes which note, and plays the picture like sheet music. //
Voyager one comes closer. In twenty thirteen, outside the Sun's bubble, its instruments felt the gas around it hum. / Electrons in the gas were oscillating at about two point six kilohertz. /
That told scientists the gas was about eight hundredths of a particle per cubic centimetre. / In twenty twenty-one, Cornell's Stella Ocker found a faint, steady hum there too, at about three kilohertz. /
But that hum is electrical: electrons wobbling in the gas, picked up by an antenna, and then played through a speaker. // [J]
"Does that count? I would really like it to count." (@dave) //
"It counts as a hum, Dave. Not as a bell." (@brain) // [J]

# chapter: Not completely silent
So space is silent, and that settles it. Well, not quite. /
Space is quiet because it's thin, not because of a law. Where there's enough gas, sound works. /
In a typical nebula, sound moves at about ten kilometres a second. / Exploding stars plough into gas and make shock waves, like a fighter jet breaking the sound barrier, and they sculpt the delicate shapes of supernova remnants. /
Sound waves in the disks around newborn stars even help clumps of material grow into planets. //
Even our own Sun does it. Sound waves bounce around inside it, and its surface heaves up and down about once every five minutes. / It's a very large, very slow bell. // [J]
And light and radio waves aren't pushes at all, so they cross the gaps easily. That's how we see the stars, and how Dave chats to the station. /
"So space is just thin and shy." (@dave) /
"Space isn't shy, Dave. It's sparse." (@brain) // [J]
Space isn't silent. It's only quiet where it's thin. And the biggest crowd of gas in the universe is a long way off. //

# chapter: The biggest bell
Two hundred and fifty million light-years away sits the Perseus galaxy cluster. Hundreds of galaxies, all wrapped in a vast cloud of hot gas. /
At its centre is a supermassive black hole, and it keeps blowing bubbles in that gas. /
In two thousand three, Andrew Fabian's team stared at the cluster with the Chandra X-ray telescope for almost two hundred thousand seconds. They found faint ripples in the gas. /
The ripples are about thirty-six thousand light-years apart. They move at more than a thousand kilometres a second. /
That means one ripple passes every nine point six million years. / The paper says they could be the sound waves that carry the black hole's energy away. / If you rang a doorbell like that for a visitor, they'd wait ten million years. // [J]
As the waves fade, they hand their energy to the gas around them. So the biggest bell in the universe is also a radiator. // [J]
That's a note. Chandra says it's a B flat, fifty-seven octaves below middle C. A note over a million billion times deeper than human hearing. /
"That's my bell. It's just bigger. And slower." (@dave) // [J]
Dave rings his bell once a second. The black hole rings once every ten million years. Dave is, relatively, a very enthusiastic bell. // [J]

# chapter: Turning it up
No ear can hear that. So in twenty twenty-two, NASA took the ripples, pulled out the waves moving outward, and raised them by fifty-seven and fifty-eight octaves. /
That is a hundred and forty-four quadrillion and two hundred and eighty-eight quadrillion times higher. /
It isn't a recording. It's the real pattern of the waves, shifted up so far that a person can hear it. //
So why is space completely silent? Because a sound is a push, and between the stars there is almost nothing to push. /
But not completely. Wherever the gas is thick enough, space rings. In ships, in rock, in nebulas, and in a cluster of galaxies. //
Dave's bell stays silent out there. But a black hole has been saying hello, in B flat, to anyone patient enough to listen. /
(slow) Dave puts the bell away. The Brain, for once, doesn't shush anything. /
(slow) Somewhere out there, it's still ringing.
