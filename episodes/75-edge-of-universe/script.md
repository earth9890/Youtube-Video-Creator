# chapter: A ladder to the edge
This is Dave. Dave has put a very tall ladder against the sky. He plans to climb to the edge of the universe, and peek over the top. // [J]
"Everything has an edge. Tables, cliffs, biscuits. Why not the universe?" (@dave) /
"It's an edge. How tall can an edge be?" (@dave) // [J]
So here is the question. What would you see at the edge of the universe? A wall? A fence? A sign that says no entry? /
Tonight Dave climbs past the farthest galaxy we have seen, and straight into a wall of light. /
And at the top, he finds something that isn't there. //

# chapter: How far is the edge
The part of the universe we can see is about ninety-three billion light-years across. That's forty-six and a half billion light-years from us in every direction. /
In it, there are somewhere between hundreds of billions and two trillion galaxies. Estimates differ. Nobody has counted. //
But the universe is only thirteen point eight billion years old. How can it be bigger than the time light has had to cross it? /
Because space itself has been stretching. A source whose light left long ago is now much farther away than the light's journey suggests. /
Even at the speed of light, Dave's climb would take forty-six and a half billion years, if the universe held still. / That's more than three times its age, and he's packed one sandwich. // [J]
"It's called the observable universe, Dave. Not the universe." (@brain) // [J]
So the edge isn't really a place. It's a time. It's how far light has been able to travel since the beginning. //

# chapter: The farthest thing
The farthest galaxy we have confirmed is called MoM-zee-fourteen. In twenty twenty-five, the James Webb telescope caught it as it was two hundred and eighty million years after the Big Bang. /
That's when the universe was only about two percent of its present age. Its light took about thirteen and a half billion years to reach us. /
But the galaxy itself is now about thirty-four billion light-years away. And it's tiny, about two hundred and forty light-years in radius, around one percent of the Milky Way. /
The farthest thing we know is also a very small thing. Dave finds it adorable. // [J]
The record keeps falling. In twenty twenty-four, Webb found a galaxy about two hundred and ninety million years after the Big Bang. MoM-zee-fourteen beat it a year later. /
He stops on that rung for a snack, and looks past it. There's nothing there. That is not because there's nothing there. //
"Surely there are more galaxies ahead of me?" (@dave) /
"Dave, look around. It isn't dark up there." (@brain) // [J]
He looks, and the sky behind the last galaxy isn't empty. It's glowing. //

# chapter: The wall of light
It's a wall, in every direction. It's called the cosmic microwave background. For the first three hundred and eighty thousand years, space was a fog of hot, glowing gas. /
ESA says that by then it had cooled to around three thousand kelvin, about twenty-seven hundred degrees Celsius. Then the fog cleared, and light flew free. /
That light is still arriving. When it set off, the gas that sent it was only about forty-two million light-years from the matter that would one day become us. /
Today that gas is about forty-five point seven billion light-years away. The wall has been backing away ever since. // [J]
It's almost perfectly smooth. NASA's WMAP found one patch of sky at two point seven two five one kelvin, and another at two point seven two four nine. / Tiny ripples, which later grew into galaxies. /
The glow is still there, stretched by expansion from orange-white light into microwaves, at about two point seven kelvin. //
We found it by accident. In nineteen sixty-four, Arno Penzias and Robert Wilson kept hearing a hiss in a horn antenna at Bell Labs. It came from every direction. /
They cleaned out the pigeons' droppings. The hiss stayed. They had spent a year trying to remove the Big Bang. They won the Nobel Prize for it. // [J]
If you ever tuned an old television between channels, about one percent of that snow was the same glow. /
Behind the wall, the early universe was opaque, like being in fog. No light can tell us what was there. That is the real edge of what we can see. //

# chapter: The ladder stretches
Dave carries on up the ladder toward the wall. It doesn't get any closer. /
That's because space is still stretching. The farther away something is, the faster it recedes. For every three point three million light-years, it's about sixty-seven kilometres a second faster. /
Astronomers still argue about that rate. One method says sixty-seven, and another says seventy-three. / It's called the Hubble tension, and nobody has resolved it. Cosmologists, to their credit, call it a tension rather than a fight. // [J]
But either way, past about fourteen and a half billion light-years, galaxies recede faster than light. Not through space. With it. /
That doesn't break relativity. Nothing overtakes light inside its own patch of space. The space between is just growing. //
And here's the odd part. Davis and Lineweaver put it plainly: we can observe galaxies that have, and always have had, recession velocities greater than the speed of light. //
So Dave's ladder stretches under him. Every rung he climbs, the next one moves away. // [J]
"Can I go any faster? I have a sandwich to finish." (@dave) /
"Not faster than light, Dave. Even you." (@brain) // [J]
"Is the wall getting any closer yet?" (@dave) /
"It's the same distance as when you started, Dave." (@brain) // [J]

# chapter: Your own edge
Here is the part nobody tells you about the edge. Every place in the universe has its own observable universe. Not ours. Theirs. /
A galaxy forty billion light-years away is also in the middle of a sphere, with its own wall of light, made from different gas. /
Somewhere out there, another Dave is leaning another ladder on the sky, looking toward us, and thinking we're the edge. // [J]
So if Dave ever got to our edge, it wouldn't be an edge. It would be the middle of someone else's view. /
And the wall he'd see would look just like the one he sees from home. Same glow, same colour, same distance. //
"So the edge keeps its distance?" (@dave) /
"It's a circle, Dave. And you're the middle." (@brain) // [J]
Dave looks down at the ladder. It's leaning against nothing. // [J]

# chapter: Probably no edge
So is there an edge to the whole universe? Probably not. /
NASA's WMAP satellite measured the curvature of space to within four-tenths of one percent of flat. That's not a proof. / But a flat universe is the kind that goes on for ever. /
The Planck satellite's final results agree: they fit a spatially flat universe. //
There's a second option. Space could be finite but have no edge, like the skin of a balloon. / Walk far enough in a straight line, and you'd come back to your start. /
"So I could climb for ever and arrive at my own ladder?" (@dave) /
"If space curves round, Dave. So far, it doesn't seem to." (@brain) // [J]
In twenty thirteen, the Planck team searched the cosmic microwave background for signs of that, matching circles and other patterns. They found no compact universe smaller than the wall we see. /
And if it does curve round, the data say it does so on a scale of at least about a hundred and thirty-seven billion light-years. / Three times as far as the edge Dave was after. //
Either way, nobody falls off. Nobody has ever found a fence. // [J]
Whether it's infinite or just very large, nobody knows. We can only see a finite slice, because light has only had a finite time. //

# chapter: The middle
So what would you see at the edge of the universe? A wall of glowing fog, the oldest light there is, in every direction. /
And if you reached it, you'd find more universe, and your own wall just as far away. The edge is a date, not a place. /
Three things to keep. The edge is a time. The wall is a circle around you. And a flat universe has no fence. //
Dave climbs down the ladder, because there's nothing to peek over. He's exactly where he started, in the middle of everything he can see. /
(slow) And so is everyone else, everywhere. Even the Brain, for once, doesn't shush him.
