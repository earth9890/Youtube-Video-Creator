# chapter: The wish
This is Dave. Dave has forgotten where he parked. It is the fourth time this week, and the car is somewhere in a very large field. //
"I wish I never forgot anything. Not a name, not a date, not one single parking space." (@dave) //
His wish is granted on the spot. From now on, Dave will remember every day of his life, in full and for good. //
It does not help with today's car. The wish only covers what happens from now on. // [J]
So what would happen if you never forgot anything? / Would you become a genius, or the most tired person alive? //
Here is the plan. First we meet the people who can really do this. Then we give Dave the full version. / And by the end, he will be begging his Brain for a delete button. //
"Quick note, Dave. I've started a shelf. One binder per day. I'm going to need a much bigger shelf." (@brain) // [J]

# chapter: The people who remember
This is not only a thought experiment. In two thousand six, scientists at the University of California, Irvine published the case of a woman they called A J. / They described her memory as nonstop, uncontrollable and automatic. //
Give her any date from her life, and she could tell you the day of the week and what she was doing. //
So the researchers tested her. They asked for the date of Easter in every year from nineteen eighty to two thousand three. / She got one wrong, by two days, and corrected it herself when asked again two years later. //
They also asked what she had been doing on each of those days. One answer, for one whole Sunday, was: house smells like ham. // [J]
In another test, the lead scientist told her a famous date was wrong by one day. She said no. He checked a second source, and the book was wrong. // [J]
The scientists named it hyperthymesia, a name the lead researcher later called a mistake, because it sounds as if you know what it is. // [J]
It isn't even new. In eighteen seventy-one, a journal described a blind man in Ohio who recalled the weather for any date back to eighteen twenty-seven. //
After a television programme about people with this memory aired, more than six hundred emails arrived from viewers who thought they had it. / By twenty eleven, the researchers had confirmed twenty-two. // [J]

# chapter: Not everything
Now the catch. The gift stops at their own lives. Shown a table of random numbers and told to memorise it, the woman laughed and said it was impossible. //
After hours with two interviewers, she couldn't say what either of them had been wearing. / And on most standard laboratory memory tests, the group scored about the same as everyone else. //
School, she says, was torture, because she couldn't hold on to facts and figures. But ask about television from the nineteen sixties and seventies, and she is unbeatable. // [J]
What mattered to her stuck. She knew the date of a famous crisis because she was a self-described news junkie and had made it part of her day. //
Dave is unimpressed. A memory of his own life is a start. But he wants the full version. //
"Then give me everything. Names, numbers, faces, facts. All of it, all the time." (@dave) //

# chapter: Everything
Something like that has happened once. In the nineteen twenties, the Russian psychologist Alexander Luria met a newspaper reporter who appears in his book only as S. / S. had been scolded for not taking notes at a meeting, so he recited the whole speech word for word. // [J]
Luria read S. lists of thirty, fifty, even seventy numbers. S. closed his eyes, and repeated every one. / Twelve and sixteen years later, he could still recite the same lists. //
But a perfect memory is a terrible way to read a story. Every word raised a picture, and every picture raised more pictures. / Luria wrote that grasping even a simple passage became an endless uphill task. //
Dave tries it on a note stuck to his fridge. It says: buy milk. //
"Buy milk. I remember the first milk. I remember every milk. Brain, why am I crying at a cow?" (@dave) // [J]
S. struggled with faces, too. To him, a face was a pattern of light and shade that kept changing, so someone he knew could look brand new. //
Luria summed it up like this. Many of us look for ways to improve our memories, / but none of us has to learn how to forget. S. had the reverse problem. //
He reportedly wrote numbers on paper and burned them, hoping the fire would take the numbers with it. It did not. // [J]

# chapter: The shelf that won't close
Back in Dave's head, the shelf is now a wall. Every smell, song and date pulls a binder off it, and the binder will not close itself. //
"Dave, you smelled toast. I am now in the toast binders. There are four thousand toast binders." (@brain) // [J]
The woman from Irvine described it as a split screen. On one side, the present. On the other, a reel of memories that never stops playing. / In an email to the researchers, she called it nonstop, uncontrollable and totally exhausting. //
To be fair, all of the people in the first group study said they enjoyed replaying their memories. / One compares every fourth of October she can remember while drying her hair, then tells an imaginary person: now you do it. // [J]
Most filed their memories by date, like a calendar. Ordinary memory is bad at telling two weeks ago from two months ago. //
In two thousand sixteen, the Irvine team compared recall after a week, and after a month. At one week, both groups remembered the same amount. / At one month, ordinary memory had dropped dramatically, and the superior memories were still going strong. //
That drop isn't a bug. It is your brain letting a month go. //
Dave has one last hope. Surely, if he remembers everything, at least he remembers it correctly. //

# chapter: The twist
This is where it goes wrong. In twenty thirteen, researchers at Irvine tested twenty people with superior memories, and thirty-eight with ordinary ones. //
First, a list of words. Then new words, some related to the list but never on it. The question was simple: which of these did you see? //
Both groups said yes to about seven in ten of the words that were never there. / The superior memories did remember more of the real words, seventy-seven percent against sixty-five. But they were fooled just as often. //
So the people who never forget still remember things that never happened. // [J]
In a second test, they took in false details from a slideshow even more than the others did. //
Remembering is not playing back a recording. Each time, your brain rebuilds the scene from pieces, using what you know now. / As one researcher on the study put it, we pull together everything in the present to come up with an approximation of the past. //
"So I remember everything, and some of it is wrong?" (@dave) //
Yes, Dave. But with total confidence. // [J]
"Dave, I am now certain of fifty things that never happened. I have binders for all of them." (@brain) // [J]

# chapter: What forgetting is for
So what is memory for? In two thousand seventeen, the neuroscientists Blake Richards and Paul Frankland, at the University of Toronto, argued it is not storage. //
Richards said the real goal of memory is to optimize decision-making. If your brain keeps surfacing old, conflicting memories, it gets harder to choose well. //
That is why it matters that the brain forgets irrelevant details. Old information becomes outdated, and keeping it around has a cost. //
Yesterday's parking space is the clearest case. By tomorrow, it is the most useless fact you own. // [J]
Frankland's lab found that the growth of new neurons in the hippocampus seems to promote forgetting. / Your brain grows new cells, in part, to let go. Here, growth means demolition. // [J]
The delete button Dave begged for was already installed. It has been running his whole life. //

# chapter: The empty slot
Dave takes it back. All of it. The Brain wheels the shelf out of his head, binder by binder, and leaves one slot empty. //
"Thank you. Most of my job is throwing things away. Nobody ever says thank you." (@brain) //
Somewhere in a very large field, a car is waiting. Dave has no idea where. He's fine.
