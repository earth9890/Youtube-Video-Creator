# chapter: Dave presses everything
This is Dave, and Dave presses every button he meets, usually more than once. //
This morning, the walk button got eleven presses. / The lift's door-close got jabbed. / The thermostat went down two degrees. //
Three buttons. Three wins. / Dave keeps score, and he's undefeated. // [J]
But in two thousand four, New York admitted that more than two thousand five hundred of its walk buttons did nothing. //
Not broken. Disconnected, under a sign that still said push button. // [J]
So why is the world full of buttons that do nothing? / And why can't Dave stop pressing them? //
Dave's morning had three buttons. / One of them was lying to him, / one was working for somebody else, / and one was working on Dave the whole time. //

# chapter: The walk button
Start with the button Dave pressed eleven times. /
New York's push-button signals go back to about nineteen sixty-four. / They sat where a quiet side street met a busy avenue. /
The avenue stayed green until a car rolled up, / or someone on foot pressed the button. //
Then the city filled with cars, / and computers took over, timing the lights along whole avenues at once. /
Side streets got so busy that cars tripped the lights anyway, / and the buttons got in the way of the computer's plan. /
So by the late nineteen eighties, most of them had been quietly switched off. //
By two thousand four, that was more than two thousand five hundred buttons out of three thousand two hundred and fifty. / The Times called them mechanical placebos. //
Nobody took them down, though. / Removing them would have cost about a million dollars, / and as one city official put it, /
"There's no harm in having it at the locations." // [J]
The sign on the pole gave two instructions. / Push button. Wait for walk signal. / Only the second one was doing anything. // [J]
The Times also met a four-year-old in Brooklyn, pressing away while his babysitter explained it never works. /
Asked why he kept going, he said, because it's fun. // [J]
By twenty sixteen, the city told the Times it had a hundred and twenty working ones left. /
And in twenty ten, ABC News checked crossings in Austin, Gainesville and Syracuse, / and found one button that worked. // [J]
But that's just America. / In twenty thirteen, the BBC timed a busy crossing on Regent Street in London. /
Press the button, a hundred and five seconds. / Don't press it, a hundred and five seconds. // [J]
Transport for London said that one only listens between midnight and seven in the morning. /
To be fair, London says most of its junction buttons do work, / and at a crossing away from any junction, the button really stops the cars. /
Britain's newer Puffin crossings even have a sensor watching the kerb. / Press the button and wander off, and it's meant to cancel your request. //
Finally, a button that checks you meant it. // [J]
Then researchers working for the Department for Transport inspected seventy-two Puffin crossings. / Only eleven had the cancel feature working. // [J]
So a walk button depends on the city, the junction and the hour. / Dave's eleven presses were a lottery ticket. // [J]

# chapter: The door-close button
Next, the lift, where Dave jabbed door-close until the doors gave in. /
In nineteen ninety, America passed the Americans with Disabilities Act. / Under its rules, lift doors must stay open at least three seconds, / and longer when someone is walking over from the call button. /
The door-close button isn't allowed to cut that short. / And many lifts are set to close at about the minimum anyway, / so there's nothing left for the button to shorten. //
The head of the elevator industry's trade group told the New York Times, /
"The riding public would not be able to make those doors close any faster." // [J]
So why does it feel like it works? / Drexel psychologist John Kounios pointed out that the doors always close eventually. /
"If the door never closed, we would stop pressing the button." //
Dave pressed, the door closed, / and his brain filed it as a win. // [J]
But here's the part nobody tells Dave. / The door-close button isn't fake. //
In a fire, a firefighter turns a key, / and from then on, the doors close only while someone holds that button down. / Let go too early, and they open again. //
So it was never really Dave's button. / It belongs to the fire department, and Dave is allowed to borrow it. // [J]

# chapter: The thermostat
Which leaves the thermostat, the one button Dave is truly proud of. /
In two thousand three, a trade magazine called Air Conditioning, Heating and Refrigeration News asked its readers a quiet question. /
Had they ever installed a dummy thermostat, wired to nothing? / Seventy people answered, and fifty-one said yes. //
It was an informal online poll, so it isn't every office on Earth. / It might be Dave's, though. // [J]
One reader in Colorado wrote in about a thermostat that controlled nothing at all. / People felt better, he said, / and service calls fell by over seventy-five percent. //
The fake thermostat fixed the complaints. / It just never touched the temperature. // [J]
Which raises a strange question. / If the room didn't change, why did the people in it feel better? //

# chapter: The lottery ticket
In nineteen seventy-five, psychologist Ellen Langer sold one-dollar lottery tickets to office workers. /
Each ticket was a card with a football player on it. / Some people picked their own card, and others were simply handed one. //
Just before the draw, she asked what price they'd sell their ticket back for. /
Handed a ticket? About two dollars. / Picked it yourself? Almost nine. // [J]
Same odds. Same dollar. The only difference was the feeling of having chosen. /
Langer called it the illusion of control, / acting as if you can steer something that's pure chance. //
Dave's walk button was the same thing, / a lottery ticket he chose eleven times in a row. // [J]
So that settles it, right? Well, not quite. //
In twenty twenty-one, a team at Wharton ran seventeen experiments with more than ten thousand people. / Choosing your own ticket mostly didn't make people feel any luckier. //
One explanation they offer: / Langer's choosers may simply have liked their football player. // [J]
So the illusion of control might be partly an illusion. Fair. // [J]
Maybe fake buttons don't even fool us. / Maybe Dave is just a man who enjoys pressing plastic. //

# chapter: The noise button
But around nineteen seventy, psychologists David Glass and Jerome Singer tested a different kind of button. /
First, they recorded a terrible soundtrack. / Two people speaking Spanish, one speaking Armenian, / a mimeograph copier, a calculator and a typewriter, all at once. // [J]
They played it in random bursts at about a hundred and eight decibels, / while volunteers tried to work through tasks. //
Some volunteers got a button on the side of their chair, and an instruction. /
"You can terminate the noise by pressing the button. But we'd prefer you not do it." //
Almost nobody pressed it. / They heard the same noise, just as loud, for just as long. // [J]
Afterwards, everyone got puzzles, some of them secretly impossible, / and pages of text full of errors to find. /
The people with no button gave up on the puzzles sooner, and missed more of the errors. /
The people with a button they never used kept going, / and did about as well as people who could really switch the noise off. //
The button didn't change the noise. / It changed what the noise did to them. //
These are lab studies from more than fifty years ago, so hold them loosely, / but they do explain Colorado. /
The dummy thermostat didn't cool the room. / It cooled the complaints. // [J]

# chapter: The button you think you have
So why is the world full of buttons that do nothing? / Partly because taking them out costs money. /
And partly because they never stopped working. / They just work on the person, not the machine. //
That doesn't make every fake button fine. / New York, under a court order from twenty twenty-one, / is putting new buttons at ten thousand crossings by twenty thirty-one. /
These ones talk and vibrate, so someone who is blind knows when it's time to walk. / A button that tells the truth beats one that only feels good. //
(slow) The next morning, Dave is back at the crossing, and he knows everything now. /
He knows the walk button might be disconnected, / that door-close belongs to the fire department, / and that his thermostat may be wired to nothing. /
He presses all three anyway. //
Dave updates his scoreboard. Buttons that worked on the machine, maybe one. / Buttons that worked on Dave, all three. //
(slow) Most buttons in your life do exactly what they say. / A few only work on you. //
(slow) And the button that matters / is the one you think you have.
