# chapter: Dave Gives a Hundred Percent
This is Dave, anchoring a team of eight at the company tug-of-war. He is pulling with everything he has. // [J]
"I'm giving a hundred percent. Honestly, a hundred and ten." (@dave)
Beside the rope, a very serious man from the physics department is reading a force gauge. / Dave, he announces, is pulling at forty-nine percent. //
That's roughly what a table from the eighteen eighties predicts for a team of eight. / So here's the question. Why does every person pull less in a group than alone, and is it just laziness? //
Dave's team then loses to a team of six from accounting, and the next morning his boss gives him a group project with seven other people. // [J]
Dave would like to know whether he is a slacker, or whether the rope is. //
To find out, we need a French engineer, a blindfold, a tape of people who weren't there, / and a famous number that took a wrong turn on its way to America. Let's start with the engineer. // [J]

# chapter: The Engineer and the Table
Maximilien Ringelmann was a French agricultural engineer. His job was to find which farm power was most efficient: horses, oxen, men, or machines. /
He hardly distinguished between them. He tested oxen, horses and men with exactly the same curiosity. // [J]
Between eighteen eighty-two and eighteen eighty-seven, he tested male students who, he wrote, "very much wanted to take part in these tests." /
They pulled a five-meter rope along a garden path, alone and together, while a dynamometer recorded the force. They were enthusiastic. The rope did not notice. // [J]
In nineteen thirteen, Ringelmann printed a table summarizing, in his words, "a great number of findings." / Each worker alone counts as one. In a pair, each furnishes ninety-three percent. In a team of eight, forty-nine. //
Seven workers produce three point nine two. So do eight. The eighth worker added exactly nothing. // [J]
Ringelmann blamed coordination. His students pulled on command, and he wrote of "the lack of simultaneity of their efforts." / He saw it in machines too: bigger engines gave less power per cylinder. Nobody accused the pistons of laziness. // [J]
And the sums back him up. The psychologist Ivan Steiner later found the loss tracked the number of links between people. / Two people have one link to keep in time. Six have fifteen. Eight have twenty-eight. //
He even noted that workers sometimes sang, to pull in time. / But he'd noticed something else. In a mill where prisoners turned a crank to grind flour, "each man, trusting in his neighbor to furnish the desired effort," /
"contented himself by merely following the movement of the crank, and sometimes even let himself be carried along by it." // [J]
So which was it? Were groups clumsy, or lazy? //

# chapter: Pull Alone, Believe You're Not
In nineteen seventy-four, the psychologist Alan Ingham and colleagues set out to separate the two. / First they repeated the rope pull. Pairs managed ninety-one percent of what two solo pullers could. Trios, eighty-two. Six people, seventy-eight. //
Then came the clever part. They blindfolded each volunteer and said other people were pulling on the same rope. / In truth, the volunteer was always pulling alone. //
Some of the "teammates" were confederates who pretended to pull, and each volunteer needed five of them. They pulled no weight at all. // [J]
"Is anyone really pulling with me, or is it just me?" (@dave) / Nobody answers. That is the experiment. // [J]
With no one to coordinate with, clumsiness was impossible. Yet when volunteers believed one other person was pulling, they pulled at ninety percent of their solo force. / With two to six others, eighty-five percent. //
So Dave, tied to a post with five imaginary colleagues, would pull about fifteen percent less. For nobody. // [J]
It wasn't just coordination. Something in the head was easing off. //

# chapter: Shouting Into Headphones
Even that had a flaw. The volunteers who weren't pulling stood watching, so smaller pulling groups meant bigger audiences. /
Five years later, Bibb Latané and two colleagues at Ohio State University wanted something cleaner and cheaper, / because rope-pulling equipment was, they wrote, "cumbersome and inefficient." So they chose clapping and shouting. //
Six male undergraduates sat in a soundproof room, one meter apart, and were asked to shout, or clap, as loud as they could. //
Before the first trial, the experimenter asked how loud they should be. In unison, the group replied, "As loud as we can." // [J]
Then six of them produced forty percent of what six solo shouters could. Pairs managed seventy-one, and fours, fifty-one. / Twelve hands clapping, they noted, are not even three times as loud as two. // [J]
Next, the team put blindfolds and headphones on everyone, and played a recording of six people shouting. / Each volunteer could now be told they were shouting with others, while shouting alone. //
Believing one other person was shouting, volunteers gave eighty-two percent of their solo volume. Believing five others, seventy-four. / Real groups of six, clumsiness included, managed thirty-six. //
The answer to Ringelmann's question was both. For shouting, they concluded, half the loss was incoordination, and half was what they named social loafing. //
Their explanation: when nobody can tell whose effort is whose, each person gets back only a sixth of their own work. / Which is a group project: six names on one slide, and no way to tell who wrote which bullet point. // [J]
They suspected a second reason. People may arrive already expecting others to slack, so they ease off first, and nobody wants to be the only one pulling. //
Later studies found the same pattern in factories, where bigger work groups produced less per person, and in churches, where a smaller share of a bigger congregation takes part. //
So is Dave a slacker? Not exactly. He is a person on a rope with no name on it. // [J]

# chapter: The Table's Missing Passport
"So it's not me. It's the rope. I knew it." (@dave)
So that settles it, right? A famous rope, a famous number, and Dave's excuse. Well, not quite. //
Latané's own paper calls Ringelmann "a German psychologist" whose study was never published, passed down through a nineteen twenty-seven summary by a man named Moede. / In nineteen eighty-six, David Kravitz and Barbara Martin finally tracked down the original. // [J]
Ringelmann was French, an engineer, not a psychologist. His data came from the eighteen eighties, and Kravitz and Martin say it may even count as the first social psychology experiment. / Moede had printed the table as a small figure, with Ringelmann's name in parentheses at the end of the caption. // [J]
And Ringelmann's table never said it was about rope-pulling. Moede said so, and everyone copied him. / Ringelmann himself never named the tasks. //
His real rope test was smaller: a mean pull per person of eighty-five kilograms alone, sixty-five in groups of seven, sixty-one in a group of fourteen. //
So was the Ringelmann effect a myth? Hardly. In nineteen ninety-three, Steven Karau and Kipling Williams combined seventy-eight studies. / Social loafing, they found, is robust, and shows up across tasks and populations. //
But robust isn't universal. How much people loaf depends on whether they're evaluated, whether the task matters, what they expect from teammates, and what culture they come from. //

# chapter: Put Your Name on the Rope
The best test came in nineteen eighty-one, when Williams, Harkins and Latané repeated the shouting trials with one change. / They told volunteers their individual loudness would be identifiable, even in a group. //
That eliminated social loafing. Group size stopped mattering. //
In a second experiment, when individual outputs were never identifiable, volunteers shouted weakly at every group size, even alone. //
Karau and Williams boil it down to two questions every puller asks. Will my effort change the result? And do I care about the result? //
So the problem isn't groups. It's anonymity. / Latané wrote that the cure is finding ways of channeling social forces so that "the group can serve as a means of intensifying individual responsibility rather than diffusing it." //
Which means a few boring rules. Give each person a visible piece. Make the piece matter. Keep the team small enough that the eighth worker is noticed. / And for the clumsy half, pull on a count, or sing. //
Dave's group project of eight becomes two teams of four, with a name beside every task, and a countdown from three. //
At next year's picnic, the physicist chalks each name on the rope, next to the gauge, and reads each pull out loud. Dave pulls ninety-one percent. //
Not a hundred and ten. But for the first time, nobody has to guess what Dave gave, including Dave. //
(slow) Dave's team still loses. Ninety-one, with his name on it.
