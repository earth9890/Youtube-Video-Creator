The answer changes who you ask, how you ask, and what you expect to happen. / To find out, we need a Pennsylvania clerk, one very scarce book, a contest that paid students in cash, and a very unhelpful secretary. Let's start with the book. // [J]

# chapter: The Clerk and the Book
Benjamin Franklin was a man who thought hard about how to ask. / In seventeen thirty-one, he helped start a lending library in Philadelphia, where about fifty subscribers paid forty shillings each, and met resistance. /
People disliked a proposer who looked like he wanted credit. / So he "put myself as much as I could out of sight," and called it a scheme of "a number of friends." It worked. /
Books were lent to subscribers on promissory notes to pay double the value if not returned. So Franklin knew exactly how to treat a borrowed book. // [J]
In seventeen thirty-six, aged thirty, he was chosen clerk of the Pennsylvania General Assembly. / The next year, when he was proposed again, a new member made a long speech against him. /
Franklin was chosen anyway, but he didn't enjoy the opposition. The man was, he wrote, "a gentleman of fortune and education," likely to gain great influence. //
Franklin decided against flattery. "I did not, however, aim at gaining his favour by paying any servile respect to him." // [J]
Instead, he'd heard the man owned "a certain very scarce and curious book." He wrote a note, asking to borrow it for a few days. /
The man "sent it immediately." Franklin returned it in about a week, with another note "expressing strongly my sense of the favour." //
Next time they met in the House, the man spoke to him, which he had never done before, "and with great civility." / He "ever after manifested a readiness to serve me on all occasions, so that we became great friends." //
Franklin credited an old maxim: "He that has once done you a kindness will be more ready to do you another, than he whom you yourself have obliged." //
His moral: it is "more profitable" to remove an opponent's hostility than to resent it. / He also noted that the clerkship brought valuable printing work. Franklin was, as ever, thinking about printing contracts. // [J]
Notice the recipe. Something small, specific, returned on time, with thanks. //
He never names the man, or the book, and he wrote it decades later, partly to teach young readers how to get on. / So it's a good lesson. But is it a result? //

# chapter: The Money Experiment
In nineteen sixty-nine, two psychologists, Jon Jecker and David Landy, tested it. / Students took part in a question-and-answer contest run by a researcher, and won small sums of money. //
Afterwards, a third of the winners were approached by the researcher himself. He asked them to return the money, because he'd paid from his own funds, and was running short. /
Another third were asked by a secretary, because the psychology department's funds were low. The last third were not asked at all. //
Then everyone rated how much they liked the researcher. The group he'd asked personally liked him the most. The group the secretary asked liked him the least. /
Same request, same money. It felt different depending on whether a person or an institution was asking. //
So the secretary is the villain of this study. // [J]
"So I should ask Gerald to give back the muffins?" (@dave) //
Please don't. Notice what the favor was: handing back money they'd just won, to the man who'd paid it. The more directly he asked, the more they liked him. //

# chapter: Why Would That Work?
The usual explanation is cognitive dissonance. You lent a book to someone you don't much like. Nobody paid you, and nobody ordered you. / So your mind quietly fixes the contradiction: I must like him. //
In nineteen fifty-nine, Leon Festinger and Merrill Carlsmith paid students one dollar, or twenty dollars, to tell the next student that a boring task was fun. / The one-dollar group later rated the task more positively. With less reward to explain it, they explained it with their opinions. // [J]
A nineteen seventy-one experiment had people treat pretend students kindly or harshly. They rated the ones they'd been kind to as more likable than the ones they'd insulted. /
The flip side is less comfortable. People also tend to dislike someone they've wronged, which makes the wrong easier to live with. //
A third reading says asking is itself flattery: it tells someone they have something you don't. Franklin swore off flattery, and then flattered a man with a book request. // [J]
Dave, who has read the first half of an article, tries this. / "Gerald, could I borrow your car this weekend? I'm told it will make you like me." (@dave) // [J]
"Get off my porch, Dave. Right now." (@old) / The door closes with the sound of a decision. // [J]
Nothing in these studies tested cars. They tested a book and some returned cash. // [J]
Dave overreached. Franklin asked for a few days' loan, not the keys to anything. // [J]

# chapter: The Catch
So that settles it, right? Ask, and they like you. Well, not quite. //
In twenty fifteen, the psychologist Yu Niiya, at Hosei University in Tokyo, ran an experiment with Japanese and American volunteers. / A confederate asked some of them for help, and they liked the confederate more than before. They also found the confederate more sociable, and felt closer. /
But when volunteers helped the confederate without being asked, there was no such increase. //
That's odd. If dissonance were the whole story, any favor should do. Instead, being asked seems to matter. //
Dave notes that this explains the muffins. Nobody asked for muffins. // [J]
Niiya's explanation draws on the Japanese idea of amae: leaning on someone close, which can draw you closer. / It would also explain the secretary, whose request had no friendship in it. //
So is the Ben Franklin effect a law? / We looked for a large, modern, preregistered replication, and couldn't find one. //
The nineteen sixty-nine study is old and small. Franklin's story is one man's memory. And Niiya's study, though it supports the effect, is modest. //
The honest summary: plausible, modestly supported, and untested on cars. // [J]
What the research supports is narrow. Ask directly, ask personally, ask for something small, and say thank you. //

# chapter: Just Ask
Here's the part that is well supported. In two thousand eight, Francis Flynn and Vanessa Lake ran a series of studies on asking for help. //
In experiments and in real-world settings, people who needed help underestimated, by as much as fifty percent, how likely others were to agree to a direct request. / Simply sitting in the asker's seat produced the bias. //
Why? Help seekers focus on the cost of helping. But the person being asked also feels the cost of saying no, to someone's face. //
So the biggest obstacle for Dave isn't Gerald. It's Dave. /
Franklin, who asked once, kindly, in writing, never had that problem. //
One Saturday, Dave knocks on Gerald's door. He asks nicely, and for something small. /
"Gerald, I hear you own a very good book about hedges. May I borrow it for a week?" (@dave) //
Gerald studies him for a long time. Then he fetches a very old, very scarce book called Privet and Its Discontents. / "Bring it back in one piece." (@old) // [J]
Dave returns it a week later, with a note. He wrote it three times, and "Dear Gerald" was the hard part. / It says thank you, and he means it. That may be the part that matters. //
Gerald nods. It isn't friendship, and it isn't hello. But it is a nod. // [J]
